# **Chapter 37 — The Child Who Was Given to the Wrong Woman**

At 11:46 p.m., Edward stopped reading.

He had reached the bottom of the document, but his attention remained fixed on the handwritten sentence.

**This is not the child assigned to her.**

The statement was not a correction.

It was not a question.

It was an instruction or an observation made by someone who knew what had happened.

Edward placed the document beside the photograph of Infant A.

Two separate records.

One showed a child received by a woman calling herself Margaret.

The other showed a child collected on the tenth day by a woman whose signature read Victoria A.

The second woman had not been told the child's original placement.

And the document explicitly stated that the child was not the one originally assigned to her.

Edward called Jonathan Pierce.

"Have you examined Helen Ward's document?"

"Yes. I've received the photograph."

"Can you identify the handwriting?"

"Not yet."

"What about the signature?"

"Victoria A. is all we have."

"Could it be Victoria Aurelius?"

Pierce paused.

"Possibly. But a first name and initial aren't sufficient identification."

"Then why use her name?"

"Someone may have wanted the document to resemble an authorisation from the Aurelius family. Or the woman herself may have used that name."

Edward looked again at the signature.

"Do you believe the woman was Victoria Aurelius?"

"I believe we need to ask Victoria Aurelius."

Edward exhaled.

"She says she never collected a child."

"Then we have another problem."

The following morning, the Aurelius family assembled in the private conference room.

Alexander had requested that the meeting take place before any further contact with the hospital or the legal archive.

Victoria sat beside him.

Adrian had prepared a comparison of the documents.

Damien had obtained the surviving hospital movement records.

Lucien had brought a folder containing the legal correspondence.

Julian sat at the far end of the table, unusually quiet.

Edward appeared on the screen.

He displayed the tenth-day acknowledgment.

Victoria read it without speaking.

Then she looked at the signature.

"That is not mine."

"Are you certain?" Edward asked.

"Yes."

"Have you ever used Victoria A. as a signature?"

"No."

"Did you use another name during the period after the births?"

"No."

"Did you send anyone to collect a child from St. Catherine's?"

"No."

Alexander looked at the document.

"Then someone used your name without your authority."

"That seems likely," Edward replied.

Adrian enlarged the lower section.

"The handwriting in the instruction is different from the signature."

"Yes," Pierce said. "We have at least two writers on the page."

Damien pointed to the final sentence.

"'This is not the child assigned to her.' That implies the woman had been expecting a different infant."

"Or that the document's author believed she should have received a different infant," Adrian said.

Lucien frowned.

"Why would someone give her the wrong child deliberately?"

Nobody answered.

Julian looked at the photograph of the infant.

"Maybe they wanted her to think it was the right one."

Alexander turned toward him.

"That is possible."

"Then the woman might not have known."

"Correct."

Julian looked at Victoria.

"Does that mean she could have been trying to help?"

Victoria considered the question.

"She might have been. But we don't know who she was, or why she used my name."

Julian nodded.

He looked down at the document again.

For the first time, the mystery seemed less like a series of mistakes and more like a series of deliberate decisions.

Someone had selected the receiving guardians.

Someone had altered the placements.

Someone had concealed the children's identities.

And someone had continued moving them even after the original arrangement had failed.

Alexander asked Edward to explain the distinction between the three known events.

Edward brought up a timeline.

**The surviving record sequence**

1. **Ninth day — First release**

   Infant A was received by a woman identified as Margaret in the provisional acknowledgment.
2. **Ninth day — Second release**

   Infant B was received by Charles Vale on behalf of an unnamed designated representative.
3. **Tenth day — Return to hospital**

   One infant was returned to maternity observation. The surviving register does not identify which infant.
4. **Tenth day — New receiving acknowledgment**

   A woman signed as Victoria A. and collected an infant. The document states that this was not the child originally assigned to her.

The identity of the infant in the tenth-day acknowledgment remains unverified.

"There's an important gap," Edward continued. "We don't know whether the infant returned on the tenth day was the same infant collected later that day."

Adrian nodded.

"Nor do we know whether the infant returned was Infant A or Infant B."

"Exactly."

Victoria looked at the timeline.

"Could Margaret's child have been returned?"

"She says the child remained with her," Edward replied. "But we haven't independently verified the entire period."

"Then she might have received Infant A on the ninth day, only for that child to be returned on the tenth?"

"Possibly. But it would contradict her account unless she was mistaken about the timing or the document's identity."

Alexander leaned back.

"We need the original acknowledgment."

Pierce nodded.

"Helen Ward believes it was filed under the name Margaret. I'm checking the archive."

"Do that," Alexander said. "And don't assume the signature belongs to anyone in this family."

The original document was located shortly before noon.

It was stored in a separate legal archive, inside a folder marked:

**TEMPORARY RECEIVING ARRANGEMENTS — SUPPLEMENTARY**

Pierce arranged for an authorised inspection.

Edward joined him.

The original paper was in better condition than the photograph suggested.

Its upper section contained several typed fields.

The infant's identity had been left blank.

The receiving party's name had been written in ink, then covered by a strip of opaque archival paper.

The signature was clear.

**Victoria A.**

But there was another detail.

The document listed the receiving party's relationship to the infant as:

**NONE — TEMPORARY CUSTODIAL RECEIPT ONLY.**

Edward read it aloud.

Pierce examined the wording.

"She wasn't receiving the child as a parent."

"She was receiving custody."

"Temporarily."

Edward turned the page.

There was a separate section for the person authorising the release.

The name was not covered.

**M. MORELLI.**

Beneath it was a handwritten statement:

**Released under revised instruction. Original assignment suspended.**

Edward stared at the page.

"Morelli authorised it."

Pierce nodded.

"That makes him the central person in the tenth-day transfer."

"Unless someone else instructed him."

"Which is likely."

Edward looked at the covered name.

"Can we recover it?"

The archivist, who had been observing them, stepped forward.

"The strip appears to be part of the original document, not a later addition. We would need a conservator to examine it."

"How long?"

"At least several days."

Edward nodded.

He had waited eighteen years.

Several more days would not change that.

But something else had changed.

Morelli was no longer merely a witness to the ninth-day release.

He had personally authorised a second transfer.

At the Valmont estate, Elena was preparing for her next university interview.

She had moved her books to the dining table because the study was occupied by Sebastian.

Her laptop displayed a list of questions.

**Why this programme?**

**What do you hope to achieve?**

**How will you contribute to the academic community?**

She stared at the last one.

Then she wrote:

*I want to learn how organisations function and how decisions affect people beyond the organisation itself.*

She read the sentence again.

It sounded too formal.

She deleted it.

Clara had sent her a message.

**CLARA:** Have you prepared?

**ELENA:** Yes.

**CLARA:** Have you prepared enough?

**ELENA:** Define enough.

**CLARA:** More than you think you need.

Elena smiled.

**ELENA:** That's not a measurable standard.

**CLARA:** Neither is your ability to worry about everything.

Elena put the phone down.

A moment later, Isabelle entered carrying two cups of tea.

"Interview preparation?"

"Unfortunately."

"Can I see?"

Elena moved her laptop.

Isabelle read the questions.

"You're answering them like an examination."

"Isn't it an interview?"

"Yes, but they want to know how you think."

Elena frowned.

"That's what I'm trying to explain."

"Then don't memorise your answers."

"I'm not memorising them."

"You've written three pages."

Elena looked at the screen.

"Two and a half."

Isabelle laughed.

She placed the tea beside Elena.

"Tell me why you want to go."

Elena considered the question.

"Because I want to study somewhere different. I want to see how other universities work. I want to learn things I wouldn't encounter here."

"Good."

"And I want to know whether I can manage independently."

Isabelle nodded.

"That's probably the most important answer."

Elena looked at her.

"What about you? Have you decided what you want to do?"

"Not completely."

"Business?"

"Possibly economics. I want to understand how the foundation's programmes are funded and evaluated. I don't want to spend my life attending meetings where people discuss helping students without knowing whether their programmes actually work."

Elena smiled.

"That sounds like you."

Isabelle took a sip of tea.

"I think I'd like to work on the scholarship programme for a while."

"Your mother will be pleased."

"She already is."

For a moment, both girls were quiet.

Then Elena said, "It's strange."

"What?"

"We spent so much time worrying about who belongs where. Now we're discussing where we want to go."

Isabelle looked at her.

"Perhaps that's better."

Elena smiled.

"Perhaps."

At the Aurelius residence, Julian had been trying to update his list.

He sat on the floor of his room, surrounded by notebooks.

The original list had become a collection of ideas, reminders, and things he wanted to ask his future sister.

He had crossed out several items.

Not because they were unimportant, but because he had begun to realise that some of them assumed too much.

He opened a new page.

**Things I Would Like to Do With My Sister**

1. Show her the library.
2. Show her the indoor pool.
3. Let her choose a film.
4. Ask what books she likes.
5. Take her to the city.
6. Find out whether she likes board games.
7. Ask what she wants to do.
8. Let her decide whether she wants company.

He stopped.

Then he wrote:

**44. Don't make plans before she gets here.**

He looked at the sentence.

It seemed sensible.

He was not entirely pleased with it.

A knock sounded.

Damien opened the door.

"What are you doing?"

"Updating the list."

"How many items?"

"Forty-four."

Damien glanced at the notebook.

"That one is new."

Julian covered the page.

"You're not supposed to read it."

"I've already read it."

"Then you're not supposed to comment."

Damien sat on the edge of the bed.

"You know you don't have to plan everything."

"I know."

"Do you?"

Julian frowned.

"Yes."

Damien looked at the notebook.

"Then why are you writing things you want to do with someone you haven't met?"

Julian thought about it.

"Because I don't know what else to do."

Damien's expression softened.

"That's fair."

Julian closed the notebook.

"Do you think she'll like us?"

Damien considered the question.

"I hope so."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only honest one."

Julian leaned back against the bed.

"Father says we shouldn't expect her to feel like she belongs immediately."

"He is right."

"But what if she doesn't want to come home?"

Damien did not dismiss the possibility.

"Then we'll have to respect that."

Julian looked unhappy.

"Even if we've been looking for her all this time?"

"Especially then."

Julian lowered his eyes.

He understood.

He simply did not like it.

That evening, Edward met Morelli again.

The meeting took place in a quiet private room at a hotel near the old legal district.

Morelli looked older than he had during their previous meeting.

He had brought no documents.

Edward placed the tenth-day acknowledgment on the table.

Morelli looked at it.

"You found it."

"Yes."

"You shouldn't have."

"Why?"

Morelli did not answer.

Edward pointed to the signature.

"You authorised the transfer."

"I did."

"Who instructed you?"

"I cannot tell you."

"Was it Eleanor Vale?"

"No."

"Was it Charles Vale?"

"No."

"Was it Alexander Aurelius?"

Morelli looked at him sharply.

"No."

"Then who?"

Morelli folded his hands.

"Someone who had been given responsibility for the child's protection."

"By whom?"

"That is not a simple question."

"It can be answered simply."

"No, Edward. It cannot."

Edward pushed the document closer.

"Someone collected a child under the name Victoria A. Your signature authorised the release. The document states that she was not receiving the child originally assigned to her."

Morelli looked at the paper.

"That is correct."

"Why was she given the wrong child?"

"Because the original placement had become unsafe."

"Unsafe for whom?"

"The infant."

"Which infant?"

Morelli's expression tightened.

"I will not identify the child from that document."

"Why?"

"Because the document does not identify her."

Edward leaned forward.

"You know which child it was."

Morelli did not deny it.

"Yes."

"Then tell me."

"No."

Edward sat back.

"Was the receiving woman actually named Victoria?"

Morelli looked at him.

"That wasn't her name."

"Then why did she sign it?"

"She was instructed to use that name."

"By whom?"

"I cannot tell you."

Edward's patience was wearing thin.

"Was she aware that she was receiving a child under a false identity?"

"She knew the name was not her own."

"Did she know the child was not the infant originally assigned to her?"

"No."

"Did she believe the child was hers to care for?"

"She believed she was being asked to protect a child temporarily."

"Did she know there were two infants?"

Morelli paused.

"Not at the time."

Edward looked at him.

"Then why was she chosen?"

Morelli looked toward the window.

"Because she was the only person the child could safely be given to."

Edward waited.

"Why?"

Morelli's answer was quiet.

"Because someone was looking for her."

The meeting continued for another hour.

Morelli revealed little else.

He confirmed that the woman had been selected by someone other than Charles Vale.

He confirmed that the child had been transferred under a false receiving identity.

He confirmed that the woman had not been told about the ninth-day assignment.

But he refused to reveal her real name.

Edward asked whether she was still alive.

Morelli said he did not know.

Edward asked whether the child remained with her.

Morelli said he could not confirm it.

Finally, Edward asked the question that had been bothering him since the tenth-day record was discovered.

"Why did you allow the child to be transferred if you believed the arrangement was unsafe?"

Morelli looked exhausted.

"Because the alternative was worse."

"What alternative?"

"Leaving her where someone could find her."

"Who?"

Morelli looked directly at him.

"The person who had already tried to take her."

Edward remembered Martha Voss's statement.

Someone had already tried to take one of the infants before the ninth day.

He had assumed it referred to an attempt to interfere with the hospital's release arrangements.

Now he understood that it might have been something more serious.

"Was the person who tried to take the child connected to the Aurelius family?"

Morelli did not answer.

"Was it someone from the Valmont family?"

"No."

"Then who?"

Morelli stood.

"Find the original tenth-day instruction."

"Where is it?"

"Not in Box 114."

"Where?"

Morelli looked at him.

"With the person who selected the receiving guardian."

At 8:05 the following morning, Edward received a message from Jonathan Pierce.

The document conservator had examined the tenth-day acknowledgment.

The opaque strip could be lifted without damaging the underlying paper.

The covered name had been recovered.

Edward opened the attachment.

For several seconds, he did not move.

The name was not Victoria Aurelius.

It was not Margaret Ellison.

It was not anyone from either family.

The receiving guardian's name was:

**MARTHA VOSS.**

Edward read it again.

Martha Voss.

The former hospital administrator.

The woman who had kept a duplicate ninth-day register.

The woman who had witnessed the two infants leaving the private nursery.

The woman who had told them she had never received either child.

Edward immediately called Pierce.

"Have you verified the signature?"

"Not yet."

"Do it."

"Edward, are you certain?"

"The name on the tenth-day acknowledgment is Martha Voss."

Silence.

Then Pierce said, "That means she wasn't just a witness."

"No."

Edward looked at the document again.

"She was the receiving guardian."

Martha Voss arrived at the meeting room three hours later.

She carried no handbag.

She looked at Edward, then at Pierce.

"You asked me to come urgently."

Edward placed the tenth-day acknowledgment on the table.

Martha's eyes moved across the page.

When she reached the name, she stopped.

For a moment, her expression did not change.

Then she sat down.

"Where did you get this?"

"From the legal archive."

She looked at the signature.

"That's my name."

"Did you sign it?"

Martha did not answer.

Edward waited.

Finally, she said, "Yes."

Pierce leaned forward.

"Why did you tell us you had never received either child?"

Martha looked at him.

"Because I didn't."

Edward pointed to the document.

"You signed a receiving acknowledgment."

"I signed a document."

"Which means you received a child."

"No."

Her answer was firm.

"I did not take a child home."

Edward studied her.

"Then what did you receive?"

Martha looked down at the paper.

And for the first time since the investigation began, she appeared genuinely frightened.

"A hospital key," she said.

Edward frowned.

"What?"

Martha looked up.

"On the tenth day, I was instructed to open a room in the old maternity wing."

She paused.

"The child was already inside."

Edward felt the room grow very still.

"Which child?"

Martha shook her head.

"I was never told."

"Did you see her?"

"Yes."

"Was she alone?"

"No."

Edward leaned forward.

"Who was with her?"

Martha's voice dropped.

"A woman."

"Who?"

"I don't know."

"Was she a nurse?"

Martha shook her head.

"She was holding the baby."

Edward glanced at the document.

"Then why does this say you received the child?"

Martha looked at the signature.

"Because I was responsible for the room."

She swallowed.

"Not the baby."

Edward stared at her.

"Then who was?"

Martha slowly looked toward the door.

"The woman who had been waiting for her."
