# **Chapter 4 — A Name in the Old Records**

The following morning began with an argument over pancakes.

Not a serious argument.

Not even an interesting one.

But, according to Nicholas Valmont, it was a matter of considerable injustice.

"Why does Edward get the last pancake?"

Edward didn't look up from his newspaper.

"Because I took it."

"That is not a valid explanation."

"It is the explanation."

Nicholas pointed accusingly at the empty serving plate.

"I was saving that."

"You were talking."

"I was thinking."

"Unfortunately, neither activity reserves food."

Elena sat between them, trying not to laugh.

Catherine shook her head.

"There's more batter in the kitchen. Ask them to make another."

"That's not the point," Nicholas replied.

"What is the point?" Sebastian asked.

"The principle."

Sebastian took a sip of coffee.

"Then I suggest you develop a better principle."

Richard lowered his tablet.

"Can we have breakfast without a debate?"

Nicholas immediately sat back.

"Of course, Father."

Elena quietly reached for the jam.

It was strange how comforting such arguments could be.

For a moment, she almost forgot that anything had changed.

Then she noticed Isabelle watching them.

Isabelle sat beside Catherine, her hands resting neatly beside her plate. She had been quiet throughout the exchange.

Elena turned toward her.

"Would you like some more pancakes? I think there's still some in the kitchen."

Isabelle shook her head.

"No, thank you. I've had enough."

"All right."

Nicholas glanced at Isabelle.

"Don't worry. The pancake dispute is a long-standing tradition."

Edward folded his newspaper.

"It began because you refused to share."

"I was six."

"And apparently you haven't improved."

Elena laughed.

Isabelle smiled too, although her expression remained slightly uncertain.

She was watching a family whose members seemed to know exactly when to interrupt one another, when to tease, and when to ignore each other.

It was a language she had not learned yet.

After breakfast, Elena went to the library to collect a book she had left there.

She found Isabelle standing near the tall windows.

The younger girl was looking at the garden below.

"Good morning," Elena said.

Isabelle turned.

"Good morning."

"Are you looking for something?"

"No. I just wanted to see the garden."

Elena walked toward the window.

"It's quite nice in the morning. The gardeners usually finish the flower beds before lunch."

Isabelle looked outside again.

"Do you spend much time here?"

"In the garden?"

"In the library. And around the house."

Elena considered it.

"Quite a lot. I study here when my room feels too quiet. Sometimes I read outside if the weather is good."

"Do you have a favourite place?"

"The little greenhouse."

Isabelle looked at her.

"The greenhouse?"

"Yes. It's behind the rose garden. It's not particularly impressive, but I like it."

"Could you show me?"

Elena smiled.

"Of course."

They left the library together.

The greenhouse was smaller than the grand glass conservatories found in the estates of wealthy families.

It was an older structure, with white-painted frames and a narrow stone path running between rows of plants.

Inside, the air smelled faintly of damp soil and leaves.

Elena opened the door.

"Here it is."

Isabelle stepped inside.

Sunlight fell through the glass roof, illuminating the small pots arranged along the shelves.

"These are yours?" she asked.

"Some of them."

Elena pointed toward a row of herbs.

"That's basil. Those are mint plants. I tried growing tomatoes last year, but the results were disappointing."

"What happened?"

"I forgot to water them during examination week."

Isabelle laughed.

"That's unfortunate."

"Very. I was quite proud of them until they died."

They walked farther inside.

Isabelle stopped beside a small plant with several new leaves.

"What is this one?"

"Rosemary."

"It looks healthy."

"Mr. Harris says I have finally learned not to overwater everything."

Isabelle smiled.

For a while, they talked about the plants.

It was easy conversation.

Neither had to mention the DNA results, the hospital, or the complicated question of which girl belonged where.

Eventually, Isabelle spoke.

"Elena?"

"Hm?"

"Can I ask you something?"

"Of course."

"Do you hate me?"

Elena stopped.

She turned toward her.

"Why would I hate you?"

"Because everything changed when I arrived."

Elena was quiet for a moment.

Then she placed a small gardening tool back on the shelf.

"Everything changed before you arrived."

Isabelle lowered her eyes.

"But I came here."

"Yes."

"And now everyone has to make room for me."

Elena considered her words.

"That's true."

Isabelle looked up, perhaps surprised by the straightforward answer.

Elena continued.

"But you didn't create the situation. You didn't ask for it to happen. Neither did I."

"I know, but—"

"And I don't think either of us should have to spend every day apologising for being born."

Isabelle stared at her.

Elena gave a small shrug.

"That would be exhausting."

For the first time since arriving at the mansion, Isabelle laughed without seeming to force it.

"You're right."

"I usually am."

"Usually?"

"Don't tell Nicholas. He'll become unbearable."

Isabelle smiled.

Then her expression became thoughtful.

"Thank you."

"For what?"

"For speaking to me normally."

Elena looked at her.

"You're a person, Isabelle. I don't know what else I'm supposed to do."

Isabelle didn't answer immediately.

She looked down at the little rosemary plant.

"I think I was afraid you would see me as someone who came to take everything away."

Elena followed her gaze.

"I don't think you can take away eighteen years."

The words were gentle.

But they were also honest.

Isabelle nodded slowly.

"No. I suppose not."

They stood together in the quiet greenhouse for a little longer.

Neither noticed Catherine watching them from the garden path.

At the same time, Edward was sitting in the Valmont Holdings headquarters, reviewing the hospital investigation.

He had requested access to every document connected to the births.

The hospital had provided the available registration records, medical summaries, and administrative correspondence.

But the missing original transfer form remained a problem.

His assistant, Daniel, entered the office carrying another folder.

"Mr. Valmont, the hospital's legal department has sent the supplementary records."

Edward accepted it.

"Anything useful?"

"They've confirmed that the secondary registration was created three days after the original birth entries."

"Three days?"

"Yes."

Edward opened the folder.

The first few pages contained routine administrative information.

Then he reached the relevant section.

Two newborns.

Two sets of identification numbers.

Two separate entries.

He compared the dates.

The births had taken place in the same hospital on the same night.

That much was already known.

But there was something else.

One of the records had been entered into the system at 2:17 a.m.

The second had been entered at 5:46 a.m.

Edward frowned.

"Why is there a three-hour gap?"

Daniel looked at the page.

"The hospital says the system was undergoing maintenance that night."

"Was it?"

"That's what their current report states."

Edward leaned back.

"Who was responsible for entering the information?"

"The original clerk has retired. The supervising administrator died several years ago."

"And the nurses?"

"Some are still employed. Others have left."

Edward closed the folder.

"Get a list of everyone who worked in the maternity ward that night."

"Yes, sir."

"And find out whether any of them transferred to another hospital."

Daniel made a note.

"Understood."

As Daniel turned to leave, Edward spoke again.

"One more thing."

"Yes?"

"Don't let the hospital know we're looking into every person who handled those records."

Daniel paused.

"Do you suspect someone inside the hospital?"

"I suspect that we don't know enough to rule anyone out."

By lunchtime, Elena had returned to her university.

She was sitting in the cafeteria with Clara, who was attempting to finish a presentation while eating a sandwich.

"You've been staring at that page for five minutes," Clara said.

Elena looked down.

Her notebook was open.

The same sentence had been written three times.

"I was thinking."

"About your family?"

"Partly."

Clara put down her sandwich.

"How is the other girl?"

"Isabelle?"

"Yes."

"She's nice."

"That's good."

"We talked this morning."

"And?"

Elena smiled slightly.

"I showed her the greenhouse."

Clara waited.

"That's it?"

"That's it."

Clara nodded.

"Sometimes that's enough."

Elena looked at her.

"Do you think it's strange?"

"What?"

"Having someone else suddenly become part of your family."

Clara considered the question.

"I think it would be strange if it wasn't strange."

Elena laughed.

"That makes no sense."

"It makes perfect sense. You just don't like the answer."

Clara returned to her presentation.

After a moment, she added, "You don't have to decide how you feel about everything immediately."

Elena looked at her notebook.

That was something she had been trying to do.

But it was difficult.

Every conversation seemed to contain a question nobody was asking aloud.

Was she still a daughter?

Was Isabelle more entitled to the family?

Would her brothers eventually become closer to their biological sister?

Would Catherine and Richard continue treating Elena the same way?

And if they did, would that be fair to Isabelle?

There were too many questions.

She closed her notebook.

"You're right."

Clara glanced at her.

"I know."

"Don't become Nicholas."

"Who is Nicholas?"

"My brother."

"Then I definitely won't."

That afternoon, Richard received an unexpected visitor.

It was Mr. Laurent, the family's legal adviser.

He entered the study carrying a thin document folder.

"Mr. Valmont, I've reviewed the older legal records."

Richard gestured for him to sit.

"Have you found something?"

"Possibly."

Mr. Laurent opened the folder.

"The original family registration documents list Elena as your daughter. Her legal adoption status was never separately recorded because the hospital documentation was accepted as proof of parentage."

Richard frowned.

"So?"

"So the current situation is more complicated than simply changing a name in the records."

Catherine, who had joined the meeting, looked concerned.

"Explain."

"The hospital must first establish what happened. If the records were deliberately altered, the matter could involve criminal conduct. We should not make any irreversible changes until that investigation is complete."

Richard nodded.

"That is sensible."

Mr. Laurent turned a page.

"There is also the matter of inheritance."

Catherine's expression tightened.

"Must we discuss that now?"

"We should at least understand the implications."

Richard looked at the document.

"Continue."

"The Valmont family trust has provisions for biological descendants, legally adopted children, and certain designated beneficiaries. Isabelle's position is straightforward once her identity is formally confirmed."

"And Elena?" Catherine asked.

"Her position depends on the original trust documents and the legal status established after the investigation."

Catherine leaned forward.

"She has been our daughter for eighteen years."

"I understand, Madam."

"She was raised here. She has attended school here. She has grown up with this family. She is not a stranger."

Mr. Laurent remained respectful.

"That history matters. But we must distinguish between family intentions and legal provisions."

Richard was silent.

He had spent much of his adult life dealing with contracts, acquisitions, negotiations, and complicated corporate structures.

He was accustomed to problems that could be solved by examining documents.

This was different.

There was no document that could tell him how to explain to Elena that the life she had known might no longer be legally defined in the same way.

He closed the folder.

"We will review the trust."

Mr. Laurent nodded.

"And until then?"

"Nothing changes."

Catherine looked at him.

"Nothing?"

Richard met her gaze.

"Nothing that would make either girl feel less secure."

That evening, the family gathered in the sitting room.

Catherine had suggested watching a film.

Nicholas had selected an action movie.

Sebastian had objected.

Edward had suggested a documentary.

Nobody had listened to Edward.

The final choice was a comedy that Nicholas claimed was a masterpiece.

"It has excellent reviews," he said.

"From whom?" Sebastian asked.

"People with taste."

"That doesn't narrow it down."

Elena sat on the sofa with a bowl of popcorn.

Isabelle sat in an armchair nearby.

Catherine and Richard shared the large sofa, while Sebastian and Edward occupied the seats nearest the fireplace.

Nicholas took the remote.

"Everyone ready?"

"No," Sebastian replied.

Nicholas pressed play anyway.

For the first fifteen minutes, everything went smoothly.

Then Nicholas began explaining the jokes before they happened.

"Watch this part. This is where the man—"

"Stop talking," Sebastian said.

"But it's important."

"It's a film. We can see it."

"You don't appreciate storytelling."

Edward glanced at Nicholas.

"Neither do the writers, apparently."

Elena nearly choked on her popcorn.

Isabelle laughed.

Nicholas looked pleased.

"See? Isabelle understands."

"She laughed at Edward," Sebastian said.

Nicholas considered this.

"An unfortunate development."

The film continued.

Halfway through, Elena noticed Isabelle looking at the popcorn bowl.

She held it out.

"Would you like some?"

"Thank you."

Isabelle took a small handful.

A few minutes later, she leaned forward.

"Could you pass the remote?"

Nicholas handed it to her.

"Careful. It's a powerful instrument."

Isabelle smiled.

"I'll try not to misuse it."

Elena watched them.

The scene was ordinary.

Almost painfully ordinary.

And perhaps that was what made it important.

Isabelle was beginning to participate in the little things.

Not because anyone had instructed her to.

But because she was slowly finding her way into the household.

Later that night, Elena went upstairs.

As she reached the corridor, she noticed light coming from the blue suite.

The door was slightly open.

She heard Catherine speaking inside.

"Would you like anything else?"

"No, thank you."

"All right. Get some rest."

Elena continued toward her room.

Then she heard Isabelle's voice.

"Mom?"

Elena stopped.

Catherine answered softly.

"Yes?"

"Could I ask you something?"

"Of course."

"Do you think Elena will ever stop feeling uncomfortable around me?"

There was a pause.

"I don't think she feels uncomfortable around you."

"But she must."

"She is adjusting. So are you."

Isabelle was quiet.

Then she spoke again.

"Sometimes I feel as if everyone is trying to make sure I belong here."

Catherine's reply was gentle.

"Because we want you to belong here."

"I know. But I don't want her to feel like she has to become someone else."

"Then remember that she doesn't."

Elena stood in the corridor for another moment.

She had not intended to listen.

She stepped away quietly and entered her own room.

At 11:18 p.m., Edward received another message.

This time, it was from the hospital investigator.

**We located a former maternity ward employee who may remember the night in question.**

Edward immediately replied.

**Name?**

A few seconds passed.

Then the answer arrived.

**Margaret Ellis. Former senior maternity nurse. Retired twelve years ago.**

Edward searched the name in the attached records.

There she was.

Margaret Ellis.

Her name appeared on the duty roster for the night Elena and Isabelle were born.

But what caught his attention was the handwritten note beside her name.

**Transferred to private medical records department — temporary assignment.**

The transfer had taken place only six days after the births.

Edward read it again.

Why would a maternity nurse be temporarily transferred to the medical records department?

He called Daniel.

The call was answered after two rings.

"Sir?"

"Find Margaret Ellis."

"Is this related to the hospital investigation?"

"Yes."

"Should I arrange an interview?"

"Not yet."

Edward looked at the old duty roster.

"First, find out where she is living and whether she has spoken to anyone about the case."

"Understood."

He ended the call.

Then he opened the hospital records again.

Two girls.

One night.

One missing transfer document.

And a nurse who had changed departments less than a week later.

It was not proof of anything.

But it was the first name that connected the missing records to a person.

Across the city, in a modest retirement apartment, an elderly woman sat at her kitchen table.

A telephone rested beside her.

She had received an unfamiliar call earlier that evening.

The caller had asked about her employment at the hospital eighteen years ago.

Specifically, the night two newborn girls had been admitted to the maternity ward.

Margaret Ellis had said she remembered very little.

That was not entirely true.

She remembered the night.

She remembered the unusual request.

She remembered being told that a certain document had to be placed in a separate file.

And she remembered being instructed not to discuss it with the parents.

For eighteen years, she had convinced herself that it was no longer her responsibility.

But now someone was asking questions.

She walked to an old cabinet and opened the bottom drawer.

Inside was a small envelope.

The paper had yellowed with age.

Her name was written on the front.

Underneath it were four words:

**Keep this until requested.**

Margaret stared at the envelope.

Then she slowly sat down.

For the first time in eighteen years, she wondered whether keeping silent had been the wrong decision.

*End of Chapter 4.*
