# **Chapter 46 — The Woman Behind the Initials**

The payment file arrived at the Valmont legal office shortly after eight in the morning.

Edward had requested the complete original record, including the supporting documents, account statements, and correspondence attached to the final payment.

What he received was considerably more complicated than he had expected.

The file contained thirty-seven pages.

Twenty-six concerned ordinary expenses.

The remaining eleven concerned a child whose identity had never been properly recorded.

Edward read the final page again.

**Do not use the name Voss again. The mother has recognised it.**

He placed the page beside the photograph of Martha Voss.

"That sentence changes things," Jonathan Pierce said.

"It changes what we need to investigate."

"Someone was using Martha's name."

"Or someone was using a name that Martha had previously used."

Jonathan looked at him.

"Do you think Martha was involved?"

"I think we need to stop treating every document as though the name written on it identifies the person who acted."

Edward opened the file to the earliest payment.

The first expense had been authorised on the eleventh day after the births.

The final payment had been made on the ninety-second day.

Between them were references to temporary accommodation, medical supplies, transport, and a monthly allowance.

There was also a separate entry.

**IDENTITY VERIFICATION — EXTERNAL CONSULTANT.**

No consultant's name.

Only an account reference.

Edward copied it into his notes.

"Whoever arranged the payments expected this child to remain outside ordinary records for at least three months," he said.

Jonathan examined the final entry.

"And then?"

"We don't know."

"Perhaps the arrangement ended."

"Perhaps."

Edward turned another page.

A small handwritten note had been attached to the payment record.

**The receiving woman has been informed that the mother is not to be approached.**

Below it was a date.

The thirtieth day.

Edward looked at the calendar on his desk.

The payment file was not merely recording expenses.

It was documenting instructions given to the people responsible for keeping a child.

And someone had changed those instructions more than once.

Martha Voss arrived at the Valmont legal office at 10:30 a.m.

She had agreed to return after Edward requested a second interview.

This time, she brought a folder of her own.

"I found something," she said before sitting down.

Edward looked at the folder.

"What?"

"An old employment record."

Martha opened it and removed a photocopied personnel form.

"My hospital employment file. I kept a copy when I left."

Edward examined the document.

It contained her name, address, employment dates, and emergency contact details.

"Why is this relevant?"

"Because I was not the only Martha Voss working at the hospital."

Edward looked up.

Martha continued.

"There was another woman. She worked in the maternity records department. We were not related. We had different positions, but people occasionally confused our files."

Jonathan leaned forward.

"Was she involved in the private nursery?"

"Not directly, as far as I know."

"Do you remember her name?"

Martha nodded.

"Margaret Voss."

Edward remained still.

The name was familiar.

Not because it had appeared in the original hospital records, but because the rental voucher had contained the instruction not to use the name Voss again.

"Was Margaret Voss employed on the tenth day?"

"I don't know. She left the hospital before I did."

"When?"

"About six months after the births."

"Do you know why?"

Martha hesitated.

"She said she was moving."

"Where?"

"She didn't tell me."

Edward wrote down the name.

"Did she have access to the private nursery?"

"She had access to maternity records. Whether she had permission to enter the private nursery is another question."

"Did she know Marcus Morelli?"

Martha considered it.

"I saw them speak once."

"When?"

"I can't remember exactly. Around the time the two children were in the private nursery."

"What were they discussing?"

"I don't know. I was walking past the records office. They stopped talking when I approached."

"Did you recognise the other person?"

"Yes. Charles Vale."

Edward exchanged a glance with Jonathan.

Martha noticed.

"You know something."

"We have evidence that Charles Vale coordinated several arrangements involving the children."

"Then perhaps Margaret Voss can explain why my name was used."

"Do you know where she is now?"

"No."

Edward closed the personnel file.

"Thank you, Ms Voss. We'll verify the employment records."

Martha stood.

Before leaving, she turned back.

"There's one more thing."

Edward waited.

"When I signed the tenth-day administrative receipt, I was told the child in Room Fourteen was not to be entered into the ordinary nursery register."

"Who told you?"

"Marcus Morelli."

"Did he explain why?"

"He said the child was being protected."

Martha looked uncomfortable.

"I asked him from whom."

"What did he say?"

"That was when he told me not to ask questions about the family."

She left the room.

Edward remained seated.

For the first time, the possibility of two women sharing the same name had become more than an administrative inconvenience.

Someone might have deliberately exploited that confusion.

And if so, the woman who had entered Room Fourteen might never have been Martha Voss.

At the Valmont Estate, breakfast had become a little less formal.

Isabelle was reviewing a document while Catherine read the morning correspondence.

Sebastian had a tablet open beside his plate.

Nicholas was attempting to persuade Elena to eat another piece of toast.

"I've already had two."

"Three."

"That doesn't make it better."

"You're studying too much."

Elena gave him a look.

"Is eating toast a compulsory condition of university applications?"

"It should be."

Richard lowered his newspaper.

"Let her eat what she wants, Nicholas."

Nicholas sat back.

"Fine."

Elena took a sip of tea.

"Thank you."

Richard returned to his newspaper, but Catherine noticed the small smile on Elena's face.

She had been watching her youngest daughter more carefully since the hospital records had surfaced.

Sometimes Elena seemed perfectly normal.

She joked with Nicholas, argued with Sebastian, discussed university plans, and helped Isabelle with her foundation work.

At other times, Catherine caught her staring at family photographs.

Not with anger.

With the expression of someone trying to remember whether a familiar place had always been familiar.

Catherine wanted to tell her that nothing about the past eighteen years had been a lie.

But she could not promise that.

"Elena," Isabelle said.

"Hm?"

"Would you be willing to look at another section of the scholarship application?"

Elena glanced at the folder.

"Which section?"

"The appeal process."

"Sure."

Isabelle turned the folder towards her.

"I want students to be able to request a review if they believe their application was rejected unfairly."

"That's sensible."

"But I don't want the review process to become another opportunity for influential people to pressure the foundation."

Elena considered the wording.

"Then the people reviewing appeals shouldn't be the same people who made the original decision."

"An independent panel?"

"Yes. And there should be written reasons for every decision."

Isabelle nodded.

"That would make it easier to identify inconsistent decisions."

Sebastian looked up.

"You're both designing an administrative system that will require additional staff."

Isabelle looked at him.

"Would you prefer a programme that makes arbitrary decisions?"

"No."

"Then perhaps you should stop complaining."

Nicholas laughed.

Sebastian gave him a warning look.

Elena smiled into her tea.

For a brief moment, the room felt almost like it had before the DNA results.

Almost.

At the Aurelius Estate, Damien was reviewing a report from the former Family Office.

He had found something unusual.

The code EFO-SL/07 had not simply remained active after Marcus Morelli's departure.

It had been transferred.

The transfer record was dated twelve days after the births.

The receiving office was listed as:

**SPECIAL ADMINISTRATIVE SERVICES — PRIVATE CLIENT DIVISION.**

Damien read the entry again.

"Adrian."

His brother entered the room.

"What?"

"Look at this."

Adrian examined the screen.

"Who authorised the transfer?"

"That's the problem. The authorisation field contains the Chairman's office code."

"Father's?"

"Yes."

"Did he approve it?"

"I asked him. He doesn't remember authorising any transfer."

Adrian's expression tightened.

"Could the code have been copied?"

"Possibly. The old system accepted certain internal credentials without requiring a separate signature if the request came through the Family Office."

"Who had access?"

"Three administrators. Two are deceased. The third was Marcus Morelli."

Adrian studied the record.

"Then Morelli may have transferred the authority himself."

"Or someone used his credentials."

"Can we identify the receiving office?"

Damien shook his head.

"The division was dissolved seventeen years ago. Its records were transferred to a company that no longer exists."

Adrian leaned back.

"Convenient."

"Too convenient."

The door opened.

Alexander entered.

"What have you found?"

Adrian explained.

Alexander listened without interrupting.

When Damien finished, Alexander examined the transfer date.

"Twelve days after the births," he said.

"Yes."

"Whoever moved the authority knew the original arrangements had already failed."

Adrian nodded.

"That's our working assumption."

Alexander looked at the screen for several seconds.

"Find the receiving office's financial records."

Damien nodded.

"And Adrian?"

"Yes?"

"Do not assume Morelli was the person who benefited from this."

Adrian looked at him.

"Father, he left the Family Office nine days after the births."

"That makes him a person of interest. It does not make him the person responsible."

Alexander returned the report.

"I want evidence before conclusions."

The contractor's documents were delivered to the forensic handwriting examiner that afternoon.

Edward requested three comparisons:

1. Martha Voss's genuine signature from her employment file.
2. The signature on the tenth-day administrative receipt.
3. The signature on the rental property authorisation.

The examiner agreed to provide an initial assessment within forty-eight hours.

While waiting, Edward contacted the hospital's former personnel department.

The first response was disappointing.

Margaret Voss's employment record had been archived under a different personnel number.

The second response was more interesting.

The personnel number belonged to a maternity records clerk who had resigned on the eighteenth day after the births.

Edward stared at the date.

"She resigned nine days after the private nursery released the children," Jonathan said.

"Not quite."

Edward turned the page.

"She submitted her resignation on the eighteenth day. Her final working day was the twenty-eighth."

"Ten additional days."

"Yes."

"Did anyone approve it?"

"Charles Vale's office received a copy."

Jonathan frowned.

"Why would a private legal office receive an employee's resignation?"

"That's what we're going to find out."

Edward requested the resignation letter.

It arrived an hour later.

The letter was short.

Margaret Voss cited personal circumstances and requested immediate release from her position.

There was no mention of another job.

No forwarding address.

No explanation.

But at the bottom of the page was a handwritten note.

**Personnel transfer completed under external authority. Do not forward correspondence to previous address.**

Edward read it twice.

The note had been added after the resignation was submitted.

The handwriting was unfamiliar.

He placed it beside the voucher.

Another instruction.

Another anonymous authority.

Another person whose identity had been removed from the record.

At 6:40 p.m., Elena sat in the library with her laptop.

She had begun preparing for her Singapore interview.

Her notes were arranged into three sections:

- Why she wanted to study abroad.
- What she hoped to learn.
- What she wanted to do after returning.

The third section was the most difficult.

She did not yet know what she wanted her career to become.

She knew she liked understanding how organisations worked. She enjoyed planning, analysing problems, and finding practical solutions.

But she didn't want to pretend she had a complete life plan at eighteen.

She deleted a paragraph that sounded too polished.

Then she wrote:

*I don't believe I need to know exactly what I will become before I begin learning. I want to develop the ability to make informed decisions, understand how organisations affect people, and build something that can stand on its own merits.*

She read it once.

Then again.

It sounded like her.

A knock interrupted her thoughts.

Isabelle entered carrying two cups of tea.

"Can I sit?"

"Of course."

Isabelle placed one cup beside Elena.

"You're preparing for the interview?"

"Trying to."

"How's it going?"

"Better than yesterday."

"That's progress."

Elena smiled.

Isabelle looked at the notes.

"You're writing about your future?"

"More or less."

"Do you know what you want?"

"Not completely."

"Neither do I."

Elena glanced at her.

Isabelle sat opposite her.

"I used to think that if I ever found my biological family, everything else would become easier."

"And now?"

"Now I have a family, but I still have to decide what I want to do with my life."

Elena looked down at her notes.

"That sounds familiar."

Isabelle smiled faintly.

"I suppose we have that in common."

For a while, neither spoke.

Then Isabelle asked, "Do you ever wonder what would have happened if none of this had been discovered?"

Elena considered the question.

"Sometimes."

"Would you have wanted to know?"

"Yes."

"Even if it changed everything?"

Elena nodded.

"I think not knowing would have been worse."

Isabelle looked at her tea.

"I agree."

The silence that followed was not uncomfortable.

It was simply the silence of two people who understood that some questions did not have easy answers.

At 8:15 p.m., Edward received a message from the handwriting examiner.

The preliminary comparison was complete.

He opened the attached report.

The signature on the tenth-day administrative receipt was not consistent with Martha Voss's genuine handwriting.

The rental authorisation was also inconsistent.

However, both signatures showed similarities to one another.

The examiner added one further observation.

The same individual appeared to have written both signatures, possibly while attempting to imitate Martha Voss's name.

Edward read the final paragraph.

**The available samples are insufficient to identify the writer. Additional authenticated handwriting samples are recommended.**

Jonathan looked over his shoulder.

"So Martha didn't sign either document."

"That's what the examiner believes."

"Then who did?"

Edward scrolled to the last attachment.

It was a scanned page from Margaret Voss's personnel file.

Her signature appeared at the bottom.

Edward placed it beside the two disputed signatures.

The handwriting was not identical.

But the way the letter M was formed was strikingly similar.

Jonathan stared at the three documents.

"Margaret."

"Possibly."

"She could have been the person using Martha's name."

"Or someone copied her handwriting too."

Edward enlarged the image.

There was a handwritten annotation beside Margaret's signature.

**M. V. — authorised to act on behalf of the receiving representative.**

No date.

No explanation.

No witness.

Edward leaned back.

For eighteen years, the investigation had been centred on a missing daughter, two infants, and a set of conflicting records.

Now another name had emerged.

Margaret Voss.

A hospital employee who resigned shortly after the children were released.

A woman whose name appeared in the personnel records, whose handwriting resembled the disputed signatures, and whose authority had somehow been accepted by a private legal office.

Edward picked up his phone.

"Find her."

At the Aurelius Estate, Victoria was reading an old letter.

It had been written by Eleanor Vale.

The letter contained no explanation of the hospital events.

Only a short paragraph.

*I cannot promise that the children will remember us. I cannot promise that their mothers will understand what happened. But I can promise that I will not allow either child to be treated as an object that can be transferred whenever an arrangement becomes inconvenient.*

Victoria read the paragraph again.

She had received the letter years earlier, but at the time she had not understood its significance.

Now every sentence seemed to contain another question.

Alexander entered quietly.

"Still reading it?"

Victoria nodded.

"Do you think Eleanor was trying to protect them?"

"I think she believed she was."

"That isn't the same as knowing she succeeded."

"No."

Victoria folded the letter.

"Alexander, what if we find her and she doesn't want to come home?"

He sat beside her.

"Then we will have to respect that."

"What if she doesn't believe us?"

"Then we will have to give her reasons to believe us."

Victoria looked at him.

"And if she is angry?"

"She has every right to be."

Victoria's eyes lowered.

For eighteen years, she had imagined meeting her daughter again.

She had imagined a child.

A teenager.

A young woman.

But she had rarely imagined the possibility that the young woman might have built a life without them and might not want that life disturbed.

She placed the letter on the table.

"I don't want to take anything away from her."

Alexander took her hand.

"Then we won't."

Neither of them noticed Adrian standing in the doorway.

He had arrived with a new report.

The former Special Administrative Services division had left behind one surviving employee register.

Most of the names were ordinary.

Accountants.

Administrative assistants.

Records officers.

But one entry stood out.

**MARGARET VOSS — TEMPORARY RECORDS COORDINATOR.**

Appointment date: ninth day after the births.

Departure date: twenty-eighth day after the births.

The office address was no longer valid.

And beneath the entry, in a different hand, was a final note.

**Transferred to the receiving household.**

Adrian looked at his father.

"She's not simply a former hospital employee."

Alexander took the document.

"Then who was she?"

Adrian shook his head.

"We don't know."

He placed a second page on the table.

It contained a name and an address.

The address belonged to a small residential property that had been sold seventeen years earlier.

The name on the original lease was not Margaret Voss.

It was **MARGARET ELLISON**.

Victoria went still.

The same Margaret Ellison who had received a baby on the ninth day.

The same woman who had called that baby Anna.

And the same woman whose name had been used to open the private account that paid for the children's temporary arrangements.

For the first time, the investigation had connected the hospital employee, the financial records, and one of the receiving guardians through a single identity.

But it raised a more troubling question.

Had Margaret Ellison been using another name?

Or had someone deliberately placed her name into records that belonged to someone else?

And if Margaret Voss and Margaret Ellison were the same woman, who had been living in the rental property under the name Victoria A.?

The answer was still missing.

But the trail was no longer cold.
