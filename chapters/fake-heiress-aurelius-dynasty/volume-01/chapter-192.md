# **Chapter 192 — The Address That Could Not Nominate Anyone**

Edward received the C-19 file at 8:36 in the morning.

It contained eleven pages.

The first was an inventory.

The second was a routing summary.

The remaining nine were correspondence extracts, each marked with a different level of restriction.

He read the inventory twice.

**C-19 — Temporary Correspondence Contact Location**

**Status:** Closed for routine administrative use.

**Permitted function:** Receipt and forwarding of designated correspondence.

**Prohibited functions:** Appointment, identity verification, personal representation, receiving authority, substantive instruction.

Edward placed the inventory beside the day-46 alternative candidate note.

The contradiction was immediate.

A location that could receive and forward correspondence had apparently become the nomination source for an independent verifier.

It could not appoint anyone.

It could not authorise anyone.

It could not even make a substantive decision on behalf of the protected principal.

Yet someone had used it as the origin of a proposed appointment.

“That's not a minor classification error,” Sophie said.

“No.”

“Could the nomination have been submitted by someone physically using the address?”

“Possibly. But the file doesn't say that.”

Edward turned to the routing summary.

It listed three separate entries.

**Day 44 — C-19 contact route confirmed for correspondence forwarding.**

**Day 45 — OC-19 candidate Adrian Holt proposed by Programme Administration.**

**Day 46 — Alternative candidate may be considered; nomination source recorded as C-19.**

**Day 47 — Adrian Holt's proposed appointment declined.**

There was no fourth entry recording an alternative candidate.

No name.

No appointment instrument.

No acceptance.

No independent verification.

The note had survived in the file, but the proposed replacement had not.

“Who entered C-19 as the source?” Sophie asked.

“That's what we're going to find out.”

Edward opened the correspondence extracts.

The first was a day-44 confirmation from the restricted correspondence office.

It stated that C-19 remained available for forwarding notices connected with the protected-principal communication procedure.

The second was a day-45 internal message concerning Adrian Holt.

The third was the day-46 note.

The fourth was a day-47 decision from Dr Evelyn Shaw.

The fifth was an instruction to preserve the rejected proposal without treating it as an appointment.

The sixth was a reminder that OC-19 remained vacant.

The seventh was a closure summary.

The final two were redacted routing records.

Edward stopped at the second-to-last page.

“Look at this.”

Sophie leaned over.

The entry was brief.

**Originating account:** Restricted Correspondence Administration.

**Destination:** Programme Administration.

**Reference:** OC-19 / Alternative Candidate.

**Source classification:** C-19.

**Named operator:** Not recorded.

There was a timestamp.

Day 46, 10:18.

Edward checked the time against the note.

The handwritten note had been added to the working package at 10:42.

Twenty-four minutes later.

“Someone sent a message from the restricted correspondence office,” Sophie said.

“Yes.”

“Then someone in Programme Administration converted its source classification into a nomination source.”

“That's one possibility.”

“Or the message itself described C-19 as the source.”

Edward nodded.

“That distinction matters.”

He looked again at the routing record.

A source classification could describe where a message entered the system.

It did not necessarily identify who had written it.

Nor did it establish that the location had authority to nominate anyone.

The record contained no attachment.

No signed instruction.

No candidate's name.

Just a reference, a classification, and a destination.

“Can we retrieve the original message?” Sophie asked.

“The file says the message was retained in the correspondence gateway.”

“Then we need the gateway record.”

Edward picked up his phone.

“Already requested.”

At 9:20, the call from Marianne Vale arrived.

She sounded as composed as she had during their previous meeting.

“I reviewed your request concerning C-19,” she said.

“Thank you.”

“I want to clarify one point before you proceed. C-19 was never recognised as an appointment source.”

“Yet the day-46 record identifies it as one.”

“It identifies it as the recorded source classification. Those are not interchangeable.”

“Who was responsible for making that distinction?”

“The originating office should have supplied a valid nomination instrument. My office was responsible for recording and checking it.”

“And if the instrument was missing?”

“The appointment could not proceed.”

Edward glanced at the inventory.

“Was an alternative candidate ever formally nominated?”

“No completed nomination appears in the register.”

“Was a name submitted informally?”

A pause followed.

“I can confirm that an alternative nomination was discussed.”

“By whom?”

“I cannot identify an individual from the appointment register.”

“Was it discussed by the restricted correspondence office?”

“I can confirm that the correspondence route was involved in transmitting the proposal.”

“Transmitting or originating?”

“I cannot establish authorship from the routing entry.”

Edward wrote that down.

“Did your office ask C-19 to nominate someone?”

“No.”

“Did C-19 have authority to do so?”

“No.”

“Did anyone have authority to treat its correspondence function as a nomination authority?”

“No.”

The answers were clear.

The reason for the mistake was not.

“Why wasn't the classification corrected?” Edward asked.

“Because the entry was not sufficient to create an appointment, and no appointment was entered.”

“That explains why no one was appointed. It doesn't explain why the record was allowed to retain an inaccurate description.”

Marianne was silent for a moment.

“An annotation was added to the closure file.”

“What annotation?”

“That C-19 was a correspondence source only and must not be treated as an originating authority.”

“Was it added before or after the day-46 note?”

“After the day-47 decision.”

Edward looked at Sophie.

“Who added it?”

“My office.”

“On whose instruction?”

“On my own review of the appointment record.”

“Did you investigate who had entered the original classification?”

“No. My immediate responsibility was to prevent it from being used as evidence of an appointment.”

Edward understood the limitation.

Marianne had corrected the legal effect of the entry.

She had not investigated its origin.

“Can you send us the annotation and the underlying routing record?”

“I can provide the records within my appointment scope. The original gateway message will require release from the correspondence office.”

“Thank you.”

Before ending the call, Marianne added something.

“There is another matter.”

Edward waited.

“The day-46 alternative candidate discussion was not the first time C-19 had appeared in an OC-19 context.”

His pen stopped.

“When?”

“Day 44. In a routing note attached to the initial proposal.”

“Was that note in the appointment register?”

“No. It was in the correspondence working file.”

“Do you have a copy?”

“I have a reference to it. Not the underlying document.”

Edward thanked her again.

When the call ended, Sophie was already looking through the day-44 entries.

“Two days before the alternative candidate note,” she said.

“And one day before Adrian Holt's proposal.”

“Which means C-19 was involved in the OC-19 process before the first candidate was formally proposed.”

Edward stared at the dates.

That changed the sequence.

They had been treating C-19 as a possible source of a replacement after Adrian Holt's independence was questioned.

But the address had appeared earlier.

Possibly before anyone had decided that Adrian Holt was unsuitable.

“Find the day-44 routing note,” he said.

Sophie opened the document request tracker.

“It's not included in the current release.”

“Then add it.”

She did.

The request went out at 9:47.

In Singapore, Elena had a different problem.

Her demonstration was scheduled for the following afternoon.

The application platform worked.

Mostly.

She had fixed the reviewer reassignment issue, added a field for the authority behind each decision, and prevented the system from treating an administrative acknowledgement as approval.

But the export function was still producing a confusing result.

When a reviewer left a programme, the system displayed the application as incomplete even if the review itself had been finished.

Elena stared at the screen.

“That is technically accurate,” Clara said through the video call.

“It is also deeply irritating.”

“Those two things can coexist.”

“I know. I built the feature.”

Clara leaned closer to her camera.

“Why not separate the application status from the reviewer status?”

Elena stopped typing.

She looked at the screen again.

The problem was obvious once Clara said it.

The application had not become incomplete.

The reviewer assignment had become vacant.

Those were different records.

Elena opened the workflow diagram and added a separate field.

**Application status:** Review completed.

**Reviewer assignment:** Closed.

**Successor assignment:** Pending.

**Decision authority:** Unchanged.

Clara read the fields.

“That looks better.”

“It also means the programme can continue without pretending someone has been appointed when they haven't.”

“Exactly.”

Elena saved the change.

For a few seconds, she simply watched the updated dashboard.

It was a small improvement.

No dramatic revelation.

No complicated family history.

Just a system that no longer confused one unfinished task with an entirely different one.

Her phone vibrated.

A message from Isabelle appeared.

**Isabelle:** I reviewed the revised export. The separation is clearer now.

**Elena:** Thank you.

**Isabelle:** One concern. If the successor assignment is pending, can the programme still issue a final decision?

Elena considered the question.

**Elena:** Only if the decision authority remains valid and the person issuing it is authorised independently of the vacant reviewer role.

**Isabelle:** Then the platform should show which authority remains active.

**Elena:** I was trying to avoid adding another field.

**Isabelle:** That is not a technical argument.

Elena stared at the message.

Then she laughed.

Clara raised an eyebrow.

“Isabelle?”

“Yes.”

“She's becoming useful.”

“She was always useful. Unfortunately, she knows it.”

Elena added another field.

**Decision authority status:** Active / Suspended / Expired / Unverified.

Clara watched the screen update.

“Now you're finished?”

“No.”

“Of course not.”

“I still need to test what happens when the reviewer assignment is closed but the decision authority is suspended.”

Clara sighed.

“Your idea of a relaxing afternoon is unusual.”

Elena smiled.

“It's a very small afternoon.”

At 11:36, Edward received the first response from the correspondence gateway.

It was not the original message.

It was a system-generated retrieval report.

The gateway had retained the transmission envelope, delivery routing, and attachment index.

The message body was held separately under restricted correspondence controls.

Edward read the report.

**Message reference:** RC-44/OC19.

**Created:** Day 44, 16:12.

**Originating terminal:** RC-ADMIN-02.

**Destination:** Programme Administration.

**Attachment:** One document, restricted.

**Transmission status:** Accepted by destination gateway.

**Named sender:** Blank.

**Operator credential:** Restricted.

He read the attachment description.

**Title:** Preliminary Reviewer Independence Query.

Edward looked up.

“RC-44.”

Sophie came around the desk.

“The day-44 note?”

“Yes.”

The report contained a short description of the attachment's purpose.

It was not a nomination.

It was a query about whether a person who had previously handled receiving-route administration could be considered for independent verification.

The document had been transmitted to Programme Administration before Adrian Holt was formally proposed.

Edward opened the next section.

**Question submitted:** Whether a candidate with previous receiving-route involvement may be considered for a limited original-channel verification function, subject to independent review.

The sender had not named Adrian Holt.

It had not named anyone.

But it had raised the question that later became central to Dr Evelyn Shaw's rejection.

Edward read the final line.

**Requested response:** Confirmation of eligibility criteria before candidate identification.

He sat back.

“This wasn't a replacement proposal.”

“No,” Sophie said.

“It was a request for the rules before anyone nominated a candidate.”

“Which would have been sensible.”

“Yes.”

But the routing record raised another question.

Why had the query been attributed to C-19?

A correspondence location could forward a question.

It could not necessarily originate it.

Edward checked the transmission envelope again.

The originating terminal was RC-ADMIN-02.

The source classification was C-19.

The destination was Programme Administration.

No named sender.

No personal signature.

“Could RC-ADMIN-02 be used by several people?” Sophie asked.

“The credential register should tell us.”

He searched the access schedule.

The terminal was a shared workstation in the restricted correspondence office.

It could be accessed by four credential groups.

Two belonged to administrative support staff.

One belonged to the correspondence supervisor.

The fourth was Gabriel Vale's limited communication credential.

Edward read the restriction beside Gabriel's entry.

**Credential purpose:** Receive designated information, communicate procedural questions, transmit conditional responses, preserve correspondence.

**Prohibited:** Appointment of independent reviewers, receiving identity confirmation, personal representation beyond designated authority, substantive decisions.

Gabriel's credential could have been used to transmit a question.

That did not mean he had written it.

It did not mean P-04 had asked him to write it.

And it certainly did not mean C-19 itself had nominated anyone.

“Four possible access groups,” Sophie said.

“Four groups. Not four people. We don't even know which credential was used.”

“Can the gateway identify it?”

“Not in this report.”

He added a request for the credential-level audit record.

Then he noticed a small note at the bottom of the page.

**Original attachment retained under correspondence preservation reference CP-44/6. Access restricted pending originating-office confirmation.**

Edward read it again.

“There's an originating-office confirmation requirement.”

Sophie nodded.

“And until that is received, we cannot identify the sender.”

“Correct.”

He looked at the clock.

It was 12:04.

They had found the first document in the OC-19 sequence that predated Adrian Holt's nomination.

It did not identify the person who sent it.

But it established something important.

The question of independence had been raised before the candidate was named.

That meant Dr Evelyn Shaw's later rejection had not created the problem.

It had exposed a problem that someone had already recognised.

At 14:10, Edward received a call from Dr Evelyn Shaw.

She had reviewed his request concerning the preliminary independence query.

“I remember the issue,” she said.

“Were you involved in the day-44 query?”

“No. I was not its author.”

“Did you receive a copy before Adrian Holt was proposed?”

“I received the question through the formal review package.”

“When?”

“Day 45.”

Edward opened his notes.

“Did the question influence your assessment?”

“It informed the scope of my assessment. It did not determine the outcome.”

“What was your concern with Adrian Holt?”

“His prior involvement in the receiving route created a conflict with the independent verification function.”

“Was that concern known before the day-44 query?”

“I cannot say who knew what before I received the package.”

“Did you ask for an alternative candidate?”

“I said an alternative could be considered if the originating authority identified someone who met the independence criteria.”

“Did you nominate anyone?”

“No.”

“Did you authorise C-19 to nominate anyone?”

Dr Shaw's answer was immediate.

“No.”

“Was C-19 ever an acceptable nomination authority?”

“No. It was a correspondence contact location. I would not have accepted an appointment proposal from an address without a competent originating authority.”

Edward made a note.

“Was there any reason to believe the query had been sent on behalf of P-04?”

“That would depend on the underlying authority for the communication. The routing label alone would not establish that.”

“Even if the message passed through a channel associated with the protected principal?”

“Especially then. A communication channel does not establish the identity or authority of its sender.”

Edward looked at the report on his desk.

The same distinction had appeared in almost every part of the investigation.

A channel was not a person.

A receipt was not an acknowledgement.

An acknowledgement was not consent.

A proposed appointment was not an appointment.

And now, a correspondence source was not an originating authority.

“Thank you, Doctor.”

“One more thing,” Dr Shaw said.

Edward paused.

“The day-44 query was not asking me to approve Adrian Holt. It was asking what independence would require before a candidate could be considered.”

“I understand.”

“I hope so. I would not want the record to suggest that I rejected a person before assessing the actual conflict.”

“I'll preserve that distinction.”

“Good.”

The call ended.

Sophie looked at him.

“That's the first clear explanation we've had of why the question existed.”

“It's an explanation of the question. Not the sender.”

“Still progress.”

Edward nodded.

He opened a fresh page in the investigation chronology.

**Day 44:** Preliminary query submitted concerning independence criteria for OC-19.

**Day 45:** Query received in formal review package; Adrian Holt proposed.

**Day 46:** Alternative candidate possibility recorded, source classification C-19.

**Day 47:** Adrian Holt declined; no alternative appointed.

He added one more line.

**Outstanding:** Identify originating authority and sender of RC-44/OC19.

At the Aurelius Estate, Alexander received a short report from Adrian.

The report concerned the external investigation's request for access to certain preserved administrative records.

Adrian placed it on the desk.

“The request is narrower than the previous ones.”

Alexander read the first page.

“C-19?”

“Yes.”

“Is it connected to the original identity records?”

“Not directly. It's part of the communication and verification procedure.”

Alexander turned to the second page.

“An independent reviewer was never appointed.”

“No.”

“And a correspondence location was listed as the source of an alternative nomination.”

“That's what the preliminary record says.”

Alexander closed the report.

“Do not allow anyone to interpret that as evidence of who the protected principal is.”

“I haven't.”

“Or evidence that the principal received the communication.”

“I know.”

Alexander looked out of the window.

For years, every incomplete record had seemed to offer a possible answer.

A date.

A transfer.

A name.

A location.

Each one had produced another question.

He had learned that a family could not build certainty from administrative convenience.

“Continue with the original records,” he said. “Not summaries.”

Adrian nodded.

“And make sure Victoria receives the same distinction.”

“I will.”

When Adrian left, Alexander remained at the desk.

He opened the small folder he kept separate from the corporate files.

It contained the preserved family correspondence concerning their missing daughter.

He did not add the C-19 report.

It had no established connection to her identity.

For now, it belonged to the investigation, not to the family.

At 17:22, Edward received the final response from the gateway office.

The credential-level audit had been recovered.

He opened the attachment.

The message had been transmitted using a temporary administrative credential.

Not Gabriel's.

Not the correspondence supervisor's.

One of the two support credentials.

The credential holder's name was available.

Edward read it.

Then he read the attached restriction notice.

The person had been authorised to forward correspondence and prepare routing records.

They had not been authorised to issue substantive instructions.

They had not been authorised to represent P-04.

They had not been authorised to nominate an independent verifier.

Edward looked at Sophie.

“We have an operator.”

“Do we have the sender?”

“No.”

“Then what do we have?”

“A person who used a credential to transmit a question.”

“Did they write it?”

“Not established.”

“Did they receive it from someone else?”

“Not established.”

“Did they know it would be classified under C-19?”

“Not established.”

Sophie exhaled.

“Another incomplete chain.”

“Yes.”

Edward turned to the final page.

It contained an additional audit event.

The preliminary query had been prepared in a different system before being transmitted through the gateway.

The preparation record included a field that had not appeared in the correspondence report.

**Prepared under instruction reference: PA-44/03.**

Edward searched the reference in the closure index.

It appeared once.

Day 44, 14:08.

Two hours before the query was transmitted.

The instruction was listed under Programme Administration.

Its subject was:

**OC-19 — Independence Criteria and Candidate Identification.**

Edward opened the entry.

The instruction had been marked as received.

The author field was blank.

The approving officer field was blank.

But the final line was visible.

**Please obtain the independence criteria before the originating office is asked to identify a replacement. Do not treat the correspondence contact as the nominating authority.**

Edward stared at the sentence.

Someone had explicitly warned against the very mistake that later appeared in the record.

And the instruction had been issued before the day-46 note.

Sophie read it over his shoulder.

“So someone knew C-19 could not nominate anyone.”

“Yes.”

“Then why was it entered as the nomination source?”

Edward looked at the blank author field.

“That is the next question.”

He printed the instruction.

On the reverse, the system had retained a routing annotation.

**Instruction forwarded for action by: D. Mercer.**

Edward did not move.

Daniel Mercer had prepared the retrieval request for PR-06.

He had assembled the closure package.

He had handled the day-160 query.

And now his name appeared on the routing line of an instruction that explicitly prohibited treating C-19 as a nomination authority.

It was not proof that Daniel had written the instruction.

It was not proof that he had ignored it.

But it meant he had been in the chain before the incorrect classification appeared.

Edward placed the page in a separate sleeve.

“Ask Daniel to come back,” he said.

Sophie looked at the time.

“Tomorrow?”

“No.”

He checked the interview room schedule.

“Today.”

At 17:41, the request was sent.

**Subject: Follow-up interview — PA-44/03 and OC-19**

The response arrived six minutes later.

Daniel Mercer confirmed attendance.

His reply contained only one sentence.

**I can explain the routing of PA-44/03, but I did not originate the instruction.**

Edward read it once.

Then again.

He had not yet asked who originated it.

Daniel had answered anyway.
