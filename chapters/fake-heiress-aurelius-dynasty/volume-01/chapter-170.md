# **Chapter 170 — The Authority That Had No Name**

Edward received the delegation record at 8:17 on Monday morning.

It was only eleven pages.

That was the first thing he disliked about it.

A delegation covering administrative closure, preservation transfer, and unresolved operational matters across three separate offices should have produced a substantial record. Instead, the file contained a two-page authority schedule, a credential register, four amendments, and a collection of references to documents that were not attached.

The cover sheet was stamped:

**FAMILY ALLOCATION REVIEW — DELEGATED CONTINUITY AUTHORITY**

Below it appeared the effective period.

**Day 70 to Day 90.**

Edward placed the file beside his laptop.

He had asked for three things.

The identity of the person who granted the delegation.

The exact scope of the authority.

And the record showing who exercised it.

The file answered the second question.

It avoided the first.

It answered the third only partially.

He began with the original authority schedule.

The document authorised a delegated officer to perform two functions:

1. Close administrative items where operational continuity had been established.
2. Transfer unresolved objections into the preservation register without requiring them to remain on the active operational register.

The second provision was particularly important.

It did not say that objections could be resolved.

It did not say that outstanding requirements could be waived.

It allowed an unresolved objection to be moved.

Edward read the wording twice.

Then he turned to the credential register.

**F-02 — Helen Sloane**

The entry was unmistakable.

Credential holder: Helen Sloane.

Office: Continuity Administration.

Access level: Delegated administrative closure.

Effective period: Day 70–90.

But the next column contained a qualification.

*Credential holder may exercise delegated functions only upon receipt of a valid instruction from the appointing authority.*

Edward leaned back.

There it was.

Helen Sloane possessed the credential.

That did not establish that she had been appointed to exercise it in every case.

And it certainly did not establish that she had personally entered the day-76 closure.

He opened the amendments.

The first changed the administrative reporting format.

The second permitted preservation transfers to be completed without returning the original item to the active register.

The third extended the delegation to include unresolved communication requirements where an alternative continuity arrangement had been recorded.

The fourth was different.

It had no signature.

Only a reference number.

**FAR/DC-70/AM-04.**

Its effective date was day 75.

Edward read the sentence at the bottom.

*The delegated officer may rely upon the operational status recorded by the responsible administrative unit, provided that the underlying objection is retained separately.*

He looked at the day-76 closure.

The active R.V. route had been recorded as operational.

The unresolved principal communication objection had been moved into preservation.

The two entries matched the amended procedure almost perfectly.

But the amendment had been issued one day before the closure.

Someone had prepared the authority that made the closure possible.

And the name of that person was missing.

At ten thirty, Helen Sloane arrived at the Valmont office.

She carried a slim folder and looked mildly irritated.

“You said the credential record was incomplete.”

“It is.”

“Then why am I here?”

“Because your credential appears on the day-76 closure.”

Helen placed her folder on the table.

“I have already explained that I did not approve the closure.”

“I remember.”

“Then I don't see what has changed.”

Edward turned the laptop towards her.

“Your credential was authorised for delegated administrative closure between day 70 and day 90. An amendment expanded that authority on day 75.”

Helen read the screen.

Her expression changed.

“Where did you get this?”

“From the delegation archive.”

“This amendment wasn't included in the copy I received.”

“Did you know it existed?”

“No.”

“Did you receive an instruction to exercise the expanded authority?”

“No.”

“Did you authorise anyone else to use F-02?”

“Absolutely not.”

Edward waited.

Helen read the amendment again.

“Who issued it?”

“That is what I'm trying to establish.”

She tapped the fourth amendment with one finger.

“This is not a minor administrative adjustment.”

“I agree.”

“It changes the conditions under which an unresolved objection can be transferred out of the active register.”

“Yes.”

“And it allows the officer to rely on an operational status entered by another unit.”

“Yes.”

Helen looked up.

“Then it is not merely a delegation. It changes what counts as sufficient evidence for exercising the delegation.”

Edward said nothing.

She continued.

“The original schedule required a valid instruction. This amendment effectively makes the operational register part of the authority chain.”

“Would that have been normal?”

“No. Not without a formal governance decision.”

“Was one made?”

“Not that I saw.”

“Did you attend a meeting about it?”

Helen shook her head.

“I attended a continuity review on day 74. We discussed physical document retention, not closure of personal communication objections.”

“Who attended?”

“Martin Keane. Peter Langford. Sophie Grant. I was there for the preservation arrangements.”

“Was the amendment discussed?”

“Not in my presence.”

Edward wrote down the date.

“Did you sign anything?”

Helen considered the question.

“I signed a technical retention confirmation. It concerned the continued storage of physical custody references.”

“Was it F-02?”

“No. It was my ordinary departmental credential.”

“Do you remember the document?”

“Yes. It had a list of records requiring preservation. I signed the last page.”

“Could that signature have been copied into the delegation file?”

Helen frowned.

“It could have been attached to something else. But that would not make it an approval.”

Edward closed the laptop.

“I need the original document.”

“You will need to ask the archive.”

“I already have.”

“And?”

“They say the original is held under a restricted governance reference.”

Helen gave a humourless laugh.

“Of course it is.”

She stood, then paused.

“Edward, there is something else.”

He looked up.

“On day 74, I asked Martin why the governance review had been postponed again.”

“What did he say?”

“He told me the objection was no longer an operational concern because the preservation route had been established.”

“That sounds familiar.”

“It should. I objected to that wording.”

“Did you put it in writing?”

“I sent an email.”

“Where is it?”

“Probably in the correspondence archive.”

Edward made a note.

Helen picked up her folder.

“One more thing. I never considered myself the authority responsible for deciding whether P-04 had been informed.”

“I understand.”

“No. I want you to understand precisely. I was responsible for retaining records. I was not responsible for deciding whether a protected person had received an explanation, understood it, or accepted representation.”

Edward met her eyes.

“I'll keep those responsibilities separate.”

Helen nodded.

This time, she left without another word.

At noon, Sophie Grant brought Edward a printed copy of the day-74 meeting notes.

“I found this in my personal correspondence archive,” she said. “It wasn't in the official meeting bundle.”

The first page was ordinary.

Attendees.

Time.

Agenda.

The second page contained a short discussion about the unresolved R.V. communication requirement.

**Sophie Grant:** Principal notification remains outstanding.

**Peter Langford:** External preservation route is configured.

**Helen Sloane:** Preservation configuration does not establish personal communication.

**Martin Keane:** Operational continuity can proceed while the communication item remains separately recorded.

Then came a line that had been crossed out.

*Closure authority to be confirmed by governance chair.*

Below it, in a different handwriting, someone had written:

*Alternative delegated authority available.*

Sophie pointed to the sentence.

“I don't remember that being said.”

“Do you recognise the handwriting?”

“No.”

“Could it be Martin's?”

“Possibly. But I wouldn't say that without comparing an original.”

Edward examined the lower margin.

A second handwritten note appeared there.

*Use amended schedule. Do not return to governance queue.*

There was no signature.

“Was this copy made before or after the meeting?” he asked.

“After. I printed it because I wanted to keep a record of the unresolved discussion.”

“Why wasn't it included in the official notes?”

“I don't know.”

“Did you send it to anyone?”

“Only to myself.”

Edward looked at the email header.

The attachment had been created at 5:42 p.m. on day 74.

The file name was:

**ContinuityReview_Objections_NotForDistribution.pdf**

He looked back at Sophie.

“Why that title?”

“Because I was told the notes were not to be circulated beyond the administrative team.”

“Who told you?”

“Martin.”

“Did he say why?”

“He said the review was sensitive and that the governance office would issue a formal record later.”

“Did it?”

“No.”

Edward turned back to the document.

The meeting notes had been preserved.

But the official record had never incorporated the objection.

The amended authority had appeared the following day.

And the closure had been entered on day 76.

It was no longer reasonable to treat those events as unrelated administrative coincidences.

Yet the sequence still did not identify who had made the decision.

That afternoon, Edward requested the original governance-chair appointment register.

The archive initially refused.

The register contained protected appointment information and could not be released under the ordinary records-access procedure.

Edward submitted a narrower request.

He did not ask for names of protected principals.

He did not ask for personal correspondence.

He asked for one specific fact:

**Which office held authority to issue amendments to delegated continuity schedules during days 70–90?**

The answer arrived shortly before five.

It was a single page.

The issuing authority was the Governance Secretariat.

The authorised signatory was the Governance Chair or a formally appointed substitute.

The substitute appointment required:

- A written appointment instrument.
- A defined effective period.
- A specified scope of authority.
- A record of acceptance by the appointed substitute.

Edward read the final paragraph.

*No substitute appointment was recorded in the central governance register for the relevant period.*

He checked the date again.

Day 75.

The fourth amendment had been issued on day 75.

The authority required to issue it had not been delegated to a substitute.

That did not automatically prove the amendment was invalid.

There might have been a separate instrument.

There might have been an emergency authorisation.

There might have been a governance decision recorded in another system.

But the amendment's reference number pointed to the Governance Secretariat.

And the central register contained no appointment.

Edward called Sophie.

“I need to ask you something.”

“Go ahead.”

“Did anyone claim that the Governance Chair had authorised the day-75 amendment?”

A pause.

“Yes.”

“Who?”

“Martin.”

“Did he show you the appointment?”

“No.”

“Did you ask?”

“I did.”

“What did he say?”

“He said the authority had been confirmed through the secretariat.”

Edward looked at the register.

“Did you contact the secretariat?”

“No. I assumed the formal record would follow.”

“Did it?”

“No.”

He thanked her and ended the call.

For several minutes, he remained still.

The problem was becoming clearer.

The amendment had been treated as authorised.

The credential had been treated as available.

The operational status had been treated as sufficient.

And the unresolved objection had been transferred.

Each step relied on the previous one.

But none of those steps, by itself, established that the original authority existed.

In Singapore, Elena was having a different kind of administrative problem.

Her academic programme portal displayed three lines.

Application received.

Completeness review passed.

Eligibility decision pending.

She stared at the screen.

Then she took a screenshot.

Clara, appearing on the video call, watched her expression.

“You look like you're about to argue with a computer.”

“I am.”

“What did it do?”

“It congratulated me for passing a review that isn't the final review.”

“That sounds reasonable.”

“It is. That's why I'm annoyed.”

Clara laughed.

Elena opened the project file she had been working on.

“I've added another rule.”

“Another one?”

“If an application is transferred between review stages, the system has to preserve the previous status. It can't just overwrite it.”

“Why?”

“Because otherwise someone can make a record look as if it was always in its current state.”

Clara leaned closer to her camera.

“Is that something that happens?”

“It can. Imagine an application that was incomplete on Monday. Someone moves it to a different department on Tuesday. On Wednesday, the dashboard shows it as under review. If the original status disappears, nobody can tell whether the applicant ever submitted the missing document.”

“So you want a history.”

“A proper one. Previous status, new status, timestamp, responsible user, and the reason for the change.”

“What if two people use the same account?”

“Then the system should flag that the individual operator cannot be identified.”

Clara smiled.

“You've become very suspicious of administrative systems.”

“I've become very familiar with them.”

Elena saved the file.

Her phone vibrated.

An email from the academic programme office.

**Eligibility review scheduled.**

She opened it.

The message confirmed that her application had moved to the next stage. It did not guarantee admission.

Elena read the message twice.

Then she smiled.

“Good news?” Clara asked.

“Progress.”

“That's not the same thing.”

“No,” Elena said. “But it's better than nothing.”

She closed the email and returned to her platform.

There was still work to do.

At six fifteen, Edward received a call from the Governance Secretariat.

The caller introduced herself as Helen Ward.

Not Helen Sloane.

Edward made sure to note the distinction.

“I understand you have requested information about the day-75 amendment,” she said.

“Yes. I am trying to establish the authority under which it was issued.”

“I have reviewed the central appointment register.”

“And?”

“There was no substitute appointment recorded for the Governance Chair during that period.”

“Was the chair personally available?”

“That is not something I can confirm from the register alone.”

“Was an emergency authorisation issued?”

“I found no emergency authorisation in the central index.”

“Is there another record system?”

A pause.

“Yes.”

Edward sat straighter.

“Which one?”

“The Governance Chair's restricted correspondence archive.”

“Can it be accessed?”

“Not through an ordinary administrative request.”

“What would be required?”

“An independent review authorisation, with a defined document reference.”

Edward wrote it down.

“Does the archive contain the day-75 amendment?”

“I cannot confirm the contents without opening the restricted index.”

“Can you check the index?”

“Not without the authorisation.”

He looked at the file on his desk.

The missing appointment might be explained by a document held outside the central register.

Or it might not.

“Who can authorise the review?” he asked.

“The current Governance Chair.”

“And if the current chair is reviewing an action taken by the previous office?”

“Then the request must be referred to the independent governance panel.”

“Has such a referral been made?”

“Not to my knowledge.”

Edward thanked her.

Before ending the call, Helen added something.

“There is one other issue.”

“What is it?”

“The amendment reference you supplied is incomplete.”

Edward looked down.

“FAR/DC-70/AM-04.”

“That is the operational reference. The originating reference should contain a second identifier.”

“Which identifier?”

“I can't provide it without the restricted index.”

“Can you tell me what kind of identifier?”

“It identifies the person who submitted the amendment for issue.”

Edward's pen stopped.

“Not the person who approved it?”

“No. The submitting officer.”

“Would that be the same person?”

“Not necessarily.”

He wrote the distinction in capital letters.

SUBMITTING OFFICER ≠ APPROVING AUTHORITY.

The call ended.

Edward returned to the first page of the delegation file.

The amendment had no signature.

The central register showed no substitute appointment.

The meeting notes suggested someone had directed the team to use it.

And now there was evidence of a separate originating identifier.

He requested the restricted index through the independent governance panel.

Then he opened his notebook and wrote three questions.

Who submitted the amendment?

Who authorised its issue?

Who decided that it could be used to close the day-76 objection?

Three questions.

Possibly three different people.

He was no longer looking for a single signature.

He was looking for the chain that had allowed an unsigned instruction to become an accepted authority.

At the bottom of the file, he found one final notation.

It had been printed in small grey text beneath the amendment reference.

**Originating submission received: Day 75, 08:12.**

**Submitted through: GSO-RESTRICTED-02.**

**Receiving office: Governance Secretariat.**

**Submitting officer: Not displayed in operational copy.**

Edward stared at the code.

GSO-RESTRICTED-02 was not a person's credential.

It was a restricted submission terminal.

Someone had used it.

Someone had submitted the amendment.

And someone else had allowed the resulting document to be treated as authority.

He closed the file.

For the first time, the missing name seemed less like an accidental omission.

It seemed to be the one detail the system had been designed not to show.
