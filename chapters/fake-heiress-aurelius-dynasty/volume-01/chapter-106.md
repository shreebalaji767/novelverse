# **Chapter 106 — The Credentials That Were Never Returned**

Martin Keane had changed his telephone number twice.

His former employer had closed its local office.

His current address was registered under a property management company rather than his personal name.

None of those details made him impossible to find.

They simply made the process slower.

Jonathan located him through an old professional certification record.

Martin was living in a quiet residential neighbourhood outside the city, working as an independent information-systems consultant. He had no known connection to Vale Private Counsel anymore.

When Edward contacted him, Martin agreed to meet.

He did not ask why.

That was the first thing Edward found unusual.

Martin Keane arrived carrying a slim laptop bag.

He was in his early fifties, with thinning hair and a careful manner. He greeted Edward politely and sat down without removing his jacket.

“I was told this concerns Northbridge.”

“It does.”

“That's a long time ago.”

“Eighteen years.”

Martin gave a small nod.

“I assumed this would come up eventually.”

Edward studied him.

“Why?”

“Because those records were never properly closed.”

Edward placed a copy of the OR-28 access log on the table.

Martin examined it.

“Do you recognise the terminal?”

“Terminal Two. Family Allocation Desk.”

“Did you maintain it?”

“Yes.”

“Were you responsible for user access?”

“During the systems migration, yes.”

“Were you employed by Northbridge?”

“Technically, I was seconded there from Vale Private Counsel.”

“Under Charles Vale?”

“Yes.”

Edward placed another document beside the first.

**Temporary access to restricted family allocation terminals permitted for system maintenance.**

Martin read it.

“I remember this.”

“Did you have access to VPC-ADMIN-04?”

“Yes.”

“Could you use Patricia Moore's supervisor credentials?”

Martin looked up.

“No.”

Edward waited.

Martin added, “Not without her approval.”

“Were you ever in possession of her access card?”

Martin looked at the document.

“I believe so.”

“Do you remember the date?”

“No.”

“Did you retain her credentials?”

Martin's expression changed.

“I don't know what you mean.”

Edward pointed to the handwritten note.

**Original user credentials retained for continuity.**

Martin read it slowly.

“That's a systems note.”

“What does it mean?”

“It means the account credentials were preserved during migration.”

“Whose credentials?”

“Patricia's.”

“Why?”

“Because the family allocation department was being moved to a replacement system. If we deleted the original permissions before the new system was verified, we could have interrupted ongoing work.”

“Did Patricia know?”

“She would have been informed.”

“Was she?”

“I don't remember.”

Edward watched him.

“Could you access her account after the migration?”

Martin hesitated.

“Technically, yes.”

“Could you approve a restricted status change?”

“Not without a supervisor approval code.”

“Did you have one?”

“No.”

“Could you use PM-06?”

Martin looked at the log.

“I could enter the code if I had access to the account.”

“Did you?”

“I don't remember.”

Edward closed the folder.

“Mr Keane, we are investigating an alteration to a record that stated an independent identity review had been completed when it had not.”

Martin's face became still.

“I didn't complete any identity review.”

“I didn't say you did.”

“I was a systems administrator.”

“Then help us understand what you did.”

Martin looked down at his hands.

“I maintained the system.”

“Did you ever alter a record?”

“I corrected technical errors.”

“Did you change the OR-28 status?”

“I don't remember that particular entry.”

“Did you change any status under VPC-ADMIN-04?”

Martin remained silent.

Edward waited.

Finally, Martin said, “I may have processed a request.”

“From whom?”

“Charles Vale's office.”

“What did it concern?”

“An administrative closure.”

“Of OR-28?”

“I can't say.”

Edward placed the R-04 register beside him.

“This is the original acknowledgement. It says the independent review was not completed, no receiving identity custodian was appointed, and no family notification was authorised.”

Martin read it.

“Those were correspondence records. I didn't handle the underlying family decisions.”

“Did you receive a request to change the status?”

“I may have.”

“Was it written?”

“I don't remember.”

“Was there a supporting document?”

Martin looked at the access log.

“There usually was.”

“Did you see one?”

“I can't remember.”

Edward leaned back.

The answer was frustratingly incomplete.

But it was not a denial.

Jonathan took over.

“Mr Keane, your transfer record says you retained original user credentials for continuity. Did that mean you could continue using Patricia Moore's account after the migration?”

Martin rubbed his forehead.

“It meant the permissions remained available to the system administrator during the transition.”

“Could you log in as Patricia?”

“If the system administrator credentials were enabled, yes.”

“Were they enabled?”

“I don't know.”

“Who controlled that?”

“I did.”

The room went quiet.

Edward looked at him.

“You controlled the system administrator credentials?”

“Yes.”

“Then you could have used Patricia's account.”

“I could have accessed the account for maintenance.”

“Could you have approved a restricted entry?”

“Not without the approval token.”

“Where was the approval token?”

“Patricia's card.”

“Which you had temporarily held.”

Martin looked away.

Edward continued.

“Did you keep a copy of the card's access data?”

“I don't remember.”

“Did the migration system retain the card's permissions?”

“Yes.”

“Could those permissions be used without the physical card?”

Martin took a long breath.

“Depending on the system configuration, yes.”

Jonathan looked at Edward.

The missing link was becoming clearer.

Martin had not necessarily needed Patricia's physical card after the migration.

He might have been able to use the credentials retained during the transition.

But that still did not establish whether he had done so.

“Was there a second authentication method?” Edward asked.

Martin nodded.

“A supervisor confirmation.”

“Was that generated automatically?”

“No. It was recorded as an approval event.”

“Could the event be generated by an administrator?”

“Not in the ordinary system.”

“In the migration system?”

Martin did not answer immediately.

Then he said, “There was a temporary override function.”

Edward's expression sharpened.

“What did it do?”

“It allowed us to reproduce an approval event when migrating records from the old system.”

“Could it be used to create a new approval event?”

“It wasn't supposed to be.”

“Could it?”

Martin looked at him.

“Yes.”

Edward asked for the original technical documentation.

Martin said he no longer had it.

But he remembered where it had been stored.

The migration manuals and system configuration records had been transferred to Northbridge's successor archive.

The same archive that had provided the incomplete terminal log.

Edward requested the original migration package.

Two days later, it arrived.

The package contained several technical documents, access tables, and a handwritten exception register.

The exception register was the most important.

It listed every occasion on which the system administrator had used the temporary override function.

There were only eleven entries.

Most concerned routine maintenance.

One concerned a missing employee record.

Another involved a corrupted payment approval.

The final entry was dated day fifty-six.

**Temporary override: Family Allocation Desk.**

**Account: VPC-ADMIN-04.**

**Supervisor reference: PM-06.**

**Purpose: Status continuity.**

**Operator: M.K.**

Edward stared at the initials.

M.K.

Martin Keane.

Jonathan read the entry again.

“So he did it.”

“Possibly.”

“It's his operator code.”

“Yes.”

“What's the distinction?”

“The entry proves he used the temporary override function. It doesn't yet prove what he changed.”

Jonathan pointed to the purpose field.

“Status continuity.”

“That's not specific enough.”

Edward turned the page.

There was a handwritten note in the margin.

**Use existing supervisor approval. Do not generate new identity determination.**

The instruction had been signed with a short reference.

**C.V.**

Charles Vale.

Edward leaned back.

“Now we have a direct instruction.”

Jonathan looked at the note.

“Charles told him to use the existing supervisor approval.”

“And not generate a new identity determination.”

“Could that have been legitimate?”

“Not if the resulting entry represented the independent review as completed.”

Edward examined the date.

Day fifty-six.

The same day the OR-28 status had been changed.

But the handwritten note did not explicitly say to close the review.

It instructed Martin to maintain continuity using an existing approval.

Someone had apparently decided that a suspended review should remain administratively active under Schedule 4.

The question was whether Martin had understood that the resulting record would be misleading.

They returned to Martin the following morning.

This time, Edward brought the original exception register.

Martin read the day-fifty-six entry without speaking.

“Do you recognise this?”

“Yes.”

“Did you make the entry?”

“Yes.”

“Did you use the temporary override?”

“Yes.”

“Under whose instruction?”

“Charles Vale's office.”

“Did Charles instruct you personally?”

“He gave the instruction through the office.”

“Was there a written request?”

Martin nodded.

“An internal systems instruction.”

“Do you still have it?”

“No.”

“Was it attached to the exception register?”

“I don't know.”

Edward placed the handwritten note before him.

“Is this Charles's instruction?”

Martin examined it.

“It appears to be.”

“Did you understand what it meant?”

“It meant the old approval had to remain linked to the administrative record during migration.”

“Why?”

“Because the original record had a supervisor approval event, but the new system couldn't reproduce the old format without an override.”

“Which original approval event?”

“PM-06.”

“Patricia Moore's approval.”

“Yes.”

“Was that approval for the closure of OR-28?”

Martin hesitated.

“I don't know.”

“Did you verify the underlying document?”

“No.”

“Why not?”

“My role was technical.”

Edward's voice remained calm.

“Did you know the independent review had not been completed?”

Martin looked at him.

“I knew the review had been suspended.”

“Did you know the receiving identity custodian had not been appointed?”

“I remember that the receiving authority field was blank.”

“Then what did you think the status change meant?”

Martin looked down.

“I thought the administrative task was being closed.”

“Which task?”

“The OR-28 review task.”

“Was the review completed?”

“No.”

“Then why close it?”

Martin rubbed his face.

“Because Charles said the process would continue under Schedule 4.”

“Did he say the independent review was no longer required?”

“No.”

“Did he say the proposed family allocation was approved?”

“No.”

“Did he tell you to change the record to say that?”

“No.”

Edward waited.

Martin added quietly, “I didn't write the wording.”

“Who did?”

“I don't know.”

“Did you see the wording before processing the override?”

Martin took a long breath.

“Yes.”

“Did you notice it said the review was administratively closed?”

“Yes.”

“Did you notice it also said further independent review was not required?”

Martin looked up.

“That part wasn't in the instruction I received.”

“Then why did you process it?”

“I thought the status was a technical continuation of the previous approval.”

“Did you check with Patricia?”

“No.”

“Did you check with Marianne Cross?”

“No.”

“Did you check with Eleanor Vale?”

“No.”

Edward closed the folder.

“Then you used an existing supervisor approval to process a status change without verifying that the approval applied to the new wording.”

Martin nodded slowly.

“Yes.”

It was the clearest admission Edward had obtained.

But it was not the entire answer.

“Who prepared the wording?” Edward asked.

Martin shook his head.

“I don't know.”

“Who sent the instruction?”

“Charles's office.”

“Who delivered it?”

“I think a clerk.”

“Name?”

“I don't remember.”

“Was Patricia involved?”

“I don't know.”

“Did you speak to her?”

“Not about the wording.”

“Did she know her credentials were being used?”

Martin was silent.

“I assumed she knew the migration process.”

“That's not what I asked.”

“No. I don't know whether she knew.”

Edward studied him.

“Did you ever tell Patricia that her supervisor approval had been used?”

“No.”

“Why not?”

“Because I believed the approval had already been given.”

“By her?”

“I assumed so.”

“Did you see her signature?”

“No.”

“Did you see a signed approval form?”

“No.”

“Then what made you believe she had approved it?”

Martin looked at the exception register.

“The system showed PM-06.”

Edward closed the file.

The investigation had reached an uncomfortable conclusion.

Martin had used a technical override to reproduce an existing supervisor approval.

He had done so under instructions from Charles Vale's office.

He had not verified whether the original approval applied to the new wording.

And the resulting entry had been used to make an unfinished review appear closed.

Whether Martin had understood the wider consequences remained uncertain.

But the administrative record had been changed.

And the change had not been authorised by the person whose approval code it carried.

At the Valmont Estate, Elena was reviewing the programme's final travel instructions.

The university had sent a reminder about arrival procedures, student identification, and accommodation check-in.

She read each instruction carefully.

Then she opened her budgeting spreadsheet.

Her travel expenses were organised into categories.

Transport.

Meals.

Emergency reserve.

Academic materials.

Personal spending.

She had deliberately kept the emergency reserve separate from her ordinary allowance.

Nicholas looked over her shoulder.

“Why are you budgeting so carefully?”

“Because I don't want to run out of money halfway through the programme.”

“You won't.”

“I'd rather know that than assume it.”

Nicholas leaned against the desk.

“You know you can ask us if you need anything.”

“I know.”

“Do you?”

Elena turned toward him.

“Yes.”

“Then why do you look uncomfortable every time someone offers help?”

She considered the question.

“Because I don't want help to become an expectation.”

Nicholas's expression softened.

“It doesn't have to.”

“I know. But I want to learn to manage things myself.”

He nodded.

“Then manage them. Just don't turn independence into a rule that prevents you from accepting something reasonable.”

Elena smiled.

“That's surprisingly sensible.”

“I have occasional moments of wisdom.”

“Occasional?”

“Don't ruin it.”

She laughed.

Nicholas picked up the printed programme schedule.

“Your first day looks busy.”

“It is.”

“Are you sure you don't want someone to accompany you?”

“No. I'll manage.”

He nodded.

“Fine.”

Then he added, “But send a message when you arrive.”

“I will.”

“And when you reach the accommodation.”

“I will.”

“And when you—”

“Nicholas.”

He stopped.

Elena smiled.

“I'll send you one message after I arrive. You can survive the rest.”

He sighed dramatically.

“I suppose I can.”

Isabelle was working in Catherine's foundation office when Catherine reviewed her proposed research placement.

The document contained a clear list of responsibilities, expected working hours, reporting arrangements, and evaluation criteria.

Catherine read it carefully.

“This is well structured.”

“Thank you.”

“You've included an independent supervisor.”

“Yes. I thought it would be better.”

Catherine nodded.

“It is.”

Isabelle looked at her mother.

“Will you be disappointed if I don't get selected?”

Catherine put the document down.

“No.”

“Even though it's your foundation?”

“Especially because it's my foundation.”

Isabelle considered that.

“I want to do something useful here. I don't want people to think I received a position simply because I'm your daughter.”

“Then do the work. Let the selection process decide.”

Isabelle smiled faintly.

“That's what I intend to do.”

Catherine returned the document.

“Submit it.”

Isabelle gathered the papers.

For the first time since returning to the Valmont household, she felt that she was beginning to establish a place that did not depend entirely on being recognised as someone's daughter.

Edward spent the afternoon reviewing Martin's statement.

There were three established facts.

First, Martin had used the temporary override function on day fifty-six.

Second, the override had reproduced Patricia Moore's existing supervisor approval code.

Third, Charles Vale's office had instructed him to use that existing approval.

But one important question remained.

Who had written the wording that described OR-28 as administratively closed and stated that further independent review was not required?

The technical instruction had not contained those words.

The override had not created them.

They had already existed in the system when Martin processed the change.

Jonathan brought in the original document request.

“We found the document that was attached to the migration instruction.”

Edward looked up.

“Where?”

“In the Northbridge continuity archive. It was filed under technical migration exceptions rather than family allocation correspondence.”

“Who prepared it?”

Jonathan placed the document on the table.

The title read:

**OR-28 — STATUS CONTINUITY REQUEST**

The first section requested that the existing administrative task remain active during migration.

The second section requested that the status be changed to:

**Administrative review closed. Existing Schedule 4 procedure to continue.**

The final section stated:

**No further independent review required for administrative continuity.**

Edward read it twice.

The document was dated day fifty-six.

It carried no handwritten signature.

Only an authorisation reference.

**REQUESTING OFFICE: FAMILY ALLOCATION**

**PREPARED BY: P. MOORE**

Edward looked at Jonathan.

“Patricia prepared the wording.”

“Yes.”

“Did she sign it?”

“No. It was submitted electronically.”

“Was the PM-06 approval attached?”

“Through the existing approval record.”

Edward stared at the document.

Patricia had said she did not remember approving the exact wording.

Martin had said he did not write it.

Now the original request showed that Patricia's office had prepared it, and Martin had processed it using her existing approval.

The distinction was becoming narrower.

But the final question remained.

Had Patricia deliberately requested the closure of the independent review?

Or had she prepared a routine continuity document believing it only meant the administrative task would remain open under Schedule 4?

Edward turned to the final page.

There was a handwritten note in the margin.

**Do not issue as identity determination. Temporary administration only.**

The initials beneath it were not Patricia's.

They were Charles Vale's.

Edward read the note again.

Charles had approved the document.

He had also added a restriction that the status must not be treated as an identity determination.

Yet the final wording said no further independent review was required.

The contradiction was unmistakable.

Someone had authorised a document that carried two incompatible instructions.

One part closed the independent review.

The other warned that the document must not be treated as an identity determination.

Edward placed the document down.

“Get Patricia back.”

Jonathan nodded.

“What do you want to ask her?”

Edward looked at the handwritten note.

“I want to know whether she prepared the wording herself, and whether Charles changed it after she submitted it.”

He paused.

“Because if he did, then the system record may have preserved the original restriction while the final status was changed around it.”

Jonathan looked at the document.

“And if she prepared it?”

“Then we need to know why.”

Edward closed the file.

For the first time, the investigation had found a document that contained both the warning and the contradiction in the same place.

The next statement would determine whether Patricia Moore had helped create that contradiction—or had been another person whose work was used to conceal it.
