# **Chapter 32 — The Guardian Who Was Never Told**

Edward read the examiner's report for the fourth time.

The reconstructed sentence remained incomplete.

*Do not tell the receiving guardian which child has been assigned until—*

The final words had been lost beneath the damaged paper.

He placed the report beside the ninth-day register.

Two infants.

Two receiving guardians.

Two separate departures.

And an instruction suggesting that neither guardian was supposed to know the identity of the child they were receiving.

It was no longer simply a question of who had taken the children.

It was a question of what those people had been told.

Edward called Jonathan Pierce.

"I need you to look at something."

"Another document?"

"Yes."

"That question sounded unnecessarily hopeful."

Edward almost smiled.

"The document examiner found a handwritten instruction beneath the erased section of the receiving authority."

He read the reconstructed sentence aloud.

Pierce was silent for a moment.

"That changes the legal interpretation."

"How?"

"If the receiving guardians were deliberately denied information about the infants' identities, we need to establish whether they were acting as ordinary guardians, temporary custodians, or representatives in an arrangement they didn't fully understand."

"Could they have believed they were receiving unrelated children?"

"Possibly. But don't confuse a possibility with evidence."

"I know."

"And there's another problem."

"What?"

"Whoever wrote that instruction was making a distinction between the child and the receiving guardian. They may have wanted the children separated from the families who were originally supposed to receive them."

Edward looked at the photograph of the register.

"Or they wanted to prevent the receiving guardians from making their own decisions."

"Exactly."

Pierce paused.

"Edward, we need to speak to Morelli again."

"He won't give us the names."

"Then don't ask him for the names."

Edward frowned.

"What should I ask?"

"Ask him what he told the guardians."

The following morning, the Valmont family gathered for breakfast.

Elena arrived a little later than usual, carrying her laptop and a notebook.

Nicholas looked at the notebook.

"More university paperwork?"

"Unfortunately."

"Didn't you submit the application yesterday?"

"Yes. Now I have to prepare for the interview."

Sebastian looked up.

"Have they scheduled it already?"

"Not yet. I just want to prepare in advance."

Richard lowered his newspaper.

"What sort of questions do they ask?"

"Why I selected the programme, what I expect to gain from it, how it connects to my studies, and what I plan to do afterward."

"Reasonable."

Elena took a seat.

"I also need to explain why I want to study abroad instead of completing the entire programme here."

"That's straightforward," Nicholas said.

"Is it?"

"Of course. You want to see another country, study somewhere different, and experience something beyond this house."

Elena glanced at him.

"That sounds rather simple."

"Sometimes the correct answer is simple."

Catherine smiled faintly.

"He's right. You don't need to turn every personal decision into a formal presentation."

Elena opened her notebook.

"I know. I just don't want to sound as though I chose the programme because it looks impressive."

"Then tell them why you chose it."

She considered that.

"I like the course structure. It has modules that aren't available in my current programme, and I want to understand how different universities approach the same subjects."

Sebastian nodded.

"That's a good academic reason."

"And I want to learn how to manage things independently."

Richard looked at her.

"You already manage quite a lot independently."

"Not everything."

"No one does."

The conversation paused as a member of the household staff brought in fresh tea.

Elena looked around the table.

For a moment, she felt an odd awareness of how much had changed.

Isabelle now occupied a place in the family routine.

The DNA investigation continued.

The hospital records remained unresolved.

Yet breakfast still happened.

People still discussed university applications, meetings, and ordinary inconveniences.

Perhaps that was what she needed most.

A life that did not stop simply because its foundations were being examined.

Isabelle entered the dining room carrying a folder.

"Good morning."

"Morning," Elena replied.

Isabelle took the seat opposite her.

"I received an email from the foundation."

"About the scholarship proposal?"

"Yes. They want me to revise the budget."

Nicholas looked interested.

"Already?"

"They're considering a pilot programme for the next academic year."

Catherine glanced at her daughter.

"Only if the revised proposal is financially practical."

"I know."

Isabelle opened the folder and showed Elena a page.

"I've divided the programme into two stages. The first would involve a smaller group of schools. If it works, the foundation could expand it."

Elena examined the page.

"That seems more manageable."

"That's what I thought."

"How many schools?"

"Eight initially."

"Eight is still quite a lot if you're trying to monitor everything yourself."

"I'm not planning to do it alone."

Elena looked up.

Isabelle smiled slightly.

"Mother has already told me that I need a proper committee."

"Good," Nicholas said. "Otherwise, you'll spend your entire year arguing with spreadsheets."

Isabelle gave him a look.

"Spreadsheets are perfectly reasonable."

"That's what they all say before the spreadsheets take over their lives."

Elena laughed.

The conversation moved on.

It was not a dramatic reconciliation.

Neither girl suddenly forgot the circumstances that had brought them together.

But they were learning how to occupy the same space without treating every ordinary interaction as a test.

And for now, that was progress.

At the Aurelius residence, Adrian had arranged a meeting with the family's legal team.

The discussion concerned Morelli's second statement.

Alexander sat at the head of the table.

Victoria was beside him.

Damien and Lucien occupied the opposite side, while Adrian reviewed the examiner's report.

"There's a possibility that the receiving guardians were not informed of the infants' identities," Adrian said.

Alexander looked at the document.

"How reliable is the reconstruction?"

"Preliminary. The paper has been damaged, and the examiner cannot recover the complete sentence."

"Could the original still exist?"

"Possibly."

"Where?"

"Morelli may know."

Lucien leaned back.

"So we're back to asking him questions."

"Different questions," Edward said from the other end of the conference call.

Adrian turned toward the screen.

"What did you find?"

"Morelli confirmed that he knew the receiving guardians' identities. He also said he knew the destinations given in the instructions."

"Did he say whether the guardians knew what they were receiving?"

"No."

Edward paused.

"But I suspect that's what we should ask next."

Victoria looked at the report.

"If they were not told, then they may have raised a child without knowing that another family was searching for her."

Alexander's expression tightened.

"Or they may have known everything."

"Yes," Victoria said. "We must consider both possibilities."

Damien spoke quietly.

"There's also the question of the woman who received the second infant."

Everyone looked toward him.

"Morelli confirmed that Charles Vale received one child on behalf of someone else. The other was received by a woman. If the woman was not informed of the child's identity, she may not have known why she was involved."

"Or she may have been the person who arranged it," Lucien said.

"Both remain possible."

Julian had been sitting quietly near the end of the table.

He looked at the report.

"Do we know if the children were safe?"

Victoria turned toward him.

"Morelli said both infants were healthy when they left the hospital."

"That's not what I meant."

His voice was quieter now.

"Afterward. Did they go somewhere safe?"

Alexander looked at his youngest son.

"We don't know."

Julian lowered his eyes.

He had imagined meeting his sister many times.

He had imagined introducing her to the family library, showing her the garden, arguing over board games, and discovering whether she liked the same food as him.

He had not spent much time imagining the eighteen years before that meeting.

The years during which she might have been ill, lonely, frightened, or perfectly happy.

He did not know.

And that uncertainty was beginning to trouble him more than he wanted to admit.

Victoria reached across the table and touched his hand.

"We will find out."

Julian nodded.

Two days later, Edward received a message from Marcus Morelli.

It contained no greeting.

Only a time and an address.

**Tomorrow. 10:00 a.m.**

**Bring the examiner's reconstruction.**

Edward showed the message to Pierce.

"He's agreed to another meeting."

"Good."

"He wants the examiner's report."

"Then he knows what we've discovered."

"Or he wants to see whether we have discovered the same thing he knows."

Pierce gathered his papers.

"Either way, don't confront him with assumptions."

"I won't."

"Ask him what the instruction meant."

Edward looked at the message.

"And ask him what he told the guardians."

"Yes."

The second meeting took place in Morelli's home.

It was a modest house on a quiet street overlooking the northern coastline.

There were no signs of wealth.

The furniture was old but well maintained. Books occupied most of the shelves. A small desk stood near the window, with neatly arranged files and several framed photographs.

Edward noticed one photograph in particular.

It showed a much younger Marcus Morelli standing beside two other men outside a hospital building.

One was Charles Vale.

The other was unfamiliar.

Edward did not comment.

Morelli brought tea into the sitting room.

"You came alone."

"As requested."

"Good."

Edward placed the examiner's reconstruction on the table.

Morelli looked at it.

His expression did not change.

"Where did you find this?"

"The ninth-day receiving authority."

Morelli read the incomplete sentence.

His fingers rested against the edge of the paper.

"That's unfortunate."

"Why?"

"Because it is incomplete."

"You recognise it?"

"Yes."

"Who wrote it?"

Morelli placed the page down.

"Eleanor Vale."

Edward leaned forward.

"She wrote the instruction?"

"Yes."

"Do not tell the receiving guardian which child has been assigned until—"

"Until the mother had been identified."

Edward stared at him.

"What mother?"

"The mother who was supposed to receive the children."

"One of the two women?"

Morelli nodded.

"Which one?"

"I don't know."

"You're saying Eleanor wanted the receiving guardians to remain unaware until the mother was identified?"

"Yes."

"Why?"

Morelli looked toward the window.

"Because the original arrangement had failed, and she believed that revealing the identities too early could put both children at risk."

"From whom?"

Morelli did not answer.

Edward waited.

Finally, Morelli continued.

"There were two separate problems. The first concerned the children's identities. The second concerned the people who wanted control over the arrangements."

"Who wanted control?"

"That is what we were trying to establish."

"Who was we?"

"Charles. Eleanor. Me."

"And Daniel Mercer?"

Morelli looked at him.

"Daniel was involved in the legal documentation. He did not make the decisions."

"Was he present on the ninth day?"

"Yes."

"At the hospital?"

"At the repository and at the legal office. He was not present for both releases."

Edward made a note.

"What about Martha Voss?"

"She was present at the hospital."

"Did she know the identities of the receiving guardians?"

"She knew the names written in the register. She did not know the identities of the people they represented."

"Did you?"

"Yes."

"Then tell me."

Morelli shook his head.

"Not yet."

Edward exhaled slowly.

"You keep saying that."

"Because you keep asking me to reveal information that may still be dangerous."

"These are eighteen-year-old events."

"That does not make the people involved harmless."

Edward studied him.

"Are you protecting someone?"

Morelli's expression became difficult to read.

"I am trying to avoid making the same mistake twice."

"What mistake?"

"Choosing what other people should know."

He reached for his tea.

"I did that once. It was enough."

Edward leaned back.

"Then tell me what happened after the release."

Morelli looked at him for several seconds.

"When the infants left the hospital, I believed the arrangement was temporary. The receiving guardians were supposed to hold them until the maternal identities had been verified and the original authority could be restored."

"How long?"

"Initially, a few days."

"How long did it actually take?"

Morelli looked down.

"Eighteen years."

Edward did not immediately respond.

He looked at Morelli.

"You're saying both children were placed temporarily?"

"Yes."

"With two different guardians?"

"Yes."

"Who were instructed not to identify the children?"

"Correct."

"Did they know they were participating in a temporary arrangement?"

"One did."

Edward's pen stopped.

"Which one?"

Morelli's expression tightened.

"I cannot tell you that."

"Why?"

"Because I do not know whether that person understood the full arrangement."

"Then what did the other person believe?"

"That the child had been placed with them permanently."

Edward stared at him.

"One guardian believed it was temporary. The other believed it was permanent?"

"That is what I was told."

"By whom?"

Morelli looked at the photograph on the shelf.

"Charles Vale."

Edward's mind moved quickly.

Charles had received Infant B.

But he had been acting on behalf of someone else.

If Charles had delivered the instruction, then he might have known the identity of the person who was meant to receive Infant B.

But the other infant had been received by an unknown woman.

And Morelli had just suggested that the two guardians had been given different explanations.

"Did the guardians know about each other?"

"No."

"Did they know there were two infants involved?"

Morelli hesitated.

"One did."

"Which one?"

"I cannot say."

Edward closed his notebook.

"Then we have another problem."

Morelli looked at him.

"Yes."

"Someone knew there were two children. Someone else may have believed they were receiving a single child. And the people who arranged the release deliberately kept the two placements separate."

"That is correct."

"Why?"

Morelli's voice was quiet.

"Because the person who selected the receiving guardians believed the children would be safer if they never met."

Edward stared at him.

"Safer from whom?"

Morelli did not answer.

Instead, he opened a drawer in the small desk and removed a photograph.

He placed it on the table.

It showed two newborn infants in separate hospital bassinets.

There were no visible names.

Only two small identification cards.

One read:

**A — 2:17**

The other:

**B — 5:46**

Edward recognised the times immediately.

The original birth records.

He picked up the photograph carefully.

"Where did you get this?"

"From the private nursery."

"Who took it?"

"I don't know."

"Why did you keep it?"

"Because it was the only photograph I had that showed both children before the release."

Edward examined it.

The infants were positioned several feet apart.

One had a small blanket folded beneath her head.

The other had a white knitted cap.

Neither photograph nor identification card established biological identity.

But the image proved something important.

The children had been together.

They had occupied the same nursery.

And someone had taken care to document them separately.

Edward looked up.

"Why didn't you give us this earlier?"

Morelli's answer was immediate.

"Because I wasn't ready to tell you what happened after this photograph was taken."

That evening, Edward returned to the Valmont mansion.

He found Sebastian in the study.

Richard was reviewing a financial report, while Catherine sat near the fireplace.

Edward placed the photograph on the table.

Sebastian leaned forward.

"What is that?"

"Two infants in the private nursery."

Catherine stood.

She approached slowly.

For several seconds, she said nothing.

Then she looked at the identification cards.

"A. 2:17."

"B. 5:46."

Edward nodded.

"Those are the original birth times."

Catherine stared at the photograph.

"Can we tell which one was mine?"

"No."

She looked at him.

"Not even from the hospital records?"

"No. The records establish the birth entries, not the identity of the children after the transfer."

Catherine lowered herself into a chair.

Richard came around the desk.

"Where did this come from?"

"Morelli."

"What did he tell you?"

Edward explained the second meeting.

The temporary placements.

The different explanations given to the receiving guardians.

The instruction to keep the children separate.

And the possibility that one guardian had believed the arrangement temporary while the other believed it permanent.

Sebastian listened without interrupting.

When Edward finished, he asked the obvious question.

"Which guardian was told what?"

"Morelli refused to identify them."

Richard's expression hardened.

"He's withholding information."

"Yes."

"Why are we allowing him to dictate the investigation?"

"Because he is the first person who has admitted knowing the receiving guardians' identities."

Richard looked at the photograph again.

"Then he knows who raised Elena."

"Possibly."

Catherine's eyes remained on the two infants.

"Does he know Isabelle?"

"No."

"Has he met either girl?"

"Not that he has admitted."

She looked at Edward.

"Did he say whether the receiving guardians were told that the children might not be biologically related to them?"

Edward paused.

"No."

"Then we need to find out."

Sebastian nodded.

"That may be more important than the names."

Richard turned toward him.

"Explain."

"If the guardians believed the children were permanently assigned to them, they may have made decisions based on incomplete information. If they believed the arrangement was temporary, they may have expected someone to return."

"And if one of them knew the arrangement was temporary while the other didn't?"

"Then one person may have knowingly kept a child from her biological family."

The room became quiet.

Catherine looked at the photograph again.

"Or one person may have believed they were protecting a child."

Nobody answered.

Because both possibilities remained open.

Later that night, Elena sat in the library.

She had completed her interview preparation and was now reading a book she had borrowed from the university library.

The door opened.

Isabelle entered carrying two cups of tea.

"You're still awake?"

Elena looked up.

"Just finishing a chapter."

Isabelle placed one cup beside her.

"Mother said you were studying."

"I was. I'm taking a break."

Isabelle sat opposite her.

For a while, they read in silence.

Then Isabelle looked at the book.

"What is it about?"

"A woman trying to restore an old family business."

"Is it interesting?"

"Quite. The business is failing, but the family keeps arguing about who should control it."

Isabelle smiled.

"That sounds familiar."

Elena laughed softly.

"Unfortunately."

Isabelle looked toward the shelves.

"Have you ever thought about what you'll do if the DNA results confirm everything?"

Elena closed the book.

"Everything?"

"That I'm their biological daughter and you're not."

Elena considered the question.

"I suppose I'll have to decide what I want."

"What if they expect you to leave?"

"I don't know."

"What if they don't?"

Elena looked at her.

"Then I'll decide what kind of relationship I want with them."

Isabelle was quiet.

"You make it sound simple."

"It isn't."

Elena took a sip of tea.

"But I can't decide my entire future based on something that hasn't happened yet."

Isabelle nodded slowly.

"That's fair."

After a moment, Elena asked, "What about you?"

"What about me?"

"What do you want to do if the results confirm you're their daughter?"

Isabelle looked at her cup.

"I want to study business. I want to work at the foundation, but not just because it's Mother's foundation. And I want to build something of my own eventually."

"That sounds good."

"I don't want to spend my life being introduced as someone's daughter."

Elena smiled.

"Then don't."

Isabelle looked at her.

"You're very good at making things sound obvious."

"Only after someone else says them first."

They both laughed.

Outside, the garden lights illuminated the pathways.

Neither girl knew that a photograph of two newborns had just been added to the investigation.

Neither knew that the man who had arranged their separation had finally admitted that their placements were supposed to be temporary.

And neither knew that one of the people who had received them might have believed the arrangement would last only a few days.

Edward was preparing to leave the study when his phone rang.

The caller was Jonathan Pierce.

"I've reviewed the legal implications of Morelli's statement," Pierce said.

"And?"

"There may be a way to establish the receiving guardians' identities without opening Box 114."

Edward stopped.

"How?"

"Through the original receiving authority. If the document was executed by Charles Vale, there should have been a corresponding acknowledgment from the people who were supposed to receive the children."

"Wouldn't those be in the same archive?"

"Not necessarily. The receiving guardians may have signed separate custody acknowledgments."

Edward thought about the different instructions.

One guardian believing the arrangement was temporary.

The other believing it was permanent.

"Where would those acknowledgments be kept?"

"With the receiving parties, if copies were issued."

"Then we need to locate the people who signed them."

"Yes."

Edward looked at the photograph lying on the desk.

Two infants.

Two separate destinations.

Two people who might have received them without knowing the full truth.

"There's another possibility," Pierce continued.

"What?"

"If the guardians signed the acknowledgments, they may have been given different versions of the same agreement."

Edward looked up.

"Different agreements?"

"Different responsibilities. Different explanations. Perhaps even different names for the children."

The room seemed suddenly colder.

Edward returned to the table and examined the photograph.

The identification cards had only letters and times.

A.

B.

Not names.

Not family names.

Not even the word *Aurelius* or *Valmont*.

He finally understood something that had been hidden in the records from the beginning.

The children might not have been assigned false identities after the release.

They might have been given no identities at all.

"Pierce," he said, "find every custody acknowledgment connected to the ninth-day release."

"I'll begin tomorrow."

"No. Begin tonight."

A pause.

"Edward, what are you thinking?"

Edward looked at the two identification cards.

"That the people who received those children may have been given two completely different stories."

He lowered his voice.

"And one of them may have spent eighteen years believing that story was true."
