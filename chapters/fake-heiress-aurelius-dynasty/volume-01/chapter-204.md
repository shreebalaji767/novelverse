# **Chapter 204 — The Request Behind the Revision**

The complete task history arrived at 8:32.

Edward opened it with Sophie standing beside his desk.

The file contained eleven pages.

Most were ordinary administrative entries: timestamps, queue assignments, access events and automated acknowledgements.

The important material occupied less than one page.

**Task:** RA-36/11 Contact Verification\
**Created:** Day 36, 09:42\
**Original instruction:** Confirm candidate contact details through an authorised channel.\
**Returned for revision:** Day 36, 09:57\
**Review account:** PA-REVIEW-06\
**Revision submitted:** Day 36, 10:06\
**Revised instruction:** Populate candidate contact field from existing protected correspondence reference.\
**Reason:** Maintain continuity of contact pending authority confirmation.

Edward read the final line.

“Pending authority confirmation.”

Sophie pulled a chair closer.

“They knew the authority hadn't been confirmed.”

“They knew confirmation was pending. That doesn't tell us what they believed about the address.”

“Could they have thought the protected reference was enough?”

“Possibly. But the original instruction asked for verification. The revision replaced verification with transcription.”

He turned to the next page.

There was a review comment.

**Do not initiate direct candidate contact until receiving-route authority is clarified. Existing protected reference may be used for administrative continuity only.**

Edward read it aloud.

Sophie frowned.

“That's contradictory.”

“Not necessarily. It may be saying they couldn't contact the candidate directly, but could use the address for an administrative notification.”

“Except nobody established that the address belonged to the candidate.”

“Exactly.”

The review comment had not authorised the address for candidate notification.

It had merely permitted its use for administrative continuity.

Someone had interpreted that permission more broadly.

At 9:15, Edward requested the individual access events behind PA-REVIEW-06.

The system initially returned six entries.

Three were already familiar.

One concerned the day-46 source classification.

Another concerned the day-47 revision of the alternative-candidate note.

The remaining two related to RA-36/11.

Both had occurred on day 36.

The first access was at 09:57.

The second was at 10:06.

The individual operators were concealed behind the shared account, but the system retained the workstation identifiers.

**PA-WS-04.**

**PA-WS-11.**

Edward checked the office allocation register.

PA-WS-04 was located in Programme Administration's general review room.

PA-WS-11 was assigned to the restricted correspondence suite.

That was a problem.

The task had been modified through a shared account, but the two modifications had originated from different workstations.

The account could not tell them who had acted.

The workstation allocation could not tell them who had been sitting there.

However, the access pattern offered a direction.

“Who was assigned to the restricted correspondence suite that morning?” Sophie asked.

Edward opened the staff schedule.

Three employees had authorised access.

A records officer.

A correspondence supervisor.

And a temporary governance liaison.

The third name was familiar.

**Marianne Cross.**

Edward did not immediately request an interview.

Marianne Cross had appeared in several earlier correspondence records. She was an administrative liaison, not the same person as Marianne Vale, the Restricted Appointments Coordinator.

Her role had been limited.

She had forwarded correspondence, received acknowledgements and maintained a transmission register.

Nothing in the available evidence established that she had authority to nominate a candidate, verify a protected identity or approve a receiving appointment.

Edward reviewed her earlier statements.

She had consistently maintained that C-19 was a forwarding location.

She had not claimed that it was a candidate's residence.

She had not claimed that it was an appointment authority.

But she had worked in the restricted correspondence suite.

And the revised task had originated from a workstation assigned to that suite.

Edward drafted a request for a clarification interview.

He included the two workstation timestamps and the task reference.

He did not include the protected address.

At 11:20, Marianne replied.

She agreed to attend.

She also attached a short statement.

**I recall a day-36 task concerning a candidate contact field. I did not author the revised instruction. I may have reviewed the wording before it was returned to Programme Administration.**

Edward read the second sentence again.

May have reviewed it.

That was different from denying involvement.

Sophie noticed his expression.

“She remembers it.”

“She remembers a review. We need to know what kind.”

“Could she have been the person at PA-WS-11?”

“Possibly. But workstation access alone won't establish that.”

He added the statement to the interview file.

At 13:00, Elena's pilot team held its second review meeting.

The programme manager had brought a printed list of proposed changes.

“Your transfer warning is useful,” he said. “But we need to distinguish between a permitted transfer and an unverified transfer.”

Elena nodded.

“Agreed.”

“We don't want the system to block every use of an existing record.”

“Neither do I.”

She opened the prototype.

“I've added three separate checks.”

She displayed the first.

**Source validity.**

“Was the original record validly created?”

The second appeared.

**Transfer permission.**

“Was this information permitted to be reused for the new purpose?”

Then the third.

**Destination verification.**

“Has the information been verified as appropriate for the person or application receiving it?”

The programme manager read the labels.

“So a document could pass the first check and fail the other two.”

“Yes.”

“And the system would preserve that distinction?”

“Yes. It wouldn't call the source record invalid just because a later transfer was inappropriate.”

The manager nodded slowly.

“That is exactly what we need.”

Elena looked at the test applications.

One had valid documents but no verified source.

Another had verified information but an expired permission.

The third had complete evidence but no assigned decision authority.

None could be treated as fully approved.

“Could we use the same approach for our external reviewers?” he asked.

“Yes. Their assignment, independence status and authority to make a decision should be separate fields.”

“Good. Add it to the next build.”

Elena entered the requirement.

The work was becoming more demanding.

It was also becoming more real.

At 15:40, Marianne Cross arrived.

She carried a thin folder and a printed copy of her earlier statement.

Edward greeted her.

“Thank you for coming.”

“I thought it would be better to clarify the day-36 task before anyone drew conclusions from the access log.”

“That's appreciated.”

He displayed the two task versions.

“Do you remember reviewing this?”

Marianne leaned forward.

“Yes.”

“Did you make the change?”

“No.”

“Did you request it?”

She studied the wording.

“I requested clarification.”

“From whom?”

“Programme Administration.”

“Why?”

“Because the original task asked for confirmation of the candidate's contact details, but the candidate's identity was restricted.”

Edward waited.

Marianne continued.

“I was concerned that an administrative officer might attempt direct contact without knowing which communication channel was authorised.”

“So you asked them not to contact the candidate?”

“I asked them not to use an unverified channel.”

“Did you suggest using CR-29/08?”

“No.”

“Did you authorise the transfer?”

“No.”

Edward pointed to the revised instruction.

“Then how did the address field become populated?”

Marianne examined the task history.

“I didn't see this final version at the time.”

“But you reviewed the wording.”

“I reviewed the restriction concerning direct contact. I did not review the completed address entry.”

“Did you work at PA-WS-11 on day 36?”

“Yes.”

“Was the shared account open?”

“Probably. It was used by several people.”

“Did you access RA-36/11?”

“I don't remember opening the candidate record.”

“Can you explain the workstation event?”

Marianne considered the question.

“I may have opened the task queue to review the correspondence restriction. That would not mean I changed the task.”

Edward made a note.

“Did you know that CR-29/08 was being used as a candidate address?”

“No.”

“Did you know a notification was later sent there?”

“No.”

Marianne folded her hands.

“I want to be precise about something.”

“Go ahead.”

“My role was to preserve the separation between correspondence and appointment administration. I could tell Programme Administration that a communication route was restricted. I could not make that route suitable for a different purpose.”

Edward looked at her.

“Did anyone ask you to do that?”

“No.”

“Did anyone ask you to identify the candidate?”

“No.”

“Did anyone ask you to appoint a receiving representative?”

“No.”

“Then what did you believe the revised task was doing?”

Marianne looked again at the words.

“From what I can see, it was intended to prevent direct contact while the authority question remained unresolved.”

“But it also populated an address.”

“Yes.”

“Without verification.”

“Yes.”

She paused.

“That should not have happened.”

Edward did not respond immediately.

Marianne continued.

“I remember the original concern. We were told that the candidate's identity was restricted and that ordinary contact could compromise the process. I understood that to mean we should wait for an authorised channel.”

“Did you believe the protected address was an authorised channel?”

“No. I believed it was a protected correspondence reference. Those are not the same thing.”

Edward closed the folder.

“Thank you.”

Marianne stood, then stopped.

“There is one more thing I should tell you.”

Edward looked up.

“What is it?”

“The day-36 task was not the first time someone had asked Programme Administration to use an existing protected reference to solve a missing contact field.”

Sophie leaned forward.

“When was the earlier request?”

Marianne opened her folder.

“I don't remember the exact date. It was before RA-36/11.”

“Was it related to the same candidate?”

“I don't know.”

“Do you remember the reference?”

She looked at a handwritten note.

“CR-29/08 was mentioned. But I don't remember whether it was the source or merely the related record.”

Edward wrote down the reference.

“Can you locate the earlier request?”

“I can try.”

“Please don't access anything outside your current authority.”

“I won't.”

Marianne left the room.

At 17:05, Edward received a message from her.

She had found an index entry.

**Request:** PA-29/14\
**Date:** Day 29\
**Subject:** Continuity of protected correspondence during receiving-route transition\
**Origin:** Programme Administration\
**Recipient:** Restricted Correspondence Liaison\
**Status:** Referred for authority clarification\
**Outcome:** No substantive approval recorded.

Edward opened the entry.

The request had been created on the same day as CR-29/08.

It asked whether an existing protected correspondence location could be used to maintain communication with a person whose receiving-route involvement might end.

The wording was careful.

It did not name the person.

It did not identify a candidate.

It did not request a new appointment.

It asked whether communication could continue if the person's receiving-route role ended.

The request had been referred to the office responsible for PI-07.

No approval was recorded.

But the next page contained a handwritten annotation.

**Continuity may be preserved. Purpose must not be expanded.**

Edward read it twice.

The annotation had no individual signature.

Only an office reference.

**RPCA/29/08.**

Sophie looked at the screen.

“So the correspondence arrangement existed before the candidate proposal.”

“Yes.”

“And someone later used its address for the candidate.”

“Yes.”

“Was that the purpose of the original request?”

“No. The original request concerned continuity for a person whose receiving-route involvement might end.”

Edward opened the chronology.

Day 29: Protected correspondence arrangement established.

Day 29: PA-29/14 asks whether its location can support continuity during a receiving-route transition.

Day 36: Address copied into a candidate's contact field.

Day 41: Notification sent to the address.

The original request and the later use were related.

But they were not the same action.

He opened the full task history again.

The request for address confirmation had been changed.

The candidate had not been contacted.

The protected correspondence location had been used without evidence of permission for that purpose.

And the person who refused the envelope had correctly declined to turn an unverified address into proof of delivery.

Edward saved the new reference.

Then another message appeared.

It came from Dr Eleanor Vale.

**The annotation on PA-29/14 is incomplete without its originating authority record.**

**Please request PAO/29/02 before drawing conclusions about the scope of the communication arrangement.**

Edward stared at the reference.

The request had been made on day 29.

The protected correspondence arrangement had been established on day 29.

But the annotation had come from an office that did not itself possess authority to expand the arrangement.

He entered the next request.

**PAO/29/02 — Original Authority for Protected Correspondence Continuity.**

The screen returned a notice.

Restricted original — identity sealed

**Custodian review required.**

Edward leaned back.

They were finally approaching the authority that had created the arrangement.

Not the administrator who copied the address.

Not the liaison who reviewed the task.

Not the person who refused the envelope.

The authority that had decided, before the candidate's proposal existed, that one person's correspondence should survive the end of a receiving-route role.

And that decision might explain why the records had been kept separate in the first place.
