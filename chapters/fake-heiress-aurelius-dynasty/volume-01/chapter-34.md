# Chapter 34 — The Woman Who Was Not Supposed to Know

Edward did not sleep.

At 11:47 p.m., he had received a photograph of an eighteen-year-old document from an unknown number.

At 11:49, he had attempted to call the sender.

At 11:50, the number had become unreachable.

By midnight, he had enlarged the photograph enough to examine every visible line.

**RECEIVING GUARDIAN — INFANT A**

**Name:** Margaret...

**Relationship to child:** NONE.

**Instruction:** The receiving party has requested that the child's identity remain undisclosed until the mother makes contact.

And at the bottom:

**Witness: Marcus Morelli.**

Beside his signature, someone had written:

*She must not be told.*

Edward had spent the next hour comparing the handwriting with the documents already recovered.

The instruction was not written by Morelli.

It was not Charles Vale's handwriting either.

It appeared to be the same hand that had written the note in Morelli's notebook:

*The children must remain separated until the person who selected their receiving guardians is found.*

Eleanor.

But the conclusion was not certain.

Edward saved the photograph in the investigation archive and called Jonathan Pierce.

The lawyer answered after several rings.

"Edward?"

"I've received another document."

"At this hour?"

"Someone sent it anonymously."

"Is it authentic?"

"I don't know yet. But Morelli's signature appears on it."

Pierce became more alert.

"What does it say?"

Edward read the document aloud.

When he finished, Pierce remained silent.

"Relationship to child: none," he finally repeated.

"Yes."

"That is interesting."

"Why?"

"Because the acknowledgment we found for Infant B identified Charles Vale as a legal representative acting on behalf of a designated guardian. This one appears to identify the receiving guardian directly."

"But the name is incomplete."

"Margaret."

"Possibly."

Pierce considered the remaining text.

"There's another important distinction. The woman requested that the child's identity remain undisclosed. That suggests she knew the identity was being withheld."

"Unlike the person who received Infant B."

"We don't know what that person was told."

Edward looked at the final handwritten sentence.

"She must not be told."

"Who is she?"

"I don't know."

"Then we have two separate unknown women."

Edward paused.

"Possibly three."

"Three?"

"The receiving guardian. The mother who was supposed to make contact. And the woman who wrote the instruction."

Pierce exhaled.

"Tomorrow, we verify the document."

Edward looked at the clock.

"Tomorrow is already here."

At 7:30 the following morning, Edward was in the Valmont study.

Richard had arrived early.

Sebastian was reviewing the previous investigation reports, while Nicholas sat near the window with a cup of coffee.

Catherine entered shortly afterward.

"Has something happened?"

Edward placed the photograph on the table.

"An anonymous source sent this last night."

Richard read it.

His expression changed when he reached the handwritten note.

"Margaret."

"We don't know whether that is the complete name."

"Is there a Margaret connected to the old investigation?"

Sebastian opened the personnel records.

"Margaret Vale. Eleanor's niece."

"She's already spoken to us," Edward said.

"Yes. But she wasn't identified as a receiving guardian."

Nicholas looked at the photograph.

"Could the document refer to her?"

"Possibly," Edward replied. "But we cannot assume that."

Catherine read the line about the mother's contact.

"Why would a receiving guardian wait for a mother to make contact?"

"Perhaps she was told that the arrangement was temporary," Sebastian said.

"Or she believed the mother had been informed and would eventually come to collect the child," Edward added.

Richard looked toward him.

"Then someone deliberately kept her waiting."

"That is one possibility."

Catherine sat down.

"Who was the mother supposed to contact?"

Nobody answered.

Edward pointed to the final sentence.

"There's something else. The instruction says, 'She must not be told.'"

Nicholas frowned.

"Whoever wrote it knew that someone was going to ask the receiving guardian questions."

"Or knew that information might reach the wrong person," Sebastian said.

Richard looked at Edward.

"Find out who sent this."

"I'm trying."

"And speak to Morelli."

"I intend to."

Catherine was still examining the photograph.

"Edward?"

"Yes?"

"Was the woman who received Infant A told that the child might belong to another family?"

"We don't know."

"Then we should not assume she deliberately kept anyone from their mother."

Edward nodded.

"Agreed."

Catherine placed the photograph down.

"She may have been waiting for someone who never came."

For a moment, nobody spoke.

That possibility was difficult to dismiss.

At the Aurelius residence, Alexander received a copy of the document shortly after breakfast.

Adrian had already contacted the repository and requested an independent examination.

Victoria read the document twice.

"Margaret."

"That's all we have," Adrian said.

"Could it be Eleanor's niece?"

"She has been interviewed. She denied receiving either child, and there is no evidence connecting her to the ninth-day release."

Damien examined the bottom of the photograph.

"Morelli witnessed this."

"Yes."

"Then he knew the receiving guardian."

Alexander nodded.

"He told us he knew both identities."

Lucien leaned forward.

"Why would he witness an acknowledgment and then spend eighteen years refusing to explain it?"

"Because he may have been instructed to keep the information confidential," Adrian replied.

"That doesn't explain why he never contacted us."

"No. It doesn't."

Victoria looked toward her husband.

"Did he say whether the guardian was given the child's name?"

"No. He said the receiving arrangements were temporary."

"Then perhaps the woman was never told the infant's identity."

Alexander considered the document.

"She may have been told that the mother would contact her."

"And if no one did?"

"Then she may have raised the child."

Victoria lowered her eyes.

A thought had occurred to her.

If the woman had believed she was caring for a child temporarily, what had happened when the expected contact never arrived?

Had she waited a week?

A month?

A year?

Had she eventually accepted that nobody was coming?

Or had she spent eighteen years believing that a mother had chosen not to return?

Victoria looked at Alexander.

"We need to find her."

"Yes."

Julian, who had been listening quietly, finally spoke.

"Would she know where my sister is?"

"She might," Alexander answered.

"Would she tell us?"

Alexander did not immediately respond.

"I don't know."

Julian looked at the photograph again.

"Then perhaps we should ask her what happened before we ask her to give anything back."

Victoria turned toward him.

"That is a good thought."

Julian nodded.

He had no intention of demanding that someone surrender a person they might have raised for eighteen years.

He wanted to meet his sister.

But he was beginning to understand that finding her would require more than locating a name.

It would require understanding the life she had lived.

At the Valmont mansion, Elena was preparing for her university interview.

She had changed her mind about her clothing twice before settling on a simple formal outfit.

Her notes were on the desk.

Her laptop was open.

A glass of water stood beside it.

Nicholas knocked.

"Ready?"

"Almost."

He looked at the desk.

"That looks like a preparation centre."

"I want to be ready."

"You are."

Elena adjusted the papers.

"What if they ask something unexpected?"

"Answer honestly."

"What if I don't know?"

"Say you don't know."

She looked at him.

"That sounds too easy."

"Because it is."

He leaned against the doorway.

"You don't need to pretend to know everything."

Elena smiled.

"I suppose not."

Nicholas glanced at the time.

"You have twenty minutes."

"Then I should stop talking."

"An excellent decision."

He left.

Elena closed her notes and took a deep breath.

The interview began ten minutes later.

The first questions concerned her academic record.

The next concerned her reasons for choosing the exchange programme.

Then the interviewer asked about her long-term plans.

Elena explained that she wanted to gain experience in a different academic environment and eventually work on projects involving education and access to learning resources.

"Why education?" the interviewer asked.

Elena paused.

"Because I think access to information changes what people believe is possible for themselves."

The interviewer nodded.

"Would you like to work in education professionally?"

"Perhaps. I'm interested in several fields, so I haven't decided on one career yet. But I'd like to build something that makes learning more accessible."

"That is a broad ambition."

"I know. I still have to understand how to make it practical."

The interviewer smiled.

"That is a sensible answer."

The conversation continued for another twenty minutes.

When it ended, Elena thanked the panel and closed her laptop.

She sat still for a moment.

Then she exhaled.

Her phone vibrated.

**Clara:** ALIVE?

**Elena:** Yes.

**Clara:** SUCCESS?

**Elena:** I think it went well.

**Clara:** EMERGENCY BISCUITS?

**Elena:** Definitely.

Elena smiled.

For the rest of the morning, she allowed herself to think about university rather than hospital records.

The document examiner contacted Edward shortly before noon.

The preliminary result was ready.

Edward and Pierce reviewed it together.

"The paper is consistent with the other ninth-day records," the examiner explained over a video call. "The hospital seal is authentic. The typewriter impression matches equipment previously used by the private nursery administration office."

"What about the signatures?" Pierce asked.

"Marcus Morelli's signature is consistent with the verified examples. The handwriting in the margin is not his."

"Can you identify it?"

"Not definitively. However, there are similarities with several handwritten instructions attributed to Eleanor Vale."

Edward looked at Pierce.

"How confident are you?"

"Moderate confidence. I would not present it as a conclusive identification."

"Can you examine the name?"

"The visible letters are consistent with Margaret, but the remainder has been removed from the photograph. We would need the original document."

Edward thanked the examiner and ended the call.

Pierce turned toward him.

"That gives us enough to ask Morelli about the document."

"Yes."

"Will he tell us?"

"We'll find out."

Edward called Morelli.

The call was answered almost immediately.

"Mr. Morelli, I received a document last night."

There was a pause.

"What document?"

Edward described it.

Silence followed.

Then Morelli said, "Where did you get it?"

"An anonymous message."

"Do you still have the original?"

"No. Only the photograph."

Morelli exhaled.

"Then someone has begun opening the records again."

"Who is Margaret?"

Another pause.

"Which Margaret?"

"The receiving guardian of Infant A."

Morelli did not answer.

Edward continued.

"The document bears your signature."

"I know."

"Did you witness the acknowledgment?"

"Yes."

"Was the guardian named Margaret?"

"Yes."

Edward sat straighter.

"Margaret what?"

"I cannot tell you."

"Why?"

"Because the name on that document was not her legal name."

Edward frowned.

"What?"

"She was using a different identity at the time."

"Why?"

"I don't know."

"Then what was her actual identity?"

Morelli's voice became firmer.

"I said I don't know."

Edward looked at Pierce.

"Was she connected to either family?"

"I don't know."

"Did she know the infant was registered under the Aurelius name?"

"No."

"Did she know the infant might be the biological daughter of Alexander and Victoria Aurelius?"

"No."

"Then what did she know?"

Morelli took a breath.

"She knew she was receiving a baby whose identity had not been established. She knew the arrangement was supposed to be temporary. She knew someone would contact her when the mother's identity was confirmed."

"Did she know there were two children?"

"No."

Edward went still.

"She didn't know about Infant B?"

"No."

"Did she know the child had been born at the same hospital as another infant?"

"No."

"Then she believed she was receiving one child in an isolated arrangement."

"Yes."

Edward looked down at his notes.

"Why was she selected?"

"I wasn't told."

"Who selected her?"

"Charles delivered the instruction."

"That isn't an answer."

"It's the only one I can give you."

Edward's voice hardened.

"Morelli, you witnessed the acknowledgment. You knew her name, her circumstances, and the terms of the arrangement. You cannot tell me you have no idea why she was selected."

"I can tell you what I witnessed."

"Then do that."

Morelli was silent for several seconds.

"She asked whether the child's mother knew where the baby was."

"Yes. Martha told us."

"She was told the mother had been informed."

"Was that true?"

"No."

Edward closed his eyes briefly.

"Who told her?"

"Charles Vale."

"Did she believe him?"

"I believe she did."

Edward looked at the document again.

"And the instruction, 'She must not be told.' Who was that referring to?"

Morelli's answer came quietly.

"Not the guardian."

Edward frowned.

"Then who?"

"The mother."

The call ended without another explanation.

Edward remained seated.

Pierce looked at him.

"Which mother?"

"Morelli wouldn't say."

"Did he explain why the mother was not supposed to be told?"

"No."

Pierce considered the information.

"Then we have a significant development."

"Which is?"

"At least one receiving guardian was deliberately kept unaware of the child's identity. The biological mother was also kept unaware of the placement."

Edward looked at the photograph.

"Both women were waiting for information that never arrived."

"Possibly."

"And someone knew that."

"Yes."

Edward stood.

"We need to find Margaret."

The search began with the name recorded in the acknowledgment.

Margaret.

The investigation team reviewed the records of women who had been associated with Vale Private Counsel during the relevant period.

They examined private guardianship applications, temporary care arrangements, hospital visitor registers, and archived correspondence.

The first day produced nothing.

The second produced three possible identities.

The third eliminated two.

The remaining record belonged to a woman who had used the name Margaret during the ninth-day arrangement.

Her actual name was not included in the public documents.

She had submitted a request for temporary guardianship through an independent legal office six months before the births.

The request had been withdrawn.

No reason was recorded.

Edward studied the application.

"Why would someone apply for temporary guardianship and then withdraw?"

Pierce examined the date.

"Perhaps her circumstances changed."

"Then why was she later selected to receive an infant?"

"That is what we need to establish."

The application contained a correspondence address.

A small residential property in a northern district.

The property had been sold fourteen years earlier.

The former owner had moved.

But the forwarding address led to a care facility.

Edward checked the admission records.

The woman had been living there for three years.

Her name was different.

Her age was consistent.

And her date of birth matched the old guardianship application.

Edward placed the record down.

"We found her."

The care facility was quiet.

It stood outside the main town, surrounded by trees and a low boundary wall.

Edward arrived with Pierce.

The administrator met them in a small office.

"You said you were looking for a former resident's visitor records?"

"Yes," Edward replied. "A woman who may have used the name Margaret eighteen years ago."

The administrator reviewed the information.

"We have a resident who matches the age range. But she has memory difficulties."

"Can we speak with her?"

"That depends on her condition today. She has good days and bad days."

Pierce nodded.

"We understand."

The administrator led them down a corridor.

At the end was a small sitting room.

An elderly woman sat beside the window.

Her hair was white.

A knitted blanket rested across her knees.

She was looking at a photograph in her hands.

The administrator approached.

"Margaret? You have visitors."

The woman looked up.

Her eyes moved to Edward.

Then to Pierce.

"Who are you?"

Edward introduced himself.

"We're investigating an old matter concerning a hospital in the city."

The woman looked at the photograph again.

"Which hospital?"

"St. Catherine's Medical Centre."

Her fingers tightened around the photograph.

For a moment, her expression changed.

"That was a long time ago."

"Yes."

Edward sat in the chair opposite her.

"We believe you may have received a newborn child on the ninth day after two infants were born there."

The woman's eyes moved back to him.

"Two?"

"Yes."

She looked confused.

"I only had one."

Edward remained still.

"One what?"

"One baby."

Pierce glanced at him.

Edward continued carefully.

"Do you remember receiving a baby from St. Catherine's?"

The woman looked toward the window.

"I remember the hospital."

"Do you remember who brought the child?"

"A man."

"Was his name Charles?"

She frowned.

"I don't remember."

"Do you remember Marcus?"

The woman looked at him sharply.

"Marcus?"

Edward nodded.

"Marcus Morelli."

The photograph slipped slightly in her hands.

"That man shouldn't have come back."

Edward leaned forward.

"Why?"

The woman looked at him.

For the first time, her expression was completely clear.

"Because he promised he would never tell them where I was."

Edward felt his heartbeat quicken.

"Who?"

She looked down at the photograph.

Then she turned it around.

It showed a young woman holding a newborn baby.

The woman's face was partly obscured by the edge of the picture.

But the baby was visible.

A small hospital identification band rested around her wrist.

Edward recognised the format.

The same hospital.

The same year.

He leaned closer.

"Is this the child you received?"

The woman looked at the photograph for a long time.

Then she nodded.

"Yes."

"Do you know who her mother was?"

The woman's eyes filled with tears.

"Of course I knew."

Edward froze.

"You knew?"

The woman nodded again.

"They told me her mother would come."

"Who told you?"

"Charles."

"Did he tell you the mother's name?"

The woman shook her head.

"No."

Edward waited.

She looked at him.

"But I knew she wasn't supposed to come."

"Why?"

The woman's fingers trembled around the photograph.

"Because they said the mother had already been told that her baby was dead."

Edward stopped breathing for a moment.

Pierce looked at him.

The woman continued, her voice barely audible.

"And I was told that if I ever saw the mother, I must never let her know the child was alive."

She lowered her eyes to the photograph.

"I thought I was protecting that little girl."

Edward looked at the image.

The infant's identification band was turned toward the camera.

Only two details were visible.

The birth time.

And the letter.

**A.**

The woman looked up again.

"Tell me something."

Edward nodded.

"Is she still alive?"

He could not answer immediately.

Because he did not yet know which child the photograph showed.

He did not know whether the woman had raised Elena.

He did not know whether the mother she had been told to avoid was Victoria Aurelius or Catherine Valmont.

And he did not know why someone had told a receiving guardian that the child's mother believed her baby was dead.

But one thing was now clear.

At least one person had known that a child was alive while her biological mother was being led to believe she was gone.

And the person who had arranged that deception had not merely hidden a child.

They had instructed another woman to keep her alive in silence.
