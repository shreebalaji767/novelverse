# **Chapter 226 — The Right to Hear the Truth**

Elena did not answer the invitation that night.

She read it once more, saved a copy, and placed her phone face down on the desk.

Then she opened her notebook.

At the top of a fresh page, she wrote:

**Questions before decisions.**

She did not know what the historical identity review concerned.

She did not know who had initiated it.

She did not know whether the records involved her biological family, a hospital error, or something entirely different.

But she knew one thing.

She would not agree to a procedure simply because someone described it as necessary.

She wanted to understand the evidence first.

At 8:15 the next morning, she sent a reply.

**I am willing to receive an explanation from an independent legal representative. This does not constitute consent to provide biological samples, disclose my personal records, or participate in any further examination. Please provide the representative's credentials and the proposed scope of the discussion in advance.**

She read the message twice.

Then she sent it.

The legal office responded within an hour.

The proposed representative was Ms. Laura Bennett, an independent solicitor appointed under the restricted identity verification procedure.

Her role was to explain the process and protect Elena's interests.

She was not representing the originating family.

She was not representing the Aurelius family.

She was not acting for the Valmont family.

She was not authorised to persuade Elena to participate.

Her appointment letter included a clear restriction:

**The representative must not disclose unverified family conclusions or present a proposed biological relationship as established fact.**

Elena checked the solicitor's registration through the professional directory.

She also asked Ms. Tan whether the programme could provide a private room for a confidential legal consultation.

Ms. Tan agreed.

“You don't have to tell me what it's about,” she said. “But if you need time away from the programme, let me know.”

“Thank you. I don't know how long this will take.”

“Then we'll work around that.”

Elena closed her laptop.

She had a meeting scheduled for the afternoon.

For now, she intended to keep it.

The consultation began at 2:00 p.m.

Laura Bennett appeared on a secure video call.

“Thank you for agreeing to speak with me,” she said. “Before we begin, I want to confirm that you understand my role.”

“I read the appointment letter.”

“Good. I represent your interests during this initial explanation. I do not represent the family that requested the historical review, and I cannot make decisions on your behalf.”

“Can you tell me who requested it?”

“Not yet. The originating authority has not been disclosed to you because the current authorisation permits only an initial explanation of the review.”

Elena frowned.

“Then how am I supposed to decide whether to participate?”

“You are not being asked to participate in the biological comparison today. You are being given the opportunity to understand why the review has reached your attention.”

“Will I be told who the originating authority is?”

“If you decide to proceed to the next stage, the disclosure conditions require that the relevant authority and the purpose of the proposed examination be explained before you provide any biological sample.”

“Can I receive that information without agreeing to testing?”

Laura paused.

“Yes. You can request further information without agreeing to testing. Some protected details may require a separate disclosure decision, but your decision not to provide a sample cannot be treated as consent.”

Elena wrote that down.

“Then let's begin with the records.”

Laura opened a document.

“An independent historical records examination identified a documentary connection between a protected personal identity record and an original clinical record from St Catherine's Hospital.”

“What kind of connection?”

“The protected identity record contains an original reference to the clinical source. The reference is genuine.”

“Does that mean the hospital record is mine?”

“Not yet. The examination established that the records are connected historically. It did not establish that every later receiving reference was correct.”

Elena looked at her notebook.

“What does the clinical record say?”

“It records a female infant delivered at 2:17 a.m.”

The room seemed to become quieter.

Elena looked at the screen.

“Is that the only reason you contacted me?”

“No. The contact was authorised because an independent examiner matched the protected personal identity record to a living person's current civil registration through two independent records.”

“And that person is me?”

Laura did not answer immediately.

“The independent examiner has verified that the living person identified through the protected record is you, Elena. That verification concerns the historical identity record and your current civil registration. It does not establish biological parentage.”

Elena sat back.

For several seconds, she said nothing.

Then she asked, “What was the name in the original record?”

“That information is subject to a separate disclosure decision. I can tell you that the historical declaration and your current personal name are different.”

“Was my name changed?”

“The examiner has not determined the reason for the difference. The records show different names. They do not establish whether the change was made lawfully, under what circumstances, or by whose decision.”

Elena looked down at her hands.

She had spent months trying to understand why her life had changed so suddenly.

Now someone was telling her that even the name she had carried since childhood might not be the first name recorded for her.

She looked back at the screen.

“Does this mean I was exchanged at birth?”

“No. That conclusion has not been established.”

“Does it mean I belong to another family?”

“No biological family relationship has been established.”

“Then what do you actually know?”

Laura answered carefully.

“We know that your current civil identity can be matched to the living person described in the protected personal identity record. We know that the protected record contains an original reference to the St Catherine's clinical source. We know that the original receiving-family reference was provisional. We do not know whether the infant in the clinical record is biologically related to the family that initiated the historical request.”

Elena wrote each point down.

The list was less dramatic than the questions in her head.

But it was more useful.

“Can I see the documents?” Elena asked.

“Some of them. The original historical records contain protected information about other individuals. You are entitled to receive an explanation of the evidence concerning you, but the source custodians must approve disclosure of the relevant extracts.”

“Can I have copies?”

“Where disclosure is authorised, yes.”

“Can I have an independent lawyer review them?”

“Certainly.”

“Can I refuse the DNA test after seeing them?”

“Yes.”

“Can I ask for a different laboratory?”

“If a biological comparison is proposed, the laboratory and examiner must meet the independence requirements. You may raise objections and request alternatives. The competent authority will consider them.”

Elena paused.

“Who pays for it?”

“The preservation and review arrangement has allocated funds for the independent process. You would not be required to pay for the proposed examination.”

“Does that mean I owe anyone anything?”

“No.”

“Can the family use the result to claim that I must move in with them?”

“No. A biological result does not create an obligation to live with anyone.”

Elena looked at Laura carefully.

“Can they use it to make decisions for me?”

“No. Your legal identity, personal autonomy, and choices are separate questions from biological parentage.”

Elena let out a breath she had not realised she was holding.

“Good.”

After the consultation, Elena requested a written summary.

Laura sent it before the end of the day.

Elena read it in full.

The final section listed the available choices.

She could decline further participation.

She could request additional documentary disclosure.

She could ask for an independent review of the historical records.

She could agree to a separate biological comparison after receiving all necessary information.

No choice would be treated as an admission or a rejection of any family relationship.

Elena saved the summary.

Then she called Edward.

He answered immediately.

“Did you speak with the representative?”

“Yes.”

“What did she tell you?”

Elena explained the historical clinical record, the protected identity record, and the independent civil registration verification.

Edward listened without interrupting.

When she finished, he asked, “How are you feeling?”

“Confused.”

“That makes sense.”

“Edward, did you know about this?”

“I knew the historical records might lead to a living identity. I did not know the identity would be disclosed to you today.”

“Did you know it might be me?”

“I knew you were a possible subject. I did not have evidence that justified telling you that.”

Elena was silent.

“Did you contact the Aurelius family?”

“No. The biological relationship has not been established.”

“Have they seen my name?”

“Not through this authorised disclosure.”

She looked toward the window.

“Then I want to keep it that way until I know more.”

“I understand.”

“No, I mean it. I don't want people arriving here with explanations about who I am.”

“They won't be approached through this process without the appropriate authority.”

Elena considered his answer.

“Good.”

She ended the call.

That evening, Isabelle called.

“Are you all right?”

Elena hesitated.

“Did Edward tell you something?”

“No. He only said you might be dealing with a personal matter and that you should have space.”

Elena appreciated that.

“Someone contacted me about my historical identity records.”

Isabelle went quiet.

“Does it concern your birth?”

“Possibly.”

“Do you want to talk about it?”

“Not yet. I don't have enough information.”

“That's fine.”

Elena waited for another question.

None came.

Instead, Isabelle said, “Do you want me to postpone the foundation review?”

“No. The meeting is separate. I'll decide if I need to change anything.”

“All right.”

“And Isabelle?”

“Yes?”

“Please don't tell the others.”

“I won't.”

Elena ended the call.

She did not feel ready to discuss the possibility with Richard or Catherine.

She did not even know what the possibility meant.

For now, she wanted to remain the person who had a pilot to prepare, an academic programme to complete, and a foundation review to conduct.

She did not want an uncertain historical record to take over every part of her life.

The next morning, Edward received a new authorisation from the RPRA.

The protected identity record could now be disclosed to Elena through her independent legal representative.

The disclosure included the historical name.

It also included the original declaration and the limited witness statement.

The receiving-family reference remained partially sealed pending a separate review.

Edward read the disclosure schedule.

The historical name was:

**Elena Aurelia.**

The date of birth matched the date in Elena's current civil registration.

The original declaration recorded a female infant.

The receiving-family reference remained provisional.

Edward looked at Eleanor.

“Now she knows the historical name.”

“Does she know the family?”

“Not yet.”

“Will the next disclosure establish it?”

“Not necessarily.”

He placed the authorisation on the desk.

The name was significant.

But names could be recorded, changed, duplicated, or entered incorrectly.

The date and documentary connection made the record more relevant.

They did not establish biological parentage.

At 11:40 a.m., Elena received the disclosure.

She opened the first page.

**Historical personal name: Elena Aurelia.**

She read it once.

Then again.

Her own first name.

A different surname.

A name she had never been told.

The next page contained the original declaration.

It recorded that a female infant had been registered under the name Elena Aurelia.

The date matched her current date of birth.

The clinical source reference was the St Catherine's record.

But the receiving-family section remained incomplete.

There was no final placement authority.

No proof that the infant had been delivered to a particular family.

No biological result.

Elena sat very still.

She had been raised as Elena Valmont.

She had been told she was not biologically related to the Valmont family.

Now an older record suggested that her original name might have been Elena Aurelia.

She opened the document again.

At the bottom of the disclosure was a note from Laura Bennett.

**The historical name is a verified entry in the protected personal identity record. It is not, by itself, proof of biological relationship to any family bearing that surname.**

Elena closed the document.

For a long time, she did not move.

Then she opened her notebook and wrote:

**A name is evidence of what was recorded. It is not proof of who gave birth to me.**

She underlined the sentence.

At the Aurelius estate, Alexander received a restricted notice.

The independent review had verified that the protected personal identity record was associated with a living person.

The identity had been disclosed to that person through an independent legal representative.

The notice did not include the person's name.

It did not state whether the person had agreed to biological testing.

Alexander read it.

Victoria stood beside him.

“Does this mean we've found her?”

“It means the records have identified a living person connected to the historical identity record.”

“Is that enough?”

“No.”

He placed the notice on the desk.

“Until a properly authorised biological comparison is completed, we cannot claim that person as our daughter.”

Victoria looked at the paper.

“Do they know about us?”

“They have received a historical name. The originating family identity remains restricted.”

Victoria nodded.

“Then we wait.”

Alexander looked toward the window.

He had spent years wanting an answer.

Now that an answer might be close, he understood how easily wanting it could become pressure on someone else.

“We wait,” he repeated.

That evening, Elena received one more message from Laura.

The original family authority had requested permission to disclose its identity to Elena.

The request had been approved in principle, subject to Elena's agreement to receive the information.

She could decline.

She could receive the identity in writing.

Or she could have Laura explain it during another confidential consultation.

Elena looked at the message.

She knew what the surname in the historical declaration suggested.

But she wanted to hear the actual evidence.

Not a guess.

Not a family story.

Not a conclusion drawn from a name.

She replied:

**I agree to receive the originating family's identity and the documents supporting its authority. This agreement does not constitute consent to biological testing or acceptance of any family relationship.**

She sent the message.

Then she put her phone aside.

The next disclosure would tell her which family had initiated the search.

It still would not tell her whether that family was biologically hers.

For the first time, Elena was not waiting for someone else to decide who she was.

She was deciding how much of the truth she was ready to hear.
