# **Chapter 124 — The Signature That Belonged to Someone Else**

Margaret Voss did not want to meet at the former hospital.

She chose a small conference room at her solicitor's office instead, explaining that she wanted the conversation documented and the original records protected.

Edward agreed.

By the time he arrived, Margaret had already arranged three folders on the table.

The first was marked **OR-28**.

The second was marked **CONDITIONAL RESPONSE**.

The third had no label.

Edward took the chair opposite her.

“You said you know who signed the final response.”

“I do.”

“Who?”

Margaret opened the second folder.

“Before I tell you, I want you to understand something. The person who signed it did not sign as the proposed recipient.”

Edward glanced at Jonathan.

“What capacity did they sign in?”

“As a representative authorised to communicate on behalf of the person being considered.”

“Were they authorised to accept the child?”

“No.”

“Were they authorised to appoint themselves as receiving representative?”

“No.”

“Then what exactly were they authorised to do?”

Margaret removed a document.

“Communicate a conditional response.”

Edward looked at it.

The signature was familiar.

Not because he had seen it in the family records, but because it appeared on several of the old administrative acknowledgements.

**Gabriel Vale.**

Edward examined the signature.

“Gabriel signed the conditional response?”

“Yes.”

“Did he write the statement?”

“No. Anna prepared the wording under my instruction. Gabriel signed it after the proposed recipient's representative reviewed the document.”

“Who was that representative?”

Margaret hesitated.

“Gabriel was acting under a written instruction from the person whose identity was being protected.”

“Was that person the proposed recipient?”

“No.”

Edward looked up.

“Then who?”

“The person who had been asked to communicate with the proposed recipient.”

Edward leaned forward.

“Explain the arrangement.”

Margaret folded her hands.

“The proposed recipient was not supposed to receive an unidentified child's records. The initial instruction was to establish whether they were willing to consider a possible arrangement, but only after sufficient information was available.”

“That sounds like the conditional response.”

“It was.”

“Then why did Gabriel sign it?”

“Because the proposed recipient had not yet been given enough information to sign anything personally. Their representative was asked to communicate their position.”

Edward looked at the statement again.

The first sentence said the person was willing to receive further information.

The second required originating identity and authority to be established.

The third refused permanent responsibility for an unidentified child.

It could plausibly have been a representative's statement.

But there was still a missing link.

“Did Gabriel have written authority to represent the proposed recipient?”

“Yes.”

“Where is it?”

Margaret opened the first folder.

“Here.”

The document was dated day 39.

Its heading read:

**LIMITED COMMUNICATION AUTHORITY — AV-18**

The first paragraph identified Gabriel Vale as an authorised intermediary.

The second defined his authority.

He could:

- Receive information concerning the proposed arrangement.
- Communicate questions to the designated representative.
- Return a conditional response.
- Preserve correspondence pending identity verification.

He could not:

- Accept permanent responsibility for a child.
- Approve a placement.
- Confirm biological identity.
- Appoint a receiving identity custodian.
- Waive independent verification.

Edward read the restrictions carefully.

“Who granted this authority?”

Margaret turned the page.

The authorising signature belonged to an individual identified only by a protected reference.

**R-12 / PROPOSED RECIPIENT REPRESENTATIVE.**

The signature field had been completed.

But the name remained sealed.

“Who was this?” Edward asked.

“I don't know the person's legal identity.”

“Did you ever see them?”

“No.”

“Did Gabriel?”

“I believe so.”

“Did he tell you who they were?”

“No.”

Edward looked at the authority.

“Then how do you know Gabriel was authorised?”

“Because the original authority was verified by Eleanor Vale.”

“Did Eleanor know the identity?”

“She received it in a sealed verification record. She confirmed the authority was genuine, but she did not disclose the person's name to me.”

Edward sat back.

The arrangement had been deliberately divided.

The proposed recipient's representative could communicate.

The administrative liaison could receive correspondence.

The independent reviewer could examine the conditions.

But no one was authorised to complete the appointment.

And the person who had supposedly been asked to consider receiving a child remained unidentified.

Jonathan pointed to the date.

“Day 39. The modified acknowledgement template was created on the same day.”

Margaret nodded.

“Yes.”

“Was that related?”

“Possibly. The Family Allocation Review department wanted a separate record of the proposed representative's communication.”

“Did you approve the modification?”

“No.”

“Did you see the altered version?”

“Not until later.”

Edward placed the original conditional response beside the authority.

“Was this signed by Gabriel in your presence?”

“No.”

“Who witnessed it?”

“Anna Mercer.”

Edward looked up.

“She said she never saw the final response.”

“Then she may have seen the draft, not the signature.”

“Did she witness Gabriel signing?”

Margaret shook her head.

“I was not present either.”

“Then who verified the signature?”

“Eleanor.”

Edward paused.

“Eleanor Vale?”

“Yes.”

“Did she verify the signature against Gabriel's known signature?”

“I believe she verified his identity and authority.”

“Those are different things.”

Margaret nodded.

“I agree.”

Edward studied the certificate.

“Was the original document sent to Cross Administrative Services?”

“Yes. Gabriel signed it and returned it through the protected correspondence channel.”

“Was it then sent to R-12?”

“Not directly. It was preserved by Cross Administrative Services and a copy was placed in the R-12 review file.”

“Why?”

“Because Dr Shaw needed to know that a conditional response existed.”

Edward looked at the date.

Day 40.

The response had arrived before Dr Shaw completed her review.

That matched the archive.

“Did Dr Shaw receive the original?”

“No. She received a copy of the response and the proposed recipient profile.”

“Did she know who had signed it?”

“She knew the signatory was Gabriel Vale.”

“Did she know whose position he was communicating?”

Margaret shook her head.

“I don't believe she did.”

Edward turned to the third folder.

“What is this?”

Margaret opened it.

Inside was a handwritten note, dated day 41.

It was addressed to her.

The writing was Gabriel's.

*The conditional response has been returned. No permanent acceptance has been given. The proposed recipient is willing to consider further information, provided the originating identity is verified and the scope of responsibility is explained.*

Below that:

*Please ensure the response is not entered as consent to appointment.*

Edward read it twice.

“This confirms Gabriel understood the limitation.”

“Yes.”

“Did he know the recipient's identity?”

“Yes.”

“Did he tell you?”

“No.”

“Why not?”

“Because the identity was protected from the originating records office until the verification conditions were satisfied.”

Edward frowned.

“That seems circular.”

“It was.”

“Who designed the procedure?”

“Charles Vale's office proposed it. Eleanor insisted on additional restrictions. Dr Shaw was asked to review the proposed recipient independently.”

“And the process never reached completion.”

“Correct.”

Edward placed the note down.

“Why did the day-47 summary say consent recorded?”

Margaret looked at him.

“Because someone used the existence of the conditional response to support the administrative status.”

“Who?”

“I don't know.”

“Did you challenge it?”

“Yes. I entered a correction in the R-04 register.”

“Did you inform Charles?”

“I did.”

“What did he say?”

Margaret looked away briefly.

“He said the response was sufficient to retain the proposed recipient as a continuity option.”

“Did you agree?”

“No.”

“Why not?”

“Because Gabriel had not communicated acceptance. He had communicated willingness to consider further information.”

She tapped the note.

“That is what this says.”

Edward examined the authority document again.

A small reference appeared in the lower corner.

**PR-06 / PROTECTED REPRESENTATION**

“What's PR-06?”

Margaret looked at the code.

“The separate register for representatives acting on behalf of proposed recipients.”

“Where is it?”

“I don't know.”

“Was it held by R-12?”

“No. R-12 received only the material required for review.”

“Cross Administrative Services?”

“No.”

“Then where?”

Margaret hesitated.

“I believe Eleanor held the register.”

Edward called Eleanor from the meeting room.

She answered after the second ring.

“Edward?”

“I'm with Margaret Voss. We're examining the day-40 conditional response and Gabriel Vale's limited communication authority.”

There was a pause.

“I see.”

“Margaret says the authority was verified by you.”

“Yes.”

“Do you still have the PR-06 register?”

Another pause.

“I have a preserved extract.”

“Can we examine it?”

“Yes. But I need to make something clear before you see it.”

“What?”

“The register identifies representatives and the scope of their authority. It does not necessarily identify the people they represented.”

Edward looked at Margaret.

“Understood.”

Eleanor gave them an appointment for the following morning.

The PR-06 extract was thinner than Edward expected.

It contained only six entries.

Each recorded a protected representative, the scope of their authority, and the office responsible for preserving the original identity record.

The entry for Gabriel Vale read:

**Representative: Gabriel Vale**

**Authority: Limited communication**

**Reference: AV-18**

**Verified: Day 39**

**Permitted activity: Conditional response and information requests**

**Permanent appointment authority: None**

The next column contained a protected principal reference.

**P-04.**

Edward looked at Eleanor.

“Who is P-04?”

“I cannot identify the person from the extract alone.”

“Do you have the original identity record?”

“Yes.”

“Can we see it?”

“Not without the required joint authority.”

“Who can authorise access?”

“The person whose identity is recorded, or their legally appointed representative, subject to the original protection instruction.”

Edward frowned.

“Is that person still alive?”

“I don't know.”

“Do you know whether the person was a family member?”

“No.”

“Were they connected to the hospital?”

“Not according to the information available to me.”

Edward looked at the reference.

P-04 was not a family name.

Not a child number.

Not a maternal record.

It was a protected principal.

Someone who had authorised Gabriel to communicate on their behalf.

“Why was the principal's identity kept separate?”

Eleanor answered carefully.

“Because the proposed recipient's representative was not supposed to become part of the child's identity record. Their role was to communicate a position, not establish the child's biological identity.”

“Was the principal the proposed recipient?”

Eleanor hesitated.

“I believe so, but I cannot certify that from the extract.”

“Why?”

“Because the protected principal could have been the proposed recipient or a person legally authorised to act for them. The original identity record was required to distinguish those possibilities.”

Edward looked at the register.

Even now, the identity remained unresolved.

But the investigation had established something important.

Gabriel had not signed as the proposed recipient.

He had signed as an intermediary.

And the person whose position he communicated had not accepted permanent responsibility.

At the Aurelius Estate, Alexander received the latest report.

He read the section concerning Gabriel's limited communication authority.

“Gabriel was acting for the proposed recipient.”

“Yes,” Edward said over the call.

“Do we know who that was?”

“No.”

“Was the person connected to either originating family?”

“We cannot establish that.”

Alexander looked at the reference P-04.

“Could this be the person who was supposed to receive one of the children?”

“Possibly. But the authority does not prove that.”

Victoria, sitting beside him, read the report.

“Did this person ever meet the child?”

“No evidence shows that.”

“Did they know the child's biological identity?”

“Apparently not. The response required the originating identity to be verified first.”

Victoria was quiet.

“Then they were being asked to consider accepting a child without knowing whose child it was.”

“That is what the records suggest.”

She looked at Alexander.

“That isn't consent.”

“No,” he replied.

“It is not even an informed decision.”

Alexander returned his attention to Edward.

“Continue with the original authority. We need to know who P-04 was.”

Edward nodded.

“That is our next step.”

In Singapore, Elena had begun her second week of classes.

Her timetable was demanding, but manageable.

She had attended the introductory lectures, joined a student project group, and started mapping the university's available research resources.

Her grant management prototype had also made progress.

She had completed the basic application tracking screen and was now working on the reviewer permissions.

The most difficult part was deciding how much information each person should be allowed to see.

A student submitting an application should be able to track its status.

A reviewer should see the material needed to assess it.

An administrator should be able to monitor deadlines and completeness.

But none of them should be able to change another person's decision without leaving a record.

Elena wrote that last sentence in her notes.

Then she added:

**Access to information is not the same as authority to make a decision.**

She looked at it for a moment.

The principle seemed obvious.

Yet she knew how easily institutions could ignore it.

Her phone vibrated.

A message from Isabelle appeared.

**The foundation committee approved the revised grant process. They're going to test it with a small group of applications.**

Elena smiled.

**That's good. Make sure the test records include rejected applications too. You need to see whether the process is fair, not just whether it works for successful applicants.**

Isabelle replied:

**I knew you'd say that.**

Elena laughed quietly.

Then she returned to her work.

The following afternoon, Edward received a message from Eleanor.

The original PR-06 identity file had been located.

It was not in her personal archive.

It had been transferred to Meridian on day 61 with the other protected correspondence records.

Its preservation reference was:

**PR-06/P-04 — ORIGINAL PRINCIPAL AUTHORITY**

The transfer inventory included a final instruction.

*The identity of the principal must remain separate from the identity of the child and the proposed receiving arrangement. No disclosure may be made until the principal's authority and the originating identity are independently established.*

Edward read it carefully.

The wording suggested that P-04 had been given a role in the proposed arrangement.

But it did not establish whether P-04 was the person expected to receive the child, a representative of that person, or someone else entirely.

He called Jonathan.

“Request the original PR-06 file.”

“Will the archive release it?”

“Not without the required authority.”

“Then what do we do?”

Edward looked at the final line of the transfer instruction.

There was another reference.

**P-04 — PERSONAL ACKNOWLEDGEMENT OUTSTANDING.**

He opened the day-41 correspondence note.

Gabriel had written that the proposed recipient was willing to consider further information.

But the original principal acknowledgement had never been completed.

That meant the administration had a representative's statement, not a verified personal response from the principal.

Edward closed the folder.

“Find out who was supposed to obtain that acknowledgement.”

Jonathan nodded.

“And if nobody was appointed?”

“Then we find out why Gabriel was allowed to speak at all.”

Edward looked at the two documents.

One recorded a conditional response.

The other recorded an authority to communicate.

Neither established that the proposed recipient had personally agreed to anything.

And the most important signature—the one belonging to the person whose decision actually mattered—was still missing.

**For eighteen years, the records had preserved the representative's words while leaving the principal's own answer unverified.**
