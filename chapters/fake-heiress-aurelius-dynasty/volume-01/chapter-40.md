# **Chapter 40 — The Name Beneath the Ink**

Edward read the word again.

**CATH...**

Five letters.

Possibly six, depending on the faint mark beneath the final stroke.

He had spent the better part of an hour examining the recovered impression under different lighting conditions. The document examiner had already warned him that the underlying writing was incomplete. It might be a name. It might be the beginning of a longer word. Without the original sheet, there was no certainty.

Yet Edward could not ignore it.

Catherine.

His mother's name.

He leaned back in his chair and looked at the two documents spread across the table.

The first was Eleanor Vale's letter to Margaret Ellison.

The second was the tenth-day acknowledgment showing that Eleanor had received Infant B.

The letter contained two distinct instructions.

The original text said that Margaret must not assume the child she had received was the child named in the original hospital instruction.

The handwritten addition clarified that the instruction applied only to Infant A.

Infant B had already been received by Eleanor.

Then there was the sentence beneath the altered section.

*If Infant B is ever returned, do not give her to the person who came for Infant A.*

And beneath that, the incomplete name.

Edward rubbed his forehead.

Someone had anticipated that Infant B might be returned.

Someone had specifically warned against giving her to the person who had come for Infant A.

But who was that person?

And why had Eleanor considered the two receiving parties so dangerous to confuse?

His phone vibrated.

A message from Jonathan Pierce appeared.

**The examiner has finished comparing the ink. We need to talk.**

Edward immediately called him.

"Tell me."

"The documents weren't written at the same time."

Edward straightened.

"How far apart?"

"That's the problem. We can't establish an exact date from ink alone. But the original letter and the handwritten addition are consistent with different writing sessions. The addition was made later."

"How much later?"

"At least several days, possibly longer. The paper also shows a slight difference in pressure and indentation."

Edward looked at the tenth-day acknowledgment.

"So Eleanor wrote the original letter before she received Infant B."

"That is the likely sequence."

"And the addition?"

"Afterward. She knew Infant B was with her when she added it."

Edward was silent.

"There's something else," Jonathan continued. "The phrase about the person who came for Infant A is written in the same ink as the partial name. It wasn't part of the original letter."

Edward stared at the page.

"Meaning Eleanor wrote it after receiving Infant B?"

"Most likely."

Edward looked again at the word.

CATH.

If the sequence was correct, Eleanor had received Infant B, then amended her instructions to Margaret, then written the incomplete name.

She had not merely anticipated a possible problem.

She had reacted to something that had already happened.

"Can the handwriting be compared with Catherine's?"

"We can compare samples, but we shouldn't confuse handwriting identification with identification of the person mentioned. Even if the writing is Eleanor's, it doesn't tell us who she meant."

"I understand."

"Edward, one more thing."

"What?"

"The phrase about the person who came for Infant A is written in the same ink as the partial name. It wasn't part of the original letter."

Edward stared at the page.

"Send me the full examination."

"I will."

The call ended.

For several seconds, Edward remained still.

Then he picked up his phone and called his father.

"Father, I need to ask Mother something about the hospital."

Richard answered after two rings.

"Has something new emerged?"

"Possibly. I don't want to alarm her unnecessarily, but I need to establish whether she remembers anyone visiting the hospital after the births."

"She has already answered that question."

"I know. This is more specific."

A pause.

"Come to the study."

Catherine was in the small sitting room adjoining the study when Edward arrived.

She was reviewing documents for the foundation. A notebook lay open beside her, filled with handwritten notes about a scholarship programme Isabelle had recently proposed.

She looked up.

"Is something wrong?"

"Not necessarily."

Richard stood near the window.

Edward placed a copy of the recovered letter on the table.

"I need you to read something."

Catherine adjusted her glasses.

Her expression changed as she reached the final paragraph.

"Where did you find this?"

"In Eleanor Vale's papers."

Catherine read the sentence again.

"If Infant B is ever returned, do not give her to the person who came for Infant A."

She looked up.

"What does this mean?"

"We don't know yet."

Her gaze moved to the incomplete writing beneath it.

"CATH..."

Edward watched her carefully.

"Does that mean anything to you?"

Catherine's fingers tightened around the paper.

"It could mean Catherine."

"Your name?"

"Or someone else's. There are many names beginning with those letters."

"Do you remember meeting Eleanor Vale?"

Catherine thought for a moment.

"I met her once, I believe. At a charity event, years before the children were born. We weren't acquainted."

"Did you ever meet her at the hospital?"

"No."

"Did anyone approach you on her behalf?"

Catherine's expression became troubled.

"Edward, what exactly are you asking?"

He hesitated.

"We believe Eleanor may have known something about the person who received Infant A. We also believe she was concerned that Infant B might be returned to the wrong person."

Catherine looked down at the letter.

"Why would she be concerned about that?"

"That's what we're trying to establish."

Richard moved closer.

"Edward, is there a reason you're connecting this to Catherine?"

"The partial name."

Richard examined it.

"That is hardly proof."

"I agree."

Catherine placed the paper down.

"Then don't let the resemblance become an answer before we've established the question."

Edward looked at her.

For a moment, he almost smiled.

It was precisely the kind of thing she would say.

"That's why I'm asking you instead of making an assumption."

Catherine's gaze softened.

"Then ask."

Edward took out his notebook.

"After you gave birth, do you remember anyone asking you to identify a person who might collect the baby?"

"No."

"Anyone asking whether you would accept a temporary arrangement?"

"No."

"Did you ever sign a document allowing someone else to receive your child?"

Catherine closed her eyes briefly.

"I signed several documents. I was exhausted. I remember the medical forms, the consent forms, and something concerning the private nursery."

"Do you remember what the nursery document said?"

"No."

"Did you read it?"

"I believe so, but I cannot honestly say I remember every line."

Edward wrote that down.

Catherine looked at him.

"Edward, are you suggesting I was the person who came for Infant A?"

"No. I'm asking whether you remember being approached."

"I don't."

Richard's expression hardened.

"That is enough for today."

Edward closed his notebook.

He had not expected an answer.

But he had hoped for a memory.

He had received neither.

At the Aurelius Estate, Victoria was reading the latest report when Adrian entered.

She did not look up immediately.

"Have they found Morelli?"

"No. He has agreed to provide additional testimony, but he still refuses to identify the people involved in the tenth-day arrangements."

Victoria turned a page.

"Does he know who received Infant A?"

"He knows the receiving guardian's name. He says the identity cannot be separated from the original instructions."

"That sounds like another excuse."

"It may be. But he has consistently distinguished between what he witnessed and what he was told."

Alexander entered behind Adrian.

"Any progress on the hospital records?"

"Some."

Adrian placed the report on the desk.

"The forensic examiner believes Eleanor's letter to Margaret was amended after the tenth-day transfer."

Victoria looked at him.

"After she received Infant B?"

"Yes."

Alexander read the relevant paragraph.

"Then Eleanor knew Infant B was with her when she wrote this."

"That's the most likely conclusion."

Victoria looked at the incomplete name.

"Catherine."

Adrian nodded.

"Possibly."

Victoria remained quiet.

She had heard the name before, of course. Catherine Valmont was the woman who had raised one of the two girls whose identities remained uncertain.

But a name on an altered document was not evidence of anything.

"Have you spoken to the Valmonts?" she asked.

"Edward has. He asked Catherine whether she remembered anyone approaching her after the births."

"And?"

"She remembers nothing."

Victoria looked down.

For eighteen years, she had lived with the belief that her daughter had disappeared because someone had failed to protect her.

Now she was being asked to consider something more complicated.

Perhaps someone had protected a child.

Perhaps someone had protected the wrong child.

Perhaps someone had deliberately made it impossible for either mother to know what had happened.

She closed the report.

"I want the original maternal pages found."

"So do I," Adrian replied.

"Not another photocopy. Not another summary. The original pages."

Alexander nodded.

"We will pursue the legal route for Box 114-B."

Victoria looked at him.

"And if the pages identify the mothers but not the children?"

"Then we will continue."

She gave a small, tired smile.

"At least we're finally asking the right questions."

Julian was sitting on the floor of the family library.

A notebook rested on his knees.

Damien passed the doorway and stopped.

"What are you doing?"

"Planning."

"For what?"

Julian looked up as if Damien had asked an unusually foolish question.

"For my sister."

Damien glanced at the notebook.

"Still?"

"Obviously."

"How many items now?"

"Forty-four."

"You haven't added anything?"

Julian looked offended.

"I'm waiting."

"For what?"

"To find out what she likes."

Damien leaned against the doorframe.

"That's sensible."

Julian stared at him.

"Don't sound so surprised."

"I'm not."

"You sounded surprised."

Damien ignored him.

Julian looked back at the notebook.

"I think I should remove some things."

"Which things?"

"The ones that might be annoying."

"Such as?"

"Taking her to every restaurant in the city. Showing her all the family buildings. Introducing her to every employee who knows our name."

Damien considered that.

"That would take several days."

"Exactly."

"Perhaps she won't want to do all of that."

Julian frowned.

"I know."

He turned to a fresh page but did not write anything.

After a moment, he said, "What if she doesn't like us?"

Damien looked at him.

"Then we'll have to give her time."

"What if she doesn't want to come here?"

"Then we won't force her."

Julian's expression fell.

"But she's our sister."

"That doesn't mean she owes us a relationship."

Julian looked down at his notebook.

He understood the words.

He simply didn't like them.

After a while, he wrote a new sentence beneath the existing list.

**45. Let her decide what she wants to do first.**

Damien read it.

"That's a good one."

Julian closed the notebook.

"I know."

Elena had discovered that university application portals were designed by people who disliked happiness.

She stared at the screen.

"Why does it need another document?"

Nicholas, sitting on the opposite side of the library table, looked up from his phone.

"What document?"

"A statement of academic interest."

"Didn't you already submit one?"

"Yes. Apparently, this one is different."

"How different?"

Elena turned the laptop toward him.

Nicholas read the instructions.

Then he looked at her.

"Write about what you want to study."

"I know."

"Then what's the problem?"

"The previous statement already explains what I want to study."

"Maybe they want you to explain it again."

"That's not an explanation. That's repetition."

Nicholas laughed.

Elena closed the instructions.

"I'll do it later."

"You've been saying that for twenty minutes."

"I've been thinking."

"About what?"

"Whether I should apply to the Singapore programme at all."

Nicholas stopped smiling.

"Why?"

Elena looked at the garden outside the library windows.

The Valmont Estate was still familiar.

The paths, the trees, the rooms, the people who had raised her.

Nothing about the physical house had changed.

But the way she experienced it had.

She was now aware of every conversation that stopped when she entered a room, every document that carried her name, every decision that had been made before she knew there was a decision to make.

"I want to go," she said eventually. "But I don't want to go just because leaving feels easier."

Nicholas put his phone down.

"Then don't."

She looked at him.

"That's your advice?"

"Yes."

"Very helpful."

"I'm serious. If you want to go because the programme is good, go. If you want to stay because there's something here you want to do, stay. But don't let the situation make the decision for you."

Elena studied him.

"Since when did you become sensible?"

"I've always been sensible."

"No."

"Occasionally sensible, then."

She smiled.

Nicholas leaned back.

"And for the record, I don't think you need to stay here to prove anything."

Elena's smile faded slightly.

"I know."

"Do you?"

She looked down at her hands.

"I'm trying to."

Nicholas did not push her.

Instead, he pointed at the laptop.

"Now finish the application before the university invents another document."

Elena laughed.

"That's the plan."

Isabelle was in Catherine's foundation office when Elena came downstairs.

A large board had been placed against the wall, covered with proposed scholarship categories and estimated annual expenses.

Isabelle stood beside it with a folder.

"Do you have a minute?" she asked.

Elena glanced at the board.

"Is this the scholarship programme?"

"Yes. I'm trying to make the selection process less dependent on personal recommendations."

"How?"

Isabelle handed her the folder.

"I want applicants to be evaluated using a combination of academic performance, financial circumstances, and a written explanation of what they intend to do with the opportunity."

Elena read the first page.

"Have you considered students who have strong academic results but interrupted education?"

"I have."

"And students who can't provide every document?"

Isabelle paused.

"I hadn't thought about that."

"Some families don't keep records properly. Some students move between schools. If you make every document compulsory, you might exclude people who need the scholarship most."

Isabelle took the folder back.

"That's a good point."

Elena shrugged.

"It's only a suggestion."

"No, it's useful."

Isabelle wrote something in the margin.

For a few moments, neither spoke.

Then Isabelle looked at her.

"Do you think I should do this?"

"Do what?"

"Work on the foundation. Try to build something here."

Elena considered the question.

"Do you want to?"

"Yes."

"Then I think you should."

Isabelle looked down at the board.

"I don't want everyone to think I'm doing it because I'm the biological daughter."

"Then make sure you're doing something worth evaluating."

Isabelle looked up.

Elena continued, "You don't have to pretend your family doesn't matter. But you don't have to rely on the family name either."

Isabelle was quiet.

Then she nodded.

"I'll remember that."

Elena glanced at the board again.

"Also, you should include a section for vocational programmes."

"Vocational?"

"Not everyone wants university. Some people need technical training, apprenticeships, or professional certification."

Isabelle immediately wrote it down.

"You're good at this."

"I'm good at pointing out problems."

"That is often the same thing."

Elena smiled.

For the first time that afternoon, Isabelle looked genuinely pleased with the work in front of her.

At Vale International Legal Group, Jonathan Pierce placed the forensic report beside Edward's notes.

"There is another possibility," he said.

Edward looked up.

"What?"

"The partial name may not be a person's name."

Edward frowned.

"Then what is it?"

"It could be the beginning of a phrase. The examiner cannot rule out a longer word because the remaining impression is too incomplete."

"Such as?"

"Cathe... Cathedral. Catherine. Cather... It could be anything."

Edward looked unimpressed.

"That's not helpful."

"It is, actually. It means we shouldn't approach the Valmonts with an accusation based on five letters."

"I wasn't planning to."

Jonathan opened another folder.

"I've also located the reference to the tenth-day envelope."

Edward sat forward.

"The one Charles took?"

"Yes. The hospital correspondence index lists it as received by Vale Private Counsel on the eleventh day."

"Was it opened?"

"The index says it was reviewed by Eleanor Vale."

"Do we have the envelope?"

"No. But we have a later entry."

Jonathan turned the page.

A short handwritten note had been recorded in the index.

**Contents reviewed. Instruction conflicts with ninth-day arrangement. Do not proceed until receiving parties are verified.**

Edward read it twice.

"Who wrote this?"

"Unknown. The handwriting doesn't match Eleanor's, Charles's, or Morelli's."

"Can we identify it?"

"We're trying."

Edward tapped the page.

"An instruction arrived after Eleanor received Infant B. She reviewed it, found it conflicted with the ninth-day arrangement, and stopped something."

"That's one interpretation."

"What else?"

"She may have been told to return the child. She may have been told to transfer the child to another guardian. She may have been given an instruction concerning the other infant."

Edward looked at him.

"Which is why we need the envelope."

"Exactly."

Edward stood and walked to the window.

For eighteen years, the children had lived under identities created by records that might never have reflected the truth.

One infant had been taken to Margaret Ellison.

Another had been received by Eleanor Vale.

At least one had been moved again.

The mothers had been given incomplete or false explanations.

And somewhere between the hospital and the private legal office, someone had decided that neither family should know where the children were.

"Find out who sent the eleventh-day instruction," Edward said.

Jonathan nodded.

"And Morelli?"

"Ask him whether he remembers receiving an envelope after the tenth-day transfer."

"He may refuse."

"Then ask him why."

That evening, Edward returned to the Valmont Estate with a copy of the new report.

He found his father in the study.

Richard was reviewing a business proposal, but he put it aside when Edward entered.

"What is it?"

"The eleventh-day instruction."

Richard read the short entry.

His expression became colder.

"Someone attempted another transfer?"

"We don't know. But Eleanor believed the instruction conflicted with the ninth-day arrangement."

"Did she follow it?"

"Apparently not."

Richard looked at the date.

"Eleven days after the births."

"Yes."

He lowered the paper.

"How many people were involved in this?"

Edward did not answer immediately.

He thought of Martin Hale, Marcus Morelli, Charles Vale, Eleanor Vale, Martha Voss, Margaret Ellison, Dr. Whitmore, and the unknown person who had selected the receiving guardians.

Too many names.

Too many incomplete records.

Too many people who had known part of the truth.

"I don't know," he said.

Richard looked toward the dark window.

"Find out."

Edward nodded.

As he turned to leave, Richard spoke again.

"And Edward?"

He stopped.

"Don't let this investigation turn into a reason to treat either girl as evidence."

Edward looked back.

"I won't."

"Isabelle and Elena are both young women. Whatever happened eighteen years ago, neither of them chose it."

"I know."

Richard returned to his papers.

Edward left the study.

For the first time that evening, he thought about Elena not as a name in a document, or a possible identity in an old report, but as his younger sister.

A girl who was still applying to universities.

A girl who still laughed with Nicholas.

A girl who had spent eighteen years believing that the people around her were simply her family.

Whatever the documents eventually proved, she deserved the truth.

Not another arrangement.

Not another carefully prepared explanation.

The truth.

At the Vale archive, a clerk was reviewing the old correspondence index when she noticed an entry that had been overlooked.

It was not filed under the children's names.

It was not filed under the hospital.

It was listed beneath the private correspondence of Charles Vale.

**ELEVENTH DAY — RETURN INSTRUCTION.**

The envelope had been transferred to a separate storage collection before the archive's reorganisation.

The clerk opened the digital index.

The sender's name was partially preserved.

Only the surname remained visible.

**MORELLI.**

Beneath it was a short description:

*Instruction concerning the return of Infant B to the original receiving representative.*

The clerk stared at the screen.

Then she opened the next line.

**RETURN NOT COMPLETED.**

And beneath that, in a different hand:

*The receiving representative has already been replaced.*

She printed the record and placed it in the restricted correspondence tray.

The investigation had been looking for the person who selected the children’s receiving guardians.

But on the eleventh day, someone had apparently changed one of those guardians again.

And the person who was supposed to receive Infant B had never received her.
