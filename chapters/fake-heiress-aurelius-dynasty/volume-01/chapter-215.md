# **Chapter 215 — The Appointment That Had Two Different Purposes**

The Meridian archive acknowledged the request at 8:26 a.m.

Edward had expected a routine response.

Instead, the acknowledgement contained a warning.

**The appointment file FAW/06 is held under restricted governance preservation. Access to the original appointment instrument requires confirmation that the requesting investigation concerns the scope of the historical witness function and does not seek disclosure of protected family identities.**

Edward read it aloud.

Eleanor folded her arms.

“They are asking us to prove we aren't asking for something we haven't asked for.”

“They are asking us to repeat the boundary.”

“Which we have already done.”

“Then repeating it should be easy.”

He drafted a clarification.

They wanted to establish:

- Who held the Family Authority Witness appointment.
- When the appointment began and ended.
- Which documents the witness was authorised to certify.
- Whether the appointment included any authority over maternity, neonatal, receiving, or placement records.
- Whether the appointment had been altered, renewed, or transferred.

They did not request the identity of the protected recipient or the originating family.

They did not request access to unrelated personal records.

Eleanor reviewed the final paragraph.

“Add that we don't need the witness's personal correspondence.”

Edward did.

Then he submitted it.

At Meridian, the request reached a governance archivist named Helen Ward.

She had been working with restricted appointment files for eleven years and had developed a dislike of vague requests.

She disliked them even more when they involved old disputes.

FAW/06 was not a large file.

It was a thin folder with a blue preservation label and a transfer record attached to the inside cover.

She checked the access conditions.

The file had been preserved because its appointment had been cited in more than one historical administrative process.

That did not mean those processes were related.

It only meant the appointment had been used as a reference.

Helen opened the index.

The first page contained the appointment heading.

**Family Authority Witness — Temporary Delegation**

**Appointment reference:** FAW/06

**Issuing institution:** St Catherine's Maternity and Records Hospital

**Effective period:** Day 24 to Day 38

**Purpose:** Witnessing execution of declarations submitted by recognised family authorities.

Below it was a separate paragraph.

**The appointment does not confer authority to certify biological identity, amend clinical records, establish receiving identity, approve transfer, accept placement, or make decisions on behalf of any protected individual.**

Helen stopped.

The wording was familiar.

It was almost identical to the limitations Dr. Cole had identified in the F-02 authority instrument.

She continued.

The appointment had been issued to a temporary liaison officer.

The person's name was present on the original page.

The copy in the index, however, had a correction strip covering part of the entry.

Helen checked the transfer record.

The original file had been moved to restricted governance preservation on Day 47.

The reason was listed as:

**Appointment scope review following disputed use of witness designation.**

Helen frowned.

That was not a routine preservation note.

She turned to the next page.

A later administrative summary described the witness as having been appointed to verify family-originating documentation.

But the original appointment did not use that language.

It authorised the witness to observe the execution of a declaration.

It did not authorise the witness to verify the truth of the declaration's contents.

Helen placed the two documents side by side.

Someone had broadened the wording.

Not dramatically.

Just enough to change what the appointment appeared to mean.

She checked the revision history.

The summary had been created on Day 47.

The original appointment had not been amended.

Only the description had changed.

Helen picked up the telephone.

Edward received the call at 10:14.

“Mr. Valmont?”

“Yes.”

“My name is Helen Ward. I'm calling from Meridian's restricted governance archive.”

“Thank you for responding.”

“I have located FAW/06. There is a discrepancy between the original appointment and a later summary.”

Edward sat straighter.

“What kind of discrepancy?”

“The original appointment authorised the officer to witness the execution of family authority declarations. The later summary described the officer as verifying family-originating documentation.”

“Was the original appointment amended?”

“No.”

“Was the officer given additional authority?”

“I have found no renewal or extension instrument.”

“Then the summary was inaccurate.”

“I would say it was broader than the underlying authority supports.”

Eleanor began taking notes.

Edward asked, “Can you confirm the officer's identity?”

“Not yet. The personal appointment page is subject to a separate disclosure restriction. I can, however, provide the appointment's scope and the discrepancy.”

“That would be useful.”

“There is another matter.”

Edward waited.

“The appointment was reviewed on Day 47 because the witness designation had been used in a later administrative process. The review concluded that the original scope could not support the additional functions attributed to it.”

“Was that conclusion acted upon?”

“The appointment had already expired on Day 38.”

“So the review was retrospective.”

“Yes.”

Edward glanced at Eleanor.

“Was the witness's signature on F-02/SOURCE/01 challenged?”

“No. The signature was not found to be false. The concern was the use of the appointment description.”

Edward leaned back.

That was important.

The witness had genuinely signed the declaration.

The appointment had genuinely existed.

But someone had later described the witness's authority more broadly than it had been granted.

“Can you provide the Day-47 review?”

“I can provide the scope findings and the revision history. The personal appointment page will require separate approval.”

“Please send the releasable material.”

“I will.”

The call ended.

Eleanor looked at him.

“So the witness was legitimate.”

“The witness was authorised to witness the declaration.”

“But not to verify the family's claims.”

“Correct.”

“And someone later used the witness's title to make the declaration look more thoroughly verified than it was.”

Edward nodded.

“Which may explain why the distinction disappeared in later summaries.”

He looked again at the Day-47 date.

It was becoming familiar.

Too many unrelated records had acquired new descriptions on that day.

Not necessarily because the same person had changed them.

Not necessarily because they belonged to the same scheme.

But the same administrative habit kept appearing.

A limited action was completed.

Then a later summary described it as something larger.

At noon, Elena received an unexpected invitation.

It came from the Singapore programme coordinator.

The proposed second-stage pilot had been approved in principle.

The coordinator wanted Elena to attend a meeting with two programme administrators and an independent reviewer.

The meeting would cover the practical use of her platform with a small group of genuine applications.

Elena read the message twice.

Then she called Clara.

“They want to use it.”

“Your platform?”

“Yes.”

“That's good!”

“It is good. But they want to use real applications.”

Clara paused.

“Isn't that what you wanted?”

“I wanted the system tested. I didn't want someone to misunderstand what the system is allowed to do.”

“Then explain it.”

“I will.”

“What's the problem?”

Elena looked at the invitation.

The pilot would involve real applicants, real documents, and real decisions made by people who had institutional authority.

It was no longer a classroom demonstration.

The platform would have to protect people who had no reason to understand its internal design.

“The problem,” Elena said, “is that if something goes wrong, I don't want to discover that everyone assumed someone else was responsible.”

Clara was quiet for a moment.

“Then make them write down who is responsible.”

Elena smiled.

“You sound like my technical adviser.”

“I sound like someone who has listened to you complain about missing permissions for three weeks.”

“That too.”

Clara laughed.

Elena accepted the meeting invitation.

But she added a condition.

Before the pilot began, the programme needed to provide a written authority map identifying who could receive documents, authenticate them, assess them, approve decisions, and confirm delivery of any awarded support.

She did not want the software deciding those things.

She wanted the institution to decide them clearly.

At 2:07 p.m., the Meridian documents arrived.

Eleanor opened the Day-47 review first.

It was four pages long.

The review had been initiated after an administrative summary cited FAW/06 as evidence that a family declaration had been independently verified.

The reviewer had compared the summary with the original appointment.

The conclusion was direct.

**The appointment authorised witnessing of execution only. It did not authorise verification of the declaration's factual contents.**

Another paragraph stated:

**The witness's signature may establish that the declaration was executed in the witness's presence. It cannot establish the biological identity of the protected recipient, the accuracy of the originating family's claim, or the authority of any receiving institution.**

Edward read the final finding.

**No evidence has been located that the witness was authorised to act as an identity examiner, receiving representative, placement officer, or personal communication custodian.**

Eleanor put down the page.

“That closes several possibilities.”

“It closes several claims,” Edward corrected.

She looked at him.

“We still don't know what the witness knew.”

“Exactly.”

He opened the revision history.

The original appointment description had been copied into a Day-47 administrative summary.

The phrase *witnessing execution* had been replaced with *verifying family-originating documentation*.

The change was attributed to a shared governance terminal.

No individual author was identified.

A separate note stated that the description had been used in a later correspondence review.

Edward searched the file.

The correspondence reference was visible.

**F-02/STATUS/33.**

He opened the preserved copy.

It was the update sent to the originating family representative on Day 33.

The message said that the personal communication request remained pending because the direct delivery channel had not been confirmed.

It did not say that the receiving institution had refused the assignment.

It did not say that the proposed channel had never received the packet.

And it did not say that the protected recipient had never been reached.

The wording was technically accurate.

But incomplete.

Edward looked at Eleanor.

“The family was told the channel was pending.”

“Yes.”

“They were not told that Northbridge had never accepted the assignment.”

“Or that it had never received the packet.”

“Which means the originating family may have believed the communication process was still being arranged.”

Eleanor returned to the Day-47 review.

“Can we prove they continued waiting?”

“No.”

“Can we prove they were never informed?”

“Not yet.”

Edward closed the correspondence.

“But we can establish that the information given to them was insufficient to explain the failure.”

At 4:30, the Aurelius family held its weekly search meeting.

Alexander listened as Adrian reviewed the latest preservation updates.

No new biological evidence had been found.

No infant identity had been confirmed.

No reliable link had been established between the separate maternity and receiving records.

Victoria sat beside him, reading the written summary.

Julian was unusually quiet.

Lucien noticed.

“You've been staring at that notebook for ten minutes.”

Julian closed it.

“I'm thinking.”

“Dangerous.”

“I'm serious.”

Damien looked up from the table.

“About what?”

Julian hesitated.

“About whether we're doing the same thing as the people we're investigating.”

Alexander turned toward him.

“Explain.”

Julian looked at the family.

“We keep looking for someone. We keep asking where she went, who moved her, what happened to her records.”

He opened his notebook.

“But we don't know what she wants.”

Victoria's expression softened.

“We don't know who she is yet.”

“I know.”

Julian turned to the last page.

“That's why I wrote this.”

He showed them the sentence.

**Ask her what she wants to do, instead of deciding everything myself.**

Nobody laughed.

Adrian leaned back.

“That's a reasonable principle.”

“It shouldn't be a special principle,” Julian replied.

Alexander looked at the notebook for a long moment.

Then he nodded.

“It shouldn't.”

The meeting continued.

The search would proceed through authorised channels.

No one would approach Elena or anyone else based on an unverified theory.

No one would claim a relationship before the evidence supported it.

And if the missing person was eventually identified, the family would have to accept that finding her did not automatically give them the right to decide her future.

Julian wrote something beneath item fifty.

**Remember this when you get excited.**

That evening, Edward received a final message from Helen Ward.

The personal appointment page for FAW/06 had been located.

Disclosure approval had not yet been granted.

However, Helen had been authorised to confirm one additional detail.

The witness appointment had been issued to a person who already held a separate temporary administrative role at St Catherine's.

The two appointments were distinct.

The administrative role involved records coordination.

The Family Authority Witness appointment involved witnessing family declarations.

The first role did not automatically grant the second authority.

Nor did the second grant the first.

Edward read the message.

Then another line appeared.

**The original appointment contains a handwritten restriction added before execution. The restriction is not present in the later administrative summary.**

He opened the attached image.

The handwriting was clear.

**Witnessing authority must not be used to authenticate any infant, maternity, neonatal, or receiving identity record.**

Edward stared at it.

The restriction had been written before the appointment was signed.

The person who issued FAW/06 had anticipated the possibility that the witness's role might be misused.

And the later summary had omitted the restriction entirely.

Eleanor came to stand beside him.

“Does it identify who wrote the note?”

“Not in this copy.”

“Can we request that?”

“Yes. But first I want the complete appointment page.”

He enlarged the image.

At the bottom, beside the handwritten restriction, was a small initial.

Not a full signature.

Just two letters.

**E.S.**

Edward looked at Eleanor.

“Do you recognise them?”

She studied the page.

“Not from this alone.”

He saved the image to the investigation file.

The initials could belong to the person who issued the appointment, the person who reviewed its wording, or someone who added the restriction before execution.

They were not enough to identify anyone.

But the note established something important.

The boundary had existed from the beginning.

The witness was never authorised to verify infant identity.

The later administrative summary had not merely misunderstood an ambiguous appointment.

It had omitted a restriction that had been written into the original instrument.

Edward closed the file.

“Tomorrow,” he said, “we find out who wrote those initials.”

And this time, the question was not simply who had been given authority.

It was who had tried to prevent that authority from being misused.
