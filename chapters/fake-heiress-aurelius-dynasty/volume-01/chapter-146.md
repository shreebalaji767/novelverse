# **Chapter 146 — The Copy That Had No Visible Signature**

Edward placed the day-181 intake report beside the photograph of the attached acknowledgement.

He had spent most of the morning examining the two documents.

The intake report was ordinary.

The attachment was not.

It had been described in the records as:

*Copy of original acknowledgement — signature not visible.*

That single description had survived three separate transfers without being expanded.

No sender had been identified.

No recipient had been named.

And no one had explained why a copy of an acknowledgement, supposedly connected to a protected personal communication, had been included with a request asking whether the principal could withdraw from a proposed arrangement.

Edward looked at Jonathan.

"Do we know whether the attachment arrived with the original letter?"

"Not conclusively."

"Who recorded it?"

"Cross Administrative Services. The intake inventory lists one written request and one supporting document. The receiving clerk didn't describe the supporting document beyond its classification."

"Was it scanned?"

"Yes. But the scan was created after the envelope was opened."

Jonathan placed a second sheet on the table.

"The electronic record shows the attachment as page two. The request itself is page one."

"That doesn't tell us whether page two was physically enclosed."

"No. It only tells us what the receiving office recorded."

Edward leaned back.

The distinction mattered.

A document could arrive with a letter. It could be attached later by an intermediary. It could be added during scanning. It could even be a page already present in an administrative file that someone had associated with the request.

Until they established the chain of custody, the attachment could not safely be treated as evidence of what the sender knew.

"Get the original intake envelope," Edward said. "And the first inventory, not the revised electronic record."

Jonathan nodded.

"Meridian has both."

"Then we'll start there."

The original envelope was stored in a separate evidence sleeve at Meridian Records and Preservation Services.

Its condition had been photographed twice since receipt.

The first examination had taken place when the day-181 request was transferred into restricted custody. The second had occurred after Edward requested preservation of every physical component.

Dr Mira Sen examined the envelope beneath a desk lamp.

She did not begin with the handwriting.

Instead, she inspected the paper.

"One sealed flap," she said. "No evidence of a second opening. The adhesive is consistent across the full length."

"Can we establish whether anything was added after the envelope was opened?" Edward asked.

"Not from the envelope alone."

She turned it over.

"There is a small indentation near the lower corner. It may have been caused by a folded sheet inside. I would like to compare it with the attachment."

The attachment had been preserved separately.

Dr Sen placed it beside the envelope, then carefully aligned the edges.

The paper was slightly narrower than the request itself.

It had also been folded into thirds.

"The fold pattern corresponds," she said.

"So it was inside?"

"Possibly. The alignment is consistent with the attachment having been folded and enclosed. But the fold alone is not enough. A similar-sized sheet could have been placed inside at a later stage."

She examined the surface.

"The request and attachment are different paper stocks."

"Does that matter?"

"It tells us they were not printed together on the same sheet or produced from the same original document batch. It does not establish when they were assembled."

Edward studied the copy.

The visible portion contained only the upper and lower sections of a form.

The middle had been reproduced faintly, and the signature area was almost entirely blank.

"Could the signature have been deliberately removed?"

"That is one possibility. But there are others."

Dr Sen adjusted the magnification.

"The reproduction may have been made from a document with a covered signature area. It could also have been copied from a form that was never signed. The original may have been folded in a way that obscured the lower section during copying."

"Can you distinguish between those?"

"Not yet."

She moved to the bottom edge.

"There's another issue."

Edward waited.

"The attachment appears to have been copied from a document that was itself a copy."

He looked at her.

"How can you tell?"

"The faint registration marks. They suggest a second-generation reproduction. The original page may have been scanned or photocopied before this version was produced."

"Could it be a copy of the PA-132 acknowledgement?"

"I cannot say that yet. The wording is consistent with a personal acknowledgement form, but the visible portion does not contain a complete document identifier."

She pointed to a faint line near the top.

"There is a partial reference here. It might be PA-132, but the first two characters are incomplete."

Edward examined it.

The resemblance was clear.

The proof was not.

"Can you compare it with the records held at Meridian?"

"Yes. But I will need the original control materials and every known reproduction, not merely the copies selected for the investigation."

Edward looked at Jonathan.

"Request the full reproduction history."

The following afternoon, Meridian produced a collection of archived copies.

They included:

- The day-132 PA-132 custody acknowledgement form.
- A preservation copy retained by Cross Personal Records Trust.
- A restricted intake reproduction from day 136.
- Two incomplete administrative extracts created during the subsequent records migration.

None was the sealed original acknowledgement package.

Dr Sen examined each item individually.

The day-132 custody form was the first to be eliminated as the direct source.

Its layout was similar, but the printed fields differed.

The form had two sections.

Section A concerned the communication representative.

Section B concerned the protected principal.

Both remained blank.

"The attachment is not a reproduction of this particular form," Dr Sen said.

"Could it be another version?"

"Yes. The wording and layout suggest the same form family, but this isn't the same page."

She moved to the preservation copy.

Its lower margin contained a small reference line.

The attachment showed a similar mark, although the characters were incomplete.

"That is more interesting."

Edward leaned forward.

"Why?"

"The preservation copy was produced before the day-132 withdrawal. Its printing characteristics are compatible with the attachment."

"Does that mean the attachment came from it?"

"No. It means the preservation copy is a plausible source. We still need a more specific correspondence."

She compared the two under magnification.

After several minutes, she placed a transparent overlay across the attachment.

A faint line appeared to match.

Then another.

Finally, a small printing defect near the right margin aligned with the same defect on the preservation copy.

Dr Sen removed the overlay.

"I am reasonably confident that the attachment was reproduced from this document or from another copy produced from the same original master."

Edward looked at the preservation copy again.

Its heading read:

**PA-132 — PERSONAL ACKNOWLEDGEMENT RECORD**

Beneath it was a statement explaining that the document was to be retained separately from any representative's conditional response.

The final paragraph was partially obscured in the attachment.

Edward read the visible wording.

*The principal's personal acknowledgement shall remain distinct from any communication received through an intermediary.*

He looked at Dr Sen.

"Does the preservation copy identify the principal?"

"No."

"Does it identify who completed the original?"

"No."

"Then we still don't know whether the attachment represents a signed acknowledgement."

"Correct."

She returned the page to its sleeve.

"We have established a likely source document, not the identity of its author or the circumstances under which it was completed."

Edward considered the distinction.

The copy might have been made before the form was completed.

It might have been made after completion, with the signature area concealed.

Or it might have been produced from a separate version of the same master.

The attachment was evidence that someone possessed a reproduction of the PA-132 document family.

It was not evidence that P-04 had signed anything.

Marianne Cross received them at the administrative office the next morning.

She had already reviewed the day-181 intake record.

"I was hoping the original envelope would answer this," she said.

"It answered one question," Edward replied. "The attachment was probably enclosed with the request."

"And the document?"

"Likely reproduced from a PA-132 acknowledgement record. We cannot establish whether it was signed."

Marianne looked uncomfortable.

"Then whoever sent it may have had access to a document that was never supposed to circulate."

"Possibly."

"Could the copy have come from your sealed package?"

Edward shook his head.

"We haven't established that the sealed package contains the same form."

"Could it have come from the preservation copy?"

"That is more plausible. But we don't yet know who had access to that copy."

Marianne opened a file.

"The preservation copy was made for the Cross Personal Records Trust. One was retained at Meridian. Another was entered into the restricted correspondence archive."

"Who could access that archive?"

"At the time, the authorised list included myself, the preservation officer, and the records supervisor."

"Was there a visitor record?"

"Yes."

She placed a printed register on the table.

Edward read through it.

The entries were sparse.

Most recorded routine preservation checks. Several involved document transfers between Meridian and Cross Administrative Services.

One entry caught his attention.

Day 149.

**Restricted correspondence reproduction reviewed.**

The authorisation field was blank.

The operator field contained a code:

**CR-02.**

Edward looked up.

"What does that code mean?"

"Cross Records, second-level access."

"Who held it?"

"At the time, two people."

"Names?"

Marianne hesitated.

"One was me. The other was a temporary records officer."

"Who?"

"Daniel Price."

Edward recognised the name.

Price had previously helped preserve the C-19 correspondence records. He had also recorded that the sender had not received the original procedure and that further requests for acknowledgement might be premature.

"Did Price have authority to release copies?"

"No. He could retrieve and inspect restricted material. He could prepare a reproduction request, but the request required my approval before anything left the archive."

"Did you approve one on day 149?"

Marianne checked the register.

"No."

"Did Price?"

"He couldn't approve his own request."

Edward looked at the entry again.

"Then what does reviewed mean?"

Marianne turned to the next page.

A handwritten note appeared beneath the access record.

*Comparison requested against original personal acknowledgement.*

No recipient.

No reason.

No approval signature.

"That wasn't part of the ordinary preservation procedure," she said.

"Who wrote it?"

"I don't recognise the handwriting."

"Could it be Daniel's?"

"I don't know."

Marianne closed the file.

"If he accessed the preservation copy, he may have been trying to determine whether the principal had acknowledged something."

"Or whether a copy existed that could be used to support an acknowledgement."

"Those are different purposes."

"Yes."

Edward looked at the day-149 entry.

The timing was difficult to ignore.

The second request for the original procedure had been received on day 149.

The restricted acknowledgement copy had also been reviewed on day 149.

That did not prove the two events were connected.

But it made coincidence an increasingly inadequate explanation.

Daniel Price agreed to meet them at Meridian.

He arrived carrying a thin folder and immediately asked whether the investigation had identified the sender at C-19.

"No," Edward said.

"Then you still don't know whether the requests came from the principal."

"No."

Price nodded slowly.

"That matters."

"It does. We are here because you accessed the restricted acknowledgement record on day 149."

Price looked at Marianne.

She did not speak.

He opened his folder.

"I remember the review."

"Why did you request it?"

"Because the day-149 correspondence asked for the original procedure again. I wanted to establish what document the sender was referring to."

"Did you find the PA-132 record?"

"I found a preservation reproduction."

"Did you make another copy?"

Price paused.

"I prepared one."

"Where is it?"

"It remained in the restricted archive."

"Did you send it to C-19?"

"No."

"Did you attach it to the day-149 request?"

"No."

Edward watched him carefully.

"Did you provide it to anyone?"

Price looked down at the folder.

"Not directly."

"What does that mean?"

"I was asked to confirm whether the acknowledgement record contained a completed principal section."

"Who asked?"

"An administrative request came through the personal custody division."

"Signed?"

"No."

"Authorised?"

"It carried a reference to PA-132."

"That wasn't my question."

Price looked uncomfortable.

"No. It did not contain a verified approval."

Marianne folded her arms.

"Why did you process it?"

"I didn't release anything. I inspected the record and prepared an internal note."

"Did the note say the principal had acknowledged the arrangement?"

"No."

"What did it say?"

Price removed a sheet.

Edward read it.

*The available preservation reproduction does not establish completion of the principal acknowledgement section. Original remains under restricted custody. No conclusion regarding personal consent or refusal can be drawn.*

Edward placed the note on the table.

"This is important. Why wasn't it included in the day-149 file?"

"It was retained in the access record."

"It wasn't distributed?"

"No."

"Who received the result?"

Price hesitated.

"The request came from Personal Custody. I was instructed to return the note to the restricted correspondence division."

"By whom?"

"The routing instruction was already attached."

Edward examined the page.

The bottom contained a short reference:

**V-17 / PERSONAL CUSTODY — PR-06 ACTIVE**

The same status that had prevented the original procedure from being released.

Edward felt the pieces shifting, but not yet connecting.

Someone had requested an acknowledgement review.

The review had found no evidence of a completed principal acknowledgement.

The result had remained restricted.

And the request for the original procedure had continued.

"Did you see the day-181 attachment?" Edward asked.

Price nodded.

"I saw the scan."

"Did it resemble the preservation copy you inspected?"

"Yes."

"Could you identify it as the same reproduction?"

"No. I would need to compare the original file."

"Do you still have it?"

Price opened his folder.

"I kept the internal note. I did not retain a separate copy of the acknowledgement."

"Was that permitted?"

"Yes. The note was part of the access record."

Edward looked at Marianne.

"Then we need the original day-149 access file, including the routing instruction and any electronic reproduction history."

Marianne nodded.

"I'll arrange it."

Price remained seated.

"There is something else."

Edward turned back.

"The request did not ask me to determine whether the principal had signed."

"What did it ask?"

"Whether the document could be treated as an acknowledgement for administrative purposes."

Edward's expression hardened.

"And your answer?"

"That the available copy did not establish completion."

"Did anyone challenge that?"

Price shook his head.

"No. They simply didn't use my note."

That evening, Edward returned to the Valmont Estate.

Jonathan had already arranged the day-149 access record chronologically.

The request had entered Personal Custody at 09:14.

Price's inspection had been recorded at 11:32.

His note had been returned at 13:06.

At 16:40, the original procedure release request had been marked:

**HELD — PR-06 REMAINS ACTIVE.**

No explanation was attached.

No new review date had been entered.

Edward read the sequence twice.

The record did not show that Price's findings had caused the release request to be held.

The release request was already blocked by the standing instruction.

But the review had established something useful to whoever wanted to understand the acknowledgement.

The available reproduction did not prove that P-04 had personally responded.

And someone had decided that this finding should remain inside Personal Custody.

"Could the day-181 sender have obtained the copy through this review?" Jonathan asked.

"Possibly. But Price says he did not release it."

"Someone else could have."

"Yes."

Jonathan placed another document beside the register.

It was a short transfer record from day 150.

**Restricted correspondence materials — internal review completed.**

The destination field read:

**V-17 / WEST CORRIDOR — PRIVATE INSTRUCTION CUSTODY.**

Edward frowned.

"Why was an acknowledgement reproduction transferred to the west corridor?"

"That's what I wanted to know."

The transfer had been authorised under the same general preservation arrangement used for Private Instruction 07.

But the contents field did not name the document.

It only said:

*Supporting material relating to principal communication status.*

Edward looked at the date.

Day 150.

One day after the second C-19 request.

The transfer receipt contained two signatures.

The first belonged to the Meridian records officer.

The second was obscured by a rectangular redaction.

A handwritten note had been added beneath it.

*Do not attach to the representative's conditional response. Retain separately until original procedure is delivered.*

Edward recognised the instruction.

It echoed the original restrictions surrounding PA-132.

The acknowledgement was not to be merged with Gabriel's conditional response.

It was not to be treated as representative consent.

And it was not to be released while the original procedure remained undelivered.

But why had someone moved the reproduction to V-17?

He studied the redacted signature.

"Can we recover this?"

"Dr Sen can examine the original receipt," Jonathan replied. "The redaction is only on the archive copy."

"Then request the original."

Jonathan hesitated.

"There's a problem."

"What?"

"The original receipt was not retained with the transfer record."

Edward looked at him.

"Where is it?"

"According to the inventory, it was returned to the person who delivered the material."

"Who delivered it?"

"The field is blank."

Edward stared at the page.

Another transfer.

Another missing identity.

Another record that documented an action without identifying the person responsible.

He closed the folder.

"Find out whether the west-corridor custody register records the person who received the package."

Jonathan nodded.

Across the world, Elena was reviewing the latest test version of her grant-management platform.

The programme coordinator had sent her a short evaluation.

The interface was still unfinished, but the central workflow was functioning.

Applications could be submitted, documents tracked, eligibility checked, and reviewer assignments recorded without silently converting missing information into rejection.

She read the evaluation again.

*The distinction between incomplete and ineligible applications is particularly useful. It prevents administrative gaps from being mistaken for a substantive decision.*

Elena smiled.

It was a small comment.

But it was exactly the problem she had wanted the system to solve.

Clara appeared on her laptop screen a few minutes later.

"You're smiling at a spreadsheet."

"It's not a spreadsheet."

"It has rows and columns."

"It's a prototype."

"That is a spreadsheet with ambition."

Elena laughed.

"You're impossible."

Clara leaned closer to her camera.

"How is the Singapore programme?"

"Busy. Interesting. Expensive."

"Expensive?"

"Not the tuition. Everything around it. Transport, meals, supplies, printing, replacement chargers—"

"Replacement chargers?"

"I've already lost one."

"How?"

"I put it somewhere safe."

"That sounds like a very dangerous place."

Elena shook her head.

They spent the next few minutes discussing the prototype. Clara suggested a clearer way to display pending applications, and Elena made a note to test it.

Before ending the call, Clara asked, "Are you going to let the foundation test it?"

"Eventually. Isabelle wants to use anonymised old applications."

"That sounds useful."

"It could be. But I don't want to give them something unreliable just because they know me."

"Then don't."

Elena looked at the screen.

"You're making that sound easy."

"It is easy. You finish the work, test it properly, and let them judge the result."

Elena smiled again.

"That's what I'm trying to do."

After the call, she returned to her prototype.

A pending decision should remain pending until someone with the proper authority made it.

It was a simple principle.

She had not yet learned how many people could spend eighteen years finding ways around it.

At the Valmont Estate, Edward received a message from Dr Sen shortly before midnight.

She had examined the original day-150 transfer receipt.

The rectangular redaction on the archive copy had concealed a handwritten recipient designation, not a signature.

The original read:

**RECEIVED UNDER PERSONAL REPRESENTATION CUSTODY — A.M.**

Below it, in a different hand, was a second line.

**Authority withdrawn. Custody receipt remains valid for preservation only.**

Edward read the message twice.

A.M.

The same initials that appeared on the day-135 Route 06 courier receipt.

The same initials associated with Anna Mercer-Smith's administrative correspondence.

But the receipt did not establish that Anna had received the acknowledgement reproduction.

It established only that someone identified by those initials had accepted custody of the material under a limited preservation arrangement.

And the second line introduced another problem.

The authority had been withdrawn.

Yet the custody receipt remained valid.

Edward opened the day-132 appointment register.

The proposed personal representation designation had been withdrawn on that date.

The day-150 transfer was eighteen days later.

Someone had continued using the old initials after the authority was withdrawn, while carefully limiting the legal meaning of the receipt.

He called Jonathan.

"Don't contact Anna yet."

"Why?"

"Because we don't know whether A.M. identifies her, someone else, or a separate administrative designation."

"What do you want me to check?"

"Find the original day-132 withdrawal schedule. Not the summary. I want the full nomination record, the identity of the proposed representative, and the person who entered the withdrawal."

Jonathan was silent for a moment.

"That schedule was restricted."

"I know."

"And if it names the same person who received the acknowledgement copy?"

"Then we'll have a connection. Not necessarily an explanation."

Edward looked once more at the day-150 receipt.

The initials were familiar.

The authority was not.

And somewhere between the withdrawn appointment and the later custody transfer, someone had decided that a person could lose the right to represent the principal while still being permitted to preserve documents concerning that principal.

The question was no longer simply who had copied the acknowledgement.

It was who had been allowed to keep it after their authority had ended.
