# **Chapter 229 — The Message from the People She Had Never Known**

The message arrived the following afternoon.

It was delivered through Laura Bennett, exactly as Elena had requested.

There was no family photograph attached.

No invitation to visit the Aurelius estate.

No elaborate explanation of how much money, influence, or effort had been spent searching for her.

Only a letter.

Laura sent a brief note with it.

*The message was written by Alexander and Victoria Aurelius jointly. It has not been edited by the legal office, except for the removal of protected third-party information. You may read it at your own pace.*

Elena opened the attachment.

**To Elena,**

We do not know whether you want to hear from us.

We understand that receiving the biological comparison result may have raised more questions than it answered.

The result confirms that we are your biological parents. We know that this does not explain your childhood, the circumstances surrounding your original identity record, or the decisions made by the people responsible for your care.

We will not pretend that we understand what this information means to you.

We have searched for the child whose identity remained unresolved in the historical records. For many years, we did not know where the search had failed or whether the records had ever reached the person they were intended to reach.

We cannot ask you to forgive us for something you have not yet had the opportunity to understand.

We cannot ask you to come home when we do not know what home means to you.

We would like the opportunity to meet you someday, if you decide that is what you want.

Until then, we will respect your boundaries.

You do not owe us a response.

**Alexander Aurelius**

**Victoria Aurelius**

Elena read the letter twice.

Then she closed it.

She had expected something more dramatic.

Perhaps a declaration that they had never stopped searching. Perhaps an explanation of how much they had suffered. Perhaps an immediate invitation to return.

Instead, they had written a letter that left the decision with her.

That made it harder to dismiss.

She opened the attachment again.

At the bottom, she noticed a handwritten sentence included in the scanned original.

*We hope you have had people who cared for you, even if we were not among them.*

Elena looked away from the screen.

She thought about the Valmont estate.

The large dining room.

The birthdays.

The school years.

The moments when Catherine had helped her with homework.

The times when Sebastian had teased her, Nicholas had complained about her borrowing his things, and Edward had quietly checked whether she had eaten.

Those memories had not become false simply because the family had not been biologically hers.

But neither had the rejection become harmless.

She closed the letter.

Then she called Laura.

“I received it.”

“Would you like to respond?”

“Not immediately.”

“That's perfectly acceptable.”

“Can I ask something?”

“Of course.”

“Did they write it themselves?”

“Yes. The legal office received the original signed letter.”

“Did they know I might not respond?”

“They were informed that you were under no obligation to do so.”

Elena paused.

“Did they ask for my address?”

“No. They were expressly told that your contact information would remain protected.”

“Good.”

She ended the call.

For the rest of the afternoon, she did not think about writing a reply.

She went to the university.

She attended her scheduled meeting.

She corrected a problem in the pilot system.

And when she returned to her apartment, she cooked dinner.

The letter remained open on her laptop.

She did not need to answer it tonight.

At the Aurelius estate, the family had gathered in the smaller sitting room.

Alexander had placed a copy of the letter on the table.

Victoria sat beside him.

Adrian stood near the fireplace, while Lucien and Damien occupied the sofa. Julian sat on the floor with his notebook.

“Has she read it?” Julian asked.

“We have confirmation that it was delivered,” Alexander replied.

“That's not the same thing.”

“No.”

“Did she answer?”

“No.”

Julian looked down at his notebook.

Lucien leaned forward.

“You know she doesn't have to answer immediately.”

“I know.”

“Then why are you checking every five minutes?”

Julian closed the notebook.

“Because I want to know if she read it.”

Victoria reached out and touched his shoulder.

“We all do.”

Adrian looked at Alexander.

“Should we prepare anything for a possible meeting?”

“Not yet.”

“We could arrange a private location.”

“We could. But we don't know whether she wants a meeting, and we should not make arrangements that create an expectation.”

Damien nodded.

“If she agrees, the location should be her choice.”

Alexander looked at the letter.

“Then we wait.”

Julian opened his notebook again.

He crossed out a line he had written earlier:

*Prepare the family room.*

Below it, he wrote:

*Don't prepare a room before she agrees to come.*

Two days passed.

Elena continued her academic work.

The pilot's first applications moved from preliminary review to the decision committee. One application was returned for a missing document. Another was accepted for consideration after an independent reviewer authenticated the supporting receipt.

Elena watched the activity log.

**Received:** 9:14 a.m.

**Format check:** Passed.

**Authentication:** Completed by assigned reviewer.

**Substantive review:** Completed.

**Committee decision:** Pending.

She smiled.

The system was doing what it was designed to do.

It was recording decisions made by authorised people rather than pretending that the software itself had authority.

Mr. Lim approached her desk.

“The committee wants to know whether the system can issue approval letters automatically.”

“It can generate a draft after the decision is recorded.”

“Can it send the letter?”

“Only after an authorised officer reviews and approves the final version.”

Mr. Lim nodded.

“Your answer is always the same.”

“Because the authority is always the same.”

He laughed.

“You should put that on the wall.”

“Please don't.”

On Friday evening, Isabelle called again.

“Can I ask how you're doing?”

“I'm fine.”

“That's not a very convincing answer.”

Elena smiled slightly.

“I'm thinking.”

“About the result?”

“About everything.”

Isabelle was quiet.

“Did the Aurelius family contact you?”

“They sent a letter through an independent representative.”

“Are you going to meet them?”

“I don't know.”

“Do you want to?”

Elena considered the question.

“I want to know what happened. I don't know whether meeting them will help me understand it.”

“That makes sense.”

“And I'm not ready to become part of another family's expectations.”

“I understand.”

Elena looked at the notebook on her desk.

“Isabelle?”

“Yes?”

“Do you remember when we first started working on the foundation review?”

“Of course.”

“You asked me whether I would ever help the Valmont family again.”

“I did.”

“I still don't know what our relationship will become. But I don't want every conversation between us to be about who belongs to which family.”

Isabelle answered quietly.

“Neither do I.”

“Then let's keep the work separate.”

“All right.”

“And please don't tell Richard or Catherine about the letter.”

“I won't.”

They ended the call.

Elena felt that something had changed.

Not dramatically.

Not completely.

But the conversation had not turned into an argument about family loyalty.

That was enough for one evening.

The following Monday, Edward received a new report from the records review team.

The report concerned the historical receiving process.

It was not a biological report.

It did not change the genetic conclusion.

But it answered one of the questions that had remained unresolved.

The original receiving identity had never been finally established.

The Day-6 amendment had changed an administrative reference.

It had not verified the infant's identity.

The later continuity summaries had progressively described the provisional reference as established.

The report identified the administrative mechanism behind the change in wording.

Margaret Voss had restricted the circulation of originating-authority objections.

Margaret Ellison had approved continuity descriptions without reviewing the original appointment restrictions.

Daniel Mercer had consolidated several distinct authorities into broad administrative descriptions and failed to correct the later wording.

None of those findings established that any one person had deliberately arranged the original infant transfer.

Edward read the final section.

**The investigation has established material failures in the preservation, communication, and description of historical identity records. It has not established the individual who made the original receiving decision or the circumstances under which the infant came to be raised by the Valmont family.**

Edward closed the report.

He called Elena.

She answered after the third ring.

“Hello?”

“I have an update on the historical investigation.”

“Does it concern the original receiving record?”

“Yes.”

“Is it important?”

“I believe so. But I don't want to send you another large collection of documents without explaining what it establishes.”

“Tell me.”

“The original receiving identity was never finally established. The hospital's proposed reference remained provisional. Later administrative summaries described it as confirmed, but those summaries were not supported by a final identity determination.”

Elena listened carefully.

“So nobody can prove who authorised my placement with the Valmonts?”

“Not yet.”

“Can you prove that the Valmont family received me through an authorised process?”

“Not from the documents currently available.”

She was silent.

Edward continued.

“We also identified how the later records became misleading. Several people made decisions that allowed provisional references to be treated as established administrative facts. But we have not established who made the original receiving decision.”

“Did anyone admit responsibility?”

“Some acknowledged their part in the administrative failures. None has established the complete chain.”

Elena looked toward the kitchen.

“So the biological result answers who my parents are. The investigation still doesn't answer how I ended up with the Valmont family.”

“Correct.”

“Thank you for being precise.”

Edward hesitated.

“There's something else.”

“What?”

“The Aurelius family has asked whether they may send a second message, but they have not requested direct contact.”

Elena thought about it.

“Tell them I received the first letter.”

“I can do that.”

“And tell them I appreciate that they respected the boundary.”

“I will.”

She paused.

“But I am not ready to meet them.”

“I'll make sure that's clear.”

That evening, Elena opened the first letter again.

She read it from beginning to end.

Then she opened a blank document.

For several minutes, she typed nothing.

Eventually, she began.

**To Alexander and Victoria Aurelius,**

I received your letter.

I have read the independent laboratory report and understand what it establishes.

I also understand that the historical investigation remains incomplete. I do not yet know how I came to be raised by the Valmont family or who was responsible for the original receiving decision.

I am not ready to meet you.

That does not mean I am refusing to learn more about the past. I would like the remaining historical questions to be investigated properly, and I would like to receive the findings through my independent representative.

I appreciate that you have not requested my contact details or attempted to approach me directly.

Please continue to respect that boundary.

I will decide whether and when I am ready for a meeting.

**Elena**

She read the message.

It was neither affectionate nor hostile.

It was honest.

She sent it to Laura with instructions to forward it without alteration.

Then she closed the laptop.

At the Aurelius estate, the reply arrived at 8:47 p.m.

Alexander opened it.

Victoria sat beside him.

He read it aloud.

Nobody interrupted.

When he finished, Victoria pressed her fingers together.

“She isn't ready.”

“No,” Alexander said.

“But she hasn't closed the door.”

“No.”

Julian looked at the letter.

“Can we answer?”

Alexander shook his head gently.

“She has already asked us to respect her boundary. We should not turn every message into another conversation.”

Julian nodded.

“Then we wait.”

Victoria looked at the final line.

*I will decide whether and when I am ready for a meeting.*

She read it again.

For years, they had searched for a child whose identity had remained hidden behind records, sealed files, and failed communication routes.

Now they knew the child had grown into a young woman with her own life, her own work, and her own decisions.

They had found her.

But finding her had not given them the right to reclaim her time.

Alexander placed the letter in the family file.

“Let her finish what she is doing,” he said.

A week later, Elena completed the pilot's first review cycle.

Five applications had moved forward.

One had been returned for additional evidence.

Every decision had a named authority, a timestamp, and an explanation.

Ms. Tan congratulated her.

“You've managed to build a system that makes people explain what they do.”

“That's the idea.”

“Will you continue developing it?”

“Yes. But I want to improve the reporting features before expanding the pilot.”

“Good. We'll discuss the next stage after the programme review.”

Elena left the meeting carrying her laptop.

Outside, the afternoon was bright.

She stopped near the campus courtyard and checked her phone.

There was a new message from Laura.

**The Aurelius family has acknowledged your response. They will not contact you directly. The historical investigation remains active.**

Elena read it.

Then she put the phone away.

She had two families connected to her past.

One had raised her and then rejected her.

The other had lost her and was waiting for permission to know her.

Neither family could decide what she would become.

That decision was hers.

She walked toward the library.

There was still work to do.

And for the first time, the future did not feel like something she had to escape from.

It felt like something she could build.
