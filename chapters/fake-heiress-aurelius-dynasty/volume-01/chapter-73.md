# **Chapter 73 — The Two Children at Westbridge**

The Westbridge archive had survived three changes of ownership, two administrative reorganisations, and one attempted liquidation.

It had not survived eighteen years of careless recordkeeping.

Edward discovered this before he had finished reading the first page of the archive inventory.

"Half the original patient files are missing," Jonathan said over the telephone. "The successor organisation has digital copies of some records, but the paper archive was moved twice. They can't guarantee that every box was transferred."

"Can they identify which boxes are missing?"

"They have a list of transferred collections. The problem is that several boxes were labelled as general medical administration rather than patient records."

"Then we look at the general administration boxes."

"I've requested access."

Edward glanced at his watch.

"How long?"

"They say a few days."

"Ask for an emergency preservation order. We have evidence of disputed identity records, unauthorised transfers, and possible falsification of medical documentation."

"Already working on it."

Edward ended the call.

On his desk lay two documents from Westbridge.

The first concerned Child 28-B.

The second concerned an unnamed female infant transferred from Meridian Children's Medical Centre.

Both entries were dated the one hundred and third day after the births.

One was connected to A-17.

The other was not.

And both involved an identification problem.

He opened his notebook.

**Question one: Were the two children physically present at Westbridge on the same day?**

**Question two: Was either child transferred out before the other arrived?**

**Question three: Why was one child's identification band replaced?**

He underlined the third question.

It was the one he liked least.

A replacement identification band was not necessarily evidence of wrongdoing. Hospitals replaced damaged bands. Residential facilities sometimes used their own temporary numbering systems.

But if a child arrived with an identification band that did not match the accompanying documentation, the discrepancy should have been recorded.

Someone had recorded it.

Someone had also failed to explain it.

The Westbridge successor organisation granted access three days later.

Its records manager, a woman named Patricia Moore, received Edward and Jonathan in a conference room.

She had arranged several archive boxes on a table.

"We have digitised the material that was considered operationally relevant," she explained. "Unfortunately, some of the original documents were not included in the digitisation project."

"Why not?" Edward asked.

"Different departments used different retention policies. Some files were classified as medical, others as residential administration. When Westbridge changed ownership, the categories were not always preserved."

"Who decided which records were retained?"

"That would have been the original management."

"Do you have the names?"

Patricia opened a folder.

"Yes. The residential director was Dr. Samuel Hale. Administrative coordination was handled by Marianne Cross for external cases. The medical liaison was Gabriel Vale."

Edward and Jonathan exchanged a glance.

"Did Dr. Hale personally supervise the children?" Edward asked.

"I cannot establish that from these records."

"Can you locate his employment file?"

"I can try."

"Please do."

Patricia opened the first archive box.

Inside were patient registers, transport acknowledgements, medical observations, and several bundles of correspondence.

She removed a thick ledger.

"This is the residential admission register."

Edward began turning the pages.

The entries were handwritten.

Some were complete. Others had missing fields or references to separate files.

He eventually found the relevant date.

**Day 103.**

There were two entries.

ENTRY ONE

**Temporary identity: 28-B**

**Sex: Female**

**Originating record: Northbridge Residential Services**

**Protected file: A-17**

**Receiving medical representative: Dr. Samuel Hale**

**Administrative representative: Marianne Cross**

ENTRY TWO

**Temporary identity: M-204**

**Sex: Female**

**Originating facility: Meridian Children's Medical Centre**

**Identification band: Replaced on arrival**

**Previous band reference: Not verified**

**Receiving medical representative: Dr. Samuel Hale**

Edward stopped.

"M-204."

Jonathan leaned closer.

"Could that be IV-204?"

"Possibly. But the prefix is different."

Patricia looked at the page.

"Would you like the supporting records?"

"Every document attached to both entries."

She searched the box and found a thin envelope marked M-204.

Inside were three pages.

The first was a transport acknowledgement.

The second was a medical observation sheet.

The third was a band replacement form.

Edward read the first page.

The child had arrived from Meridian at 9:40 a.m.

The transport had been authorised by a temporary medical custodian.

No guardian was listed.

The second page contained routine observations.

The infant had been examined, fed, and settled in the nursery.

The third page was more interesting.

**Previous band removed due to discrepancy between band reference and accompanying transfer documentation.**

**New temporary band issued: M-204.**

**Original band retained in sealed evidence envelope.**

Edward looked up.

"Where is the envelope?"

Patricia searched the archive box.

There was no evidence envelope.

"Perhaps it was stored separately."

"Is there a record of its transfer?"

"I'll check."

Edward returned to the band replacement form.

At the bottom, someone had written:

**Do not reconcile with A-17 until originating identification materials are received.**

He read it again.

Then he turned to Jonathan.

"The two entries were connected."

"Not necessarily as the same child."

"No. But someone knew they might be connected."

They spent the next two hours reconstructing the nursery records.

Westbridge had used a small residential nursery for infants requiring temporary supervision. The room contained six beds, although the admission records showed that no more than four children were present at any one time.

Child 28-B had been assigned bed three.

M-204 had been assigned bed five.

Both were admitted on day 103.

The room allocation sheet showed that the children had occupied the same nursery for at least eleven days.

Edward placed the sheet beside the admission register.

"So they were physically together."

"According to the room records," Jonathan replied.

"Do we have daily observations?"

Patricia found another folder.

There were several routine nursing notes.

Most were unremarkable.

One recorded that Child 28-B had experienced mild feeding difficulties.

Another mentioned that M-204 had been unsettled during the night.

A third described a routine medical examination.

Then Edward found a note dated day 106.

**Both temporary identities reviewed. No permanent identification authorised. Staff instructed to maintain separate observation records.**

Below it was a handwritten addition.

**Do not transfer either child until Dr. Hale completes review.**

"Why would they need to maintain separate observation records?" Jonathan asked.

"Because the identities were disputed," Edward said.

"Or because they were concerned about mixing up the two children."

Edward considered that.

It was possible.

Two infants had arrived within the same period. One had an unresolved protected identity file. The other had arrived with a disputed identification band.

Keeping separate records would have been sensible.

But the instruction to maintain separate records did not explain why someone had previously tried to connect them.

He turned the page.

The next entry was dated day 109.

**Child 28-B: medical review completed.**

**M-204: originating identification query pending.**

Then, in the margin:

**External representative has requested confirmation that neither child is to be registered under Aurelius family identity.**

Edward stared at the sentence.

"Who made the request?"

Patricia checked the reference.

"There's no name."

"Was it received by telephone or in writing?"

"That isn't specified."

"Find the correspondence register."

The correspondence register contained a clue.

On day 109, Westbridge had received a telephone instruction from Cross Administrative Services.

The caller had requested that both children remain under temporary identities.

The instruction had been recorded by a receptionist.

A separate note stated:

**Caller repeated that neither child should be identified as the Aurelius infant without written originating confirmation.**

Edward read the sentence carefully.

It was different from the earlier instruction.

The earlier instruction had said Child 28-B was not the Aurelius infant.

This one said neither child should be identified as the Aurelius infant without confirmation.

The difference mattered.

One was a conclusion.

The other was a warning against making a conclusion.

"Who called?" Jonathan asked.

The receptionist had recorded only the company name.

No individual.

"Could it have been Marianne?" Edward asked.

Patricia searched the duty log.

"Marianne Cross was listed as the administrative contact that day."

Edward made a note.

"Was Gabriel involved?"

"His name appears on a medical liaison review dated the following morning."

She retrieved the document.

It was short.

**Medical liaison review — Day 110**

**Subject: M-204 and 28-B**

**No medical basis for identity determination.**

**Identification must be resolved through originating documentation and independent verification.**

**Temporary records to remain separate.**

The signature belonged to Gabriel Vale.

Edward looked at Jonathan.

"That is the first sensible thing we've found from him."

Jonathan examined the page.

"At least he acknowledged that medical records couldn't establish biological identity."

"Yes."

Edward placed the review beside the earlier instruction.

Gabriel had not confirmed that either child was or was not the Aurelius infant.

He had explicitly stated that he lacked a medical basis for deciding.

But somebody had already told Margaret Voss, Marianne Cross, and the receiving party that Child 28-B was not the Aurelius infant.

The contradiction was becoming difficult to ignore.

Patricia returned with a second ledger.

"This is the nursery movement register."

Edward opened it.

It recorded when children were moved between rooms, taken for examinations, or transferred to external medical facilities.

The two children had remained in the same nursery until day 114.

Then Child 28-B was moved to a separate observation room.

M-204 remained in the original nursery.

On day 118, Child 28-B was transferred out.

The destination field was incomplete.

M-204 remained at Westbridge.

On day 121, M-204 was transferred to another medical facility.

The receiving institution was identified only by a code.

**HMC-4.**

Edward looked at the page.

"Where is the key for these destination codes?"

Patricia checked the back of the register.

There was a handwritten index.

HMC-4 corresponded to a private medical centre called **Harrow Medical Centre**.

Jonathan frowned.

"That's not Meridian."

"No."

"Was it connected to Northbridge?"

Patricia checked the institutional directory.

"It was an independent facility. Westbridge used it for specialist paediatric assessments."

Edward returned to the movement register.

"What happened to Child 28-B after day 118?"

"There's no destination name."

"Can we trace the transport?"

"We have a vehicle reference."

He read it.

**CS-04.**

"Cross Administrative Services again."

Patricia nodded.

"The company supplied transport coordination for Westbridge."

Edward wrote it down.

Two children had arrived at Westbridge on the same day.

They had shared a nursery.

One had been moved on day 118.

The other had been moved three days later.

Neither had been given a permanent identity.

And the original band belonging to M-204 was missing.

Edward looked at the records manager.

"Do you have the employment file for Dr. Hale?"

"I found a personnel index."

"Where is he now?"

"He retired several years ago. I have an address in the file."

Edward closed the register.

"Please give us a copy."

Dr. Samuel Hale lived in a quiet residential neighbourhood.

He was seventy-three, with silver hair and a measured manner.

When Edward explained why they had come, the retired doctor listened without interrupting.

Then he asked a question.

"Are the children alive?"

Edward had not expected that to be his first concern.

"We don't know."

Dr. Hale lowered his eyes.

"I see."

"Do you remember the Westbridge nursery?"

"Of course."

"Do you remember two female infants admitted on the same day?"

Dr. Hale was silent for several seconds.

Then he nodded.

"I remember the situation."

"Do you remember their identities?"

"No."

"Do you remember their temporary numbers?"

"One was 28-B."

"And the other?"

"M-204, I believe."

Edward leaned forward.

"Were they the same child?"

Dr. Hale looked at him.

"No."

"Are you certain?"

"I was responsible for the medical assessments. They were two different infants."

"How do you know?"

"Because I examined them separately."

He paused.

"Different physical characteristics, different medical observations, different feeding patterns. They were not the same child."

Edward absorbed the answer.

It was the first direct confirmation that 28-B and M-204 were separate children.

But it did not tell him who either child was.

"Did you know their biological identities?"

"No."

"Did you know where they came from?"

"I knew the administrative histories."

"Then tell me."

Dr. Hale folded his hands.

"Child 28-B arrived through Northbridge. The accompanying documents were incomplete. The child was healthy enough for residential observation, but the identity file was sealed."

"And M-204?"

"That child arrived from Meridian. Her identification band did not match the transfer documentation."

"Was the original band retained?"

"It should have been."

"Where is it?"

"I don't know."

Edward placed the band replacement form on the table.

"Your staff recorded that the original band was sealed."

"Then it should have been in the medical records archive."

"It isn't."

Dr. Hale looked troubled.

"That is unfortunate."

"Was it ever examined?"

"Yes."

"By whom?"

"An external records officer."

"Name?"

Dr. Hale frowned.

"I cannot remember."

"Was it Marianne Cross?"

"No."

"Gabriel Vale?"

"No."

"Who?"

Dr. Hale thought for a while.

"An older woman. She came with a representative from the hospital."

"Do you remember her appearance?"

"Grey hair. Glasses. She carried a brown leather case."

Edward wrote down the description.

"Did she tell you why she was examining the band?"

"She said the originating identification materials needed to be checked before any permanent registration."

"Did she give you a report?"

"No. She asked me to keep the child under the temporary number until the review was completed."

"Did you?"

"Yes."

Edward placed the medical liaison review before him.

"Gabriel Vale signed this."

Dr. Hale nodded.

"He was involved in the administrative coordination."

"Did he ever tell you that Child 28-B was not the Aurelius infant?"

Dr. Hale's expression changed.

"He told me that the children were not to be identified as belonging to any family without confirmation."

"Did he say that about both children?"

"Yes."

"Did anyone say that 28-B was definitely not the Aurelius infant?"

Dr. Hale looked down.

"Charles Vale."

Edward waited.

"He came to Westbridge shortly after the children arrived. He asked whether either had been identified as the Aurelius child."

"What did you tell him?"

"That I had no authority to make that determination."

"What did he say?"

"He said one of them had already been ruled out."

"Which one?"

"28-B."

Edward felt his attention sharpen.

"Did he provide evidence?"

"No."

"Did you ask why?"

"I did."

"What was his answer?"

Dr. Hale looked toward the window.

"He said the originating office had confirmed it."

"Did he show you the confirmation?"

"No."

"Did you believe him?"

"I believed that he might have received information I did not have. I did not treat his statement as a medical finding."

Edward remained quiet.

Dr. Hale continued.

"I was uncomfortable with the entire arrangement. The children were being moved through a system in which nobody seemed to possess the complete identity records."

"Why didn't you refuse to receive them?"

"Because they were infants. They needed care."

It was a simple answer.

And perhaps the most honest one Edward had heard all week.

Before they left, Dr. Hale remembered something else.

"There was a meeting on day 116," he said.

"Who attended?"

"Charles Vale, Marianne Cross, and a representative from the originating office."

"Who was the representative?"

"I don't remember her name."

"Was it a man or a woman?"

"A woman."

"Did she examine both children?"

"She asked to see their medical records. She did not handle the children."

"Did she ask about the identity bands?"

"Yes."

"Did she receive anything?"

Dr. Hale hesitated.

"The original band from M-204 was taken for examination."

Edward looked at Jonathan.

"By whom?"

"The woman from the originating office."

"Was there a receipt?"

"I believe so."

"Can you locate it?"

"I no longer have access to the old records."

"Do you remember anything written on the receipt?"

Dr. Hale closed his eyes.

"Only that it was marked as evidence."

Edward leaned forward.

"Evidence of what?"

"I don't know."

The doctor opened his eyes.

"But I remember one sentence."

"What sentence?"

"She said the band had to be preserved because it might be the only physical link between the child and her original maternity record."

Edward sat back.

The original band.

Not a photograph.

Not a photocopy.

Not a handwritten transfer note.

The actual identification band that had been removed when M-204 arrived at Westbridge.

If it still existed, it might provide the first reliable connection between a child at Westbridge and the hospital where she had been born.

"Did she take the band away?" Edward asked.

"Yes."

"Did she say where she was taking it?"

"No."

"Did anyone object?"

Dr. Hale shook his head.

"Charles Vale said the originating office had authority to examine it."

Edward looked at Jonathan.

They both understood the importance of that statement.

Someone had removed the only physical identification item that could have connected M-204 to her original maternity record.

And the person who removed it had claimed to represent the originating office.

But which office?

Meridian?

St Catherine's?

The Aurelius Family Office?

Or another organisation entirely?

On the drive back, Jonathan opened his notebook.

"We have confirmed two separate children."

"Yes."

"28-B and M-204."

"Yes."

"28-B was told to be treated as not the Aurelius infant. M-204's identity band was removed for examination."

"Correct."

"Could M-204 be Isabella's daughter?"

Edward looked out of the window.

"She was transferred from Meridian, and IV-204 was associated with Isabella's maternity record. That's a possibility."

"But not proof."

"Not proof."

"Could 28-B be Elena?"

"Also a possibility. But we have no evidence connecting her to Elena."

Jonathan closed the notebook.

"Then we're still looking at two children whose identities are unknown."

"Three, perhaps."

Jonathan turned.

"What do you mean?"

Edward opened the copy of the Westbridge movement register.

"Child 28-B left on day 118. M-204 left on day 121. But the original St Catherine's Infant A transfer still has no confirmed destination after Gabriel took her on day six."

"You're suggesting a third child."

"I'm saying we have three separate administrative trails. We have not established whether any of them eventually converged."

Jonathan was quiet.

Edward continued.

"One trail begins at St Catherine's. One begins at Isabella's maternity clinic and passes through Meridian. One begins at Northbridge under 28-B."

"And all three might be connected to the Aurelius search."

"Or only some of them."

He closed the folder.

"Until we find original identification materials, we should stop assigning family names to temporary numbers."

Jonathan nodded.

"What's next?"

Edward looked at the notes.

"The woman who collected M-204's original band."

"And the destination of 28-B."

"Both."

His phone vibrated.

A message had arrived from Patricia Moore.

She had located an additional item in the Westbridge archive.

It was a scanned receipt, previously filed under general medical administration.

Edward opened the attachment.

The receipt was dated day 116.

**Item received: Original infant identification band — M-204.**

**Condition: Intact.**

**Purpose: Independent identity verification.**

**Receiving representative: A. Holt.**

Edward stared at the name.

Adrian Holt.

The same man who had written to Margaret Voss on behalf of the Aurelius Family Office on day 31, denying Victoria's authorisation and requesting preservation of the records.

But there was a problem.

Adrian Holt had not been an investigator.

He had been the Aurelius family's legal and administrative representative.

And if he had personally collected the original band from Westbridge, then the Aurelius Family Office had possessed a physical piece of evidence connected to M-204.

For eighteen years.

Edward read the receipt again.

At the bottom, beneath the signature, was a final instruction.

**Do not return the original item to Westbridge. Retain under sealed family-office evidence custody.**

He immediately called Jonathan.

"Change our next priority."

"What happened?"

"Find Adrian Holt."

A pause.

"Is he still with the Aurelius family?"

"I don't know."

Edward looked at the receipt.

"But we need to find out what happened to the band."

He ended the call.

For the first time, the Aurelius family might possess something more valuable than a disputed register or a copied signature.

They might possess the original identification band of a child whose identity had never been confirmed.

And if Adrian Holt had kept it, he might have known far more about the missing children than anyone had yet admitted.
