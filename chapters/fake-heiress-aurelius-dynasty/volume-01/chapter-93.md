# **Chapter 93 — The Woman Who Refused to Sign**

Margaret Voss did not welcome Edward's second visit.

She welcomed him even less when he explained why he had come.

"You're asking me about an appointment that never happened," she said.

"I'm asking why your initials appear beside the originating facility's authority."

"They appear on many documents."

"Not this kind."

Margaret looked at the photocopy Edward had placed on her desk.

Her expression remained composed, but her fingers stopped moving.

**ORIGINATING FACILITY REPRESENTATIVE — PENDING.**

**M.V.**

She read it again.

"Where did you find this?"

"In the R-04 trust correspondence."

"That file should not have been released."

"It wasn't. The trustee provided the register under the authority granted to our legal team."

Margaret pushed the document back.

"I was never the receiving identity custodian."

"I didn't say you were."

"Then what do you want?"

Edward sat opposite her.

"I want to know whether you were appointed as the originating facility representative."

Margaret did not answer immediately.

Outside her office, a printer started and stopped. Someone walked past the glass partition carrying a stack of folders.

Finally, she said, "I was nominated."

Jonathan, seated beside Edward, looked up.

"By whom?"

"That is not written there?"

"No."

"Then I won't guess."

Edward waited.

Margaret sighed.

"Charles Vale submitted the nomination."

"When?"

"During the first month of the arrangement."

"Did you accept?"

"No."

"Why?"

She looked at him.

"Because I had no authority to certify the identity of a child based on documents I had not been allowed to examine."

Edward felt the significance of the answer immediately.

"You were asked to certify the identity?"

"I was asked to verify the relationship between the originating records and the receiving schedule."

"That sounds like the receiving custodian's responsibility."

"It was supposed to be a joint responsibility. The originating representative was meant to confirm that the medical record belonged to the originating child. The receiving custodian was meant to confirm that the child recorded at the receiving institution was the same child."

"Then why were you nominated?"

"Because I held the restricted hospital register."

"That made you a records custodian."

"Exactly."

Margaret folded her hands.

"It did not make me a witness to every transfer, a medical examiner, or a representative of every family mentioned in those records."

Edward studied her.

"Did Charles understand that?"

"He understood it perfectly."

Margaret brought out an old notebook.

Its cover was worn, and the pages had softened around the edges.

She opened it to a date near the beginning of the investigation.

There was a short entry in her handwriting.

*Nomination received. Authority insufficient. Originals unavailable. Refused pending independent verification.*

Edward read it.

"Did you keep this at the time?"

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because I knew the nomination might later be represented as an appointment."

Jonathan leaned forward.

"Did someone try to do that?"

"Not in a formal document. But I was repeatedly referred to as the person responsible for confirming the receiving arrangements."

"By Charles?"

"By Charles, Gabriel, and occasionally people from the Family Office."

"Did you correct them?"

"Every time."

Edward looked at the notebook.

"Did you ever receive a completed appointment?"

"No."

"Did anyone ask you to sign the recipient schedule?"

Margaret's expression changed.

"Yes."

"When?"

"Day 34."

Edward looked at Jonathan.

The same day Adrian Holt received Part Two of Annex C.

"Who brought it?"

"Charles."

"What did he want you to sign?"

"Acknowledgement that the proposed receiving identities had been verified against the originating records."

"Had they?"

"No."

"Did you explain that?"

"I explained that I had not been given the complete records."

"What did he say?"

Margaret closed the notebook.

"He told me the children had already been placed and that the schedule needed to be regularised."

Edward recognised the phrase.

It had appeared in several earlier interviews.

Regularise the paperwork.

Complete the documentation.

Let the records catch up.

"Did he ask you to sign anyway?"

"Yes."

"And you refused?"

"Yes."

"Was that the last time?"

Margaret looked down at the notebook.

"No."

The second request had come on day 41.

This time, the document was not a recipient schedule.

It was an acknowledgement of custody.

The wording stated that Margaret Voss had received the completed Annex C identity schedule from Adrian Holt and had verified that the proposed receiving identities were consistent with the originating records.

She had refused to sign.

But there was something else.

"Did you keep a copy?" Edward asked.

Margaret opened another section of the notebook.

She removed a folded sheet.

It was a handwritten correction to the proposed acknowledgement.

Across the top, she had written:

**I DID NOT RECEIVE OR VERIFY THE COMPLETED SCHEDULE.**

Below it, she had listed three reasons.

1. The original neonatal identification card had not been reconciled with the receiving records.
2. The maternal references remained separate and could not be treated as proof of receiving identity.
3. No independent receiving identity custodian had been appointed.

Edward read the third point twice.

"You knew there was no receiving custodian."

"Of course."

"Did you know the trust had been created?"

"Yes."

"Did you know about the AV-18 envelope?"

Margaret looked up sharply.

"What envelope?"

"The one addressed to the person formally appointed as receiving identity custodian."

For the first time, she appeared genuinely surprised.

"No."

"You never saw it?"

"No."

"Did you know a document had been sealed under that condition?"

"I knew a recipient schedule was being held. I did not know there was a separate envelope."

Edward put the photograph on the table.

Margaret examined the seal.

"Who has this?"

"The trustee."

"And it remains unopened?"

"Yes."

She returned the photograph.

"Keep it that way."

Edward watched her carefully.

"Why?"

"Because whoever prepared that instruction knew the appointment had not been completed."

"How can you tell?"

"Because they didn't write a person's name. They wrote a position."

She tapped the condition.

"A document addressed to a real custodian would have identified the custodian, or at least the institution responsible for appointing one. This is addressed to an office that never existed."

Edward asked about the initials on the R-04 register.

Margaret explained that her nomination had been submitted through the St Catherine's records office.

She had not been nominated by the Aurelius Family Office.

She had not been nominated by the Valmonts.

Her name had been entered because she held the original restricted custody register.

"Who approved the nomination?" Jonathan asked.

"No one."

"Was it rejected?"

"Not formally. It remained pending until the nomination was withdrawn."

"Who withdrew it?"

Margaret shook her head.

"I never received a withdrawal notice."

Edward turned to her.

"Then how did you learn it had been withdrawn?"

"Someone from Vale Private Counsel told me I was no longer required."

"Who?"

"Gabriel."

"Did he explain why?"

"He said the records would be handled through another route."

"Which route?"

"He didn't say."

Edward considered that.

"Did you ever ask?"

"Yes."

"And?"

"He told me it was no longer my responsibility."

Margaret's voice hardened.

"I had been asking for the original records for weeks. Suddenly, I was no longer required to verify them."

She paused.

"I was relieved."

Edward looked at her.

"Relieved?"

"Yes. I was tired of being asked to certify things I could not verify."

It was an understandable reaction.

But it had also created an opportunity for someone else to take control.

"Why didn't you report the repeated requests?" Jonathan asked.

Margaret looked at him.

"To whom?"

"Hospital management. The Family Office. The police."

"At the time, I believed the matter was an internal records dispute. I had no evidence that a child had been deliberately misidentified. I had evidence that documents were incomplete and people were pressuring me to sign them."

"That was serious."

"I know that now."

She looked at the old notebook.

"But I was also dealing with two families who had been told their children were safe, hospitals that would not release records without authorisation, and private representatives who claimed to be acting under authority I could not independently verify."

Edward said nothing.

Margaret continued.

"I thought refusing to sign would prevent an incorrect identity declaration. I did not realise how easily the same people could simply find someone else to sign a different document."

The admission was quiet.

It was not an excuse.

It was a description of how a flawed process had continued despite individual refusals.

Before Edward left, Margaret showed him one more entry.

It was dated day 47.

The same day Helena Brooks's nomination had been withdrawn.

*Independent authority required. Neither originating family administration nor placement administrator should control receiving verification.*

Below it was a reference.

**R-04/AV-18/47.**

Edward looked up.

"Did you write this?"

"No."

"Who did?"

"I don't know."

"How did it come into your possession?"

"It was attached to a copy of the nomination register Gabriel brought me."

"Did you keep the original?"

"No. It was a copy."

"Was there a signature?"

"No."

"Any initials?"

Margaret shook her head.

"Only the reference."

Edward took a photograph of the page with her permission.

As he put away his phone, Margaret spoke again.

"Mr Valmont."

He stopped.

"Yes?"

"Do not assume that refusing to sign made me innocent of everything that happened."

Edward turned.

Margaret's expression was grave.

"I kept records. I refused false certification. But I also remained in the process after I knew the process was unsafe. I should have done more."

Edward considered her words.

"Then help us establish what happened."

"I will."

"And if you remember anything else?"

"I'll tell you."

For the first time since he had begun questioning her, Margaret sounded less like a defensive records custodian and more like someone who had finally decided to stop protecting the boundaries of her old responsibilities.

The evidence from Margaret changed the investigation's direction.

Until then, Edward had assumed the central problem was a failed verification process.

Now he had evidence that people had actively tried to obtain signatures certifying a completed process that had not occurred.

The distinction mattered.

An incomplete process could result from confusion, delay, or administrative failure.

A request to falsely certify its completion suggested something more deliberate.

Jonathan placed the day 34 acknowledgement beside the day 41 version.

"Two attempts."

"At least two."

"Both refused."

"And both were designed to make it appear that the originating and receiving identities had been reconciled."

Jonathan looked at the documents.

"Who eventually signed?"

Edward shook his head.

"We don't know."

"Someone must have."

"Or someone created a record suggesting that someone had."

They began tracing the signatures on every surviving copy of the recipient schedule.

One version contained no signature.

Another bore an initial that could not be attributed.

A third carried a stamp from Vale Private Counsel.

But none contained a valid acceptance by a receiving identity custodian.

The more documents they examined, the clearer the central absence became.

There had never been a completed receiving authority.

And yet several records referred to receiving verification as though it had already occurred.

That evening, Edward visited the Valmont estate.

Richard was in his study when Edward arrived.

Sebastian was reviewing a financial report, while Nicholas had occupied the sofa with a tablet.

Catherine entered a few minutes later.

"Have you found something?" Richard asked.

Edward placed the latest investigation summary on the desk.

"Margaret Voss has confirmed that she refused two attempts to obtain her signature certifying the recipient schedule."

Richard read the first page.

"Why was she asked to sign?"

"Because she held the originating hospital records."

"Was she qualified to verify the receiving identities?"

"No."

Richard's expression darkened.

"Then the request was improper."

"Yes."

Sebastian turned a page.

"Who signed instead?"

"We haven't identified anyone."

Nicholas put down his tablet.

"Does this mean someone deliberately falsified the records?"

"It means someone attempted to create the appearance of completed verification. We still need to identify whether a false certification was ultimately issued."

Catherine looked at the chronology.

"Did Margaret know which child was ours?"

"No."

"Did she know which child belonged to the Aurelius family?"

"No."

"Then she cannot tell us whether Elena or Isabelle was the child we were searching for."

Edward shook his head.

"Not yet."

Catherine looked toward the window.

Elena's name remained absent from the investigation report.

That absence had become almost unbearable.

She had raised Elena.

She had watched her grow.

And now every new discovery seemed to place another layer of uncertainty between the girl she knew and the child whose identity had been lost.

"How much longer will this take?" she asked.

Edward did not pretend to know.

"I can't give you a date."

Richard closed the report.

"Then continue until you have something reliable."

Edward nodded.

In Elena's room, a different kind of preparation was underway.

Her passport documents had been arranged in a folder.

The university had requested additional information about her academic interests and the project she intended to develop during the international programme.

Elena had been revising her project proposal.

Her original idea concerned the reliability of digital records in small organisations.

She wanted to examine how information was created, updated, corrected, and preserved when there was no dedicated technical department.

The project was not particularly glamorous.

That was part of its appeal.

Small organisations often depended on paper registers, scattered spreadsheets, and individual employees who knew where everything was kept.

When someone left, retired, or misplaced a file, important information could disappear.

Elena wanted to design a simple system that made records easier to maintain without requiring expensive software or specialised equipment.

She had already prepared a basic prototype.

Now she was rewriting the explanation.

Her first version had been too technical.

The second had been too broad.

The third, she hoped, would be understandable to someone who had never written a line of code.

Nicholas knocked before entering.

"Still working?"

"Yes."

"Have you eaten?"

Elena glanced at the clock.

"Oh."

Nicholas stared at her.

"That means no."

"I had a snack."

"That is not dinner."

"It was a substantial snack."

"Elena."

She smiled.

"All right. I'll come down."

Nicholas picked up the folder beside her laptop.

"What's this?"

"My project proposal."

He glanced at the title.

"Reliable Records for Small Organisations."

"Yes."

"That sounds useful."

"It is supposed to be."

"Can I read it?"

"After dinner."

"Why?"

"Because if you read it now, you'll ask questions, I'll explain the prototype, and we'll both forget dinner."

Nicholas considered that.

"Fair."

He put the folder down.

Elena saved her work and closed the laptop.

For a moment, she looked around the room.

Her childhood home was still familiar.

The bookshelves, the desk, the photographs, the small collection of souvenirs from family trips.

But the future was no longer arranged entirely around it.

Singapore was becoming real.

She would leave soon.

And for the first time, she was beginning to feel excited rather than simply nervous.

The following morning, Jonathan received a call from the trustee.

They had discovered another entry in the original R-04 correspondence register.

It had been filed under a different reference and had therefore escaped the earlier search.

The entry concerned the withdrawal of Margaret Voss's nomination.

But the withdrawal had not been submitted by Charles Vale.

It had not been submitted by Gabriel Vale.

It had not come from the Family Office.

It had been authorised by an independent administrative office.

**ST CATHERINE'S — ORIGINATING RECORDS AUTHORITY.**

Jonathan read the scan.

"That doesn't make sense."

Edward examined it.

"Why?"

"St Catherine's had already transferred the restricted records. Why would the hospital withdraw Margaret's nomination?"

"Perhaps the hospital believed it still controlled the originating authority."

"Did it?"

Edward looked at the next line.

The withdrawal had been submitted on day 47.

The reason was recorded as:

*Nominee's authority limited to custody of original records. Independent verification must be performed by an officer who has not previously handled the transfer schedule.*

Jonathan leaned forward.

"So they wanted someone independent."

"Yes."

"Then why didn't they nominate someone else?"

Edward turned the page.

There was a second line.

*Replacement nominee to be appointed following review of original maternity and neonatal registers.*

No replacement name appeared.

No further appointment was recorded.

The hospital had withdrawn Margaret's nomination because she was not independent enough.

But it had never appointed anyone in her place.

Edward stared at the blank space.

Another position left empty.

Another authority that had never existed.

He requested the complete St Catherine's records authority register.

The hospital's successor institution agreed to provide the surviving pages under legal supervision.

When the documents arrived, one page was missing.

Not torn.

Not damaged.

Removed.

The preceding page recorded the withdrawal of Margaret Voss's nomination.

The following page recorded an unrelated administrative appointment.

Between them should have been the replacement nomination.

Edward examined the binding.

The removal had been deliberate.

A small notation remained in the lower corner of the preceding page.

**SEE SEALED APPOINTMENT — SC/RA/47.**

"There's another sealed file," Jonathan said.

Edward nodded.

"Find it."

The file was not held at the hospital.

It had been transferred to the successor institution's legal archive, where it was classified as a restricted administrative appointment.

The archive manager located the reference but refused immediate access.

It required authorisation from the institution's records governance committee.

Edward submitted the request.

The answer came three hours later.

Access was approved.

The file contained only four pages.

The first confirmed that Margaret Voss's nomination had been withdrawn.

The second stated that the replacement custodian must be independent of all organisations involved in the placement process.

The third recorded the appointment procedure.

The fourth contained the actual nomination.

Edward read the name.

Then he read it again.

It was not Charles Vale.

Not Adrian Holt.

Not Marianne Cross.

Not Helena Brooks.

And not anyone from either family.

The nominated officer had been an independent neonatal records auditor employed by a separate medical standards organisation.

The appointment was dated day 49.

But the acceptance section was blank.

At the bottom of the page, a handwritten note had been added.

*Nominee declined after reviewing the available transfer material.*

Jonathan looked at Edward.

"So even the independent candidate refused."

Edward turned the page.

There was a brief explanation attached.

*The originating records do not establish a continuous identity chain. Any certification at this stage would exceed the available evidence.*

He placed the document on the desk.

For the first time, the investigation had found someone who had been appointed correctly.

And that person had refused the authority for the same reason Margaret had.

The evidence was insufficient.

Edward read the final page.

It contained a reference to a meeting held on day 50.

Three representatives had attended.

The originating hospital's records director.

The independent medical auditor.

And a representative of Vale Private Counsel.

The meeting minutes were incomplete.

Only one sentence remained in the surviving copy.

*The representative of Vale Private Counsel maintains that the receiving arrangements cannot be delayed further.*

Beneath it was the auditor's response.

*Then the receiving arrangements must remain provisional.*

The minutes ended there.

No final resolution was recorded.

Edward looked at the date.

Day 50.

One day after the independent nominee had declined.

He turned the page over.

A faint handwritten note was visible through the paper.

It had not been part of the typed minutes.

**If no one can certify the identity, who gave permission to decide it?**

There was no signature.

Edward sat back.

He had spent weeks trying to identify the person who had authorised the transfers.

Now he was beginning to suspect that the most important decision had never been authorised at all.

Someone had proceeded anyway.

And somewhere in the surviving records was the evidence of who had decided that provisional arrangements were good enough to become permanent.
