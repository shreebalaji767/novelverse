# **Chapter 121 — The Courier Who Remembered Two Packages**

Nora Whitcomb agreed to meet them, but not immediately.

Her first response was a request for the documents Edward intended to discuss.

Her second was a question.

“Are you investigating the deliveries or the people who arranged them?”

Edward had answered carefully.

“Both, but we are not asking you to identify any child. We want to establish what you transported, who gave it to you, and where you delivered it.”

Nora had considered that for several hours.

Then she agreed.

The meeting was scheduled for the following afternoon.

Nora lived in a modest house overlooking a quiet stretch of coastline.

She was in her late sixties, with neatly arranged silver hair and a habit of listening without interrupting.

Edward introduced himself and Jonathan.

Nora offered them tea.

Then she placed a small leather case on the table.

Edward recognised it immediately.

It was brown, worn at the corners, with a narrow brass clasp.

“Is that the case you used for deliveries?”

“Yes.”

“Did you carry it to V-17?”

“Several times.”

“Do you remember a delivery on day 40?”

Nora opened the case.

Inside were several old identity cards, courier permits, and a folded copy of her professional registration.

She removed one document.

“My assignment register.”

Edward looked at it.

The day-40 entry was brief.

**Collection: V-17.**

**Delivery: Cross Administrative Services.**

**Reference: CA-17.**

**Authority: Document transport only.**

The signature beneath it was hers.

“Do you remember the contents?”

“I wasn't supposed to examine them.”

“Did you?”

“No. The envelope was sealed when I received it, and I delivered it sealed.”

“Who gave it to you?”

“Mr Charles Vale.”

Edward leaned forward.

“Personally?”

“Yes.”

“Did he tell you who had sent the document?”

“No.”

“Did he tell you who was supposed to receive it?”

“Cross Administrative Services.”

“Did he explain why it was being delivered privately?”

“He said the document contained confidential medical and administrative information. He wanted it delivered without entering the general correspondence system.”

“Did you question that?”

“I was a courier. I checked the authorisation, not the contents.”

Edward placed the day-40 cover sheet before her.

“Do you recognise this?”

Nora examined it.

“Yes. I remember the envelope.”

“Was this inside?”

“I can't say.”

“Was the sender identified?”

“No.”

“Did anyone ask you to deliver a response back to V-17?”

Nora looked at him.

“Not that day.”

Edward waited.

She closed the assignment register.

“But there was another delivery.”

The day-61 entry was on the next page.

Nora turned it around.

**Collection: V-17.**

**Delivery: Meridian Records and Preservation Services.**

**Reference: PI-07 / ANNEX C PRESERVATION TRANSFER.**

Edward's expression changed.

“That was the sealed archive box.”

“I believe so.”

“Do you remember its size?”

“Larger than the first envelope. It was a rigid document case, not a cardboard box.”

“Was it sealed?”

“Yes.”

“Who gave it to you?”

“Mr Vale's solicitor.”

“Thomas Greer?”

“I believe that was his name.”

“Did you deliver it directly to Meridian?”

“Yes.”

“Did you open it?”

“No.”

“Did you receive a copy of its contents?”

“No.”

Nora looked at the reference again.

“I remember the second delivery because the instructions were unusual.”

“What instructions?”

“The receiving office had to acknowledge physical custody, but I was told not to ask whether they accepted the contents.”

Edward exchanged a glance with Jonathan.

“Did you understand the distinction?”

“I understood it as a courier. A person can receive a sealed package without agreeing with its contents or the reason it was sent.”

“Did Meridian object?”

Nora thought for a moment.

“The clerk asked whether I had authority to explain the documents.”

“What did you say?”

“That I had no authority beyond delivery.”

“Did they accept the package?”

“They signed for physical custody.”

“Did they accept the instructions?”

“No.”

“How do you know?”

Nora looked at the old assignment register.

“Because the clerk specifically wrote that acceptance of the package did not constitute acceptance of the authority attached to it.”

Edward leaned back.

The same distinction again.

It had been written into the courier instruction, repeated by the receiving clerk, and preserved in the transfer record.

Someone had been careful to prevent custody from becoming authority.

Yet elsewhere, the records had repeatedly blurred those two concepts.

Edward showed Nora the day-40 conditional response.

She read it slowly.

“Have you seen this before?” he asked.

“No.”

“Do you recognise the handwriting?”

“I don't know.”

“Did you deliver an envelope containing a statement like this?”

“I delivered a sealed envelope. I did not know what was inside.”

“Could the sender have been the person who gave you the envelope?”

“Possibly.”

“Did Charles Vale say it came from him?”

“No. He said he was arranging its delivery.”

Edward showed her the day-40 courier record.

“Was there anyone else present?”

Nora considered the question.

“A woman came into the office while I was waiting.”

“Which office?”

“V-17.”

“Do you remember her?”

“She was older than me, perhaps in her fifties at the time. She wore a dark coat and carried a brown leather case.”

Edward glanced at the case on the table.

“Like yours?”

“Similar, but smaller.”

“Did she speak to Charles?”

“Yes.”

“Did you hear what they said?”

“Not everything.”

“What did you hear?”

Nora thought carefully.

“I remember her saying, ‘You cannot ask them to accept what you have not verified.’”

Edward did not move.

“And Charles?”

“He replied that he wasn't asking for acceptance. He was asking them to consider the arrangement.”

“Did she agree?”

“No.”

“What did she say?”

Nora looked toward the window.

“She said, ‘Then stop recording consideration as agreement.’”

Edward exchanged a glance with Jonathan.

“Did she leave anything?”

“I saw her hand Mr Vale a folded paper.”

“Was it the conditional response?”

“I don't know.”

“Did you transport it?”

“No. I was only asked to deliver the sealed envelope.”

“Did you see her again?”

Nora nodded.

“On day 61.”

The second meeting had taken place at V-17.

Nora remembered it because the woman had been waiting near the private office when she arrived to collect the document case.

“She was carrying the same smaller brown case,” Nora said.

“Did she speak to you?”

“She asked whether the package would go directly to Meridian.”

“What did you tell her?”

“That was the instruction.”

“Did she ask what was inside?”

“No. She said the contents were not my responsibility.”

“Did she give you anything?”

Nora paused.

“Yes.”

Edward leaned forward.

“What?”

“A small envelope.”

“Was it addressed?”

“To the receiving office.”

“Which receiving office?”

“Meridian.”

“Did you deliver it with the document case?”

“No.”

“Why not?”

“She told me it was not part of the authorised transfer.”

“Then why did she give it to you?”

Nora looked uncomfortable.

“She asked me to keep it until the delivery had been acknowledged.”

Edward was silent.

“Did you agree?”

“Yes.”

“Where is it now?”

Nora opened the leather case again.

She removed a narrow paper sleeve.

It was old, yellowed at the edges, and marked with a handwritten reference.

**MERIDIAN — RECEIVING ACKNOWLEDGEMENT**

Edward stared at it.

“Have you opened it?”

“No.”

“Why not?”

“Because she told me it should remain sealed unless the receiving office confirmed that the package had been preserved without alteration.”

“Did that confirmation arrive?”

Nora shook her head.

“No one contacted me.”

“Why didn't you return it?”

“I tried.”

“To whom?”

“Mr Vale's office. They told me the document was no longer required and that I should retain it until further instruction.”

“Who told you that?”

“His solicitor.”

Edward looked at Jonathan.

The envelope had never entered the official Meridian inventory.

It had remained with the courier.

A separate acknowledgement.

A document that might establish whether the receiving office had accepted the physical package, rejected it, or merely taken custody.

“May we have it examined?” Edward asked.

Nora considered.

“I will allow it to be preserved and examined. But I want a copy of the report.”

“Of course.”

Dr Sen was contacted.

The envelope was examined at the preservation laboratory the following morning.

Its seal was intact.

The exterior bore no sender's name.

Only the intended office and a reference:

**MERIDIAN / PI-07 / CUSTODY ACKNOWLEDGEMENT**

Dr Sen documented the condition and obtained permission to open it.

Inside was a single acknowledgement slip.

It was not the official transfer receipt.

It was a separate form.

The heading read:

**RESTRICTED PACKAGE — PHYSICAL CUSTODY CONFIRMATION**

Three boxes appeared beneath it.

- Received in sealed condition.
- Contents examined.
- Authority attached to contents accepted.

The first box had been marked.

The second and third were blank.

At the bottom, the receiving clerk had written:

*Package accepted for preservation only. No review of contents undertaken. No authority to determine identity or receiving status accepted.*

The signature was legible.

**M. L.**

Jonathan checked the original Meridian transfer receipt.

“The day-61 receipt was signed by the same clerk.”

Edward nodded.

“Then we can establish that the package arrived sealed and that Meridian accepted physical custody only.”

Dr Sen turned the slip over.

There was another note on the reverse.

**Separate acknowledgement retained pending joint review.**

Below it were two initials.

**E.V.**

Edward looked at the date.

Day 61.

Eleanor had known the separate acknowledgement existed.

Yet she had not placed it in the main archive.

“Why would she keep it separate?” Jonathan asked.

“Perhaps because the main transfer receipt could later be mistaken for acceptance of the contents.”

“Or acceptance of the authority.”

Edward looked at the three boxes.

The first had been marked.

The other two had not.

“Either way,” he said, “the document was meant to prevent another misunderstanding.”

Eleanor confirmed the note that afternoon.

“Yes,” she said. “I asked for a separate acknowledgement.”

“Why?”

“Because the transfer receipt was too broad. It said Meridian had received the package under the preservation instruction. I wanted the receiving office to make clear that it had accepted custody, not the contents or any authority contained within them.”

“Did you see this slip?”

“Yes.”

“Did you keep a copy?”

“No. I kept the original in the restricted correspondence file, but I did not know it remained with the courier.”

“Why wasn't it entered into Meridian's official register?”

“Because it was never returned to me. I assumed it had been misplaced.”

Edward placed the slip before her.

“Did you know the courier retained it?”

“No.”

“Who instructed you to keep it separate?”

“I did.”

“Did Charles agree?”

“He objected.”

“What did he say?”

“He believed the ordinary transfer receipt was sufficient.”

“And you?”

“I disagreed.”

Eleanor looked at the document.

“Meridian had accepted the package. That was all. No one had accepted the proposed recipient, and no one had verified the identity of the child.”

“Did you know the contents of the package?”

“I knew it contained Annex C, Part One, Part Two, Schedule 4, and the associated restricted instructions.”

“Did you know whether the package contained the final identity of the child?”

“No. The files contained records and proposed associations. They did not contain a completed biological determination.”

Edward paused.

“Then what did you believe the package was supposed to accomplish?”

Eleanor considered her answer.

“To preserve the evidence until the people with the proper authority could examine it together.”

“Which people?”

“The originating representative, the medical records authority, and the receiving identity authority.”

“Was the receiving identity authority ever appointed?”

“No.”

Eleanor looked directly at him.

“That was the problem from the beginning.”

Back at the office, Edward reviewed Nora's statement.

The courier had confirmed two deliveries.

Day 40: a sealed correspondence envelope to Cross Administrative Services.

Day 61: a sealed document case to Meridian.

She had also preserved an unregistered acknowledgement slip showing that Meridian accepted physical custody without examining the contents or accepting the authority attached to them.

The evidence was valuable.

But one question remained.

Who was the woman with the smaller brown leather case?

Nora had described her as an older woman with a dark coat.

Arthur Bell had remembered the same case.

Eleanor had confirmed that an intermediary had been used.

And the courier's register contained a reference to an external administrative identity.

Edward requested the restricted identity acknowledgement associated with V17-EXT/06.

This time, Meridian did not refuse.

The original custodian's copy had been recovered from Eleanor's personal archive.

The identity field could be examined under legal preservation conditions.

Dr Sen compared the signature and the registration details.

Then she called Edward.

“The intermediary's identity is confirmed.”

Edward arrived at the laboratory.

Dr Sen placed the certificate before him.

The name was clear.

**Marianne Cross.**

Edward stared at it.

Jonathan looked from the certificate to the courier's statement.

“Marianne?”

“She was the woman with the brown leather case,” Dr Sen said. “Her identity is supported by the registration record and her original signature.”

Edward looked at the date.

Day 40.

Marianne had administered Cross Administrative Services.

She had also delivered the original conditional response to her own company.

The response had arrived through her hands, but the sender remained unidentified.

“Did she ever tell us she was the courier?” Jonathan asked.

“No.”

“She told us she was the administrative liaison.”

“She may have been both.”

Edward gathered the documents.

“Bring her in.”

Marianne Cross arrived the next morning.

When Edward placed the courier certificate before her, she looked at it for a long time.

Then she nodded.

“Yes.”

“You were V17-EXT/06.”

“Yes.”

“You delivered the day-40 envelope to your own company.”

“I did.”

“Why didn't you tell us?”

“You asked who prepared the package and whether it had been dispatched. You didn't ask whether I had personally carried an earlier document.”

Edward looked at her.

“Did you write the conditional response?”

“No.”

“Did you know who wrote it?”

Marianne hesitated.

“I knew who gave me the envelope.”

Edward leaned forward.

“Who?”

Marianne looked at the certificate.

“I was instructed not to disclose that person's identity until the originating records were verified.”

“By whom?”

“Charles Vale.”

“Did you agree?”

“I agreed to preserve the sender's confidentiality until the records could be properly reconciled. I did not agree that the response could be treated as consent.”

Edward placed the original statement before her.

“Did you see this?”

“Yes.”

“Did you know it was conditional?”

“Of course.”

“Did you ever tell anyone the sender had accepted the proposed arrangement?”

“No.”

“Did you ever tell Charles?”

“I told him the response did not authorise appointment.”

“Did he accept that?”

Marianne looked away.

“He said it was enough to keep the proposed recipient under consideration.”

“Under consideration.”

“Yes.”

“Not appointed?”

“Not appointed.”

“Not informed?”

“Not formally notified.”

Edward studied her.

“Then why was the modified acknowledgement template created?”

Marianne's expression tightened.

“I did not request the alteration.”

“Did you know about it?”

“I discovered it later.”

“When?”

“After the day-47 administrative closure.”

“Why didn't you report it?”

“I corrected the R-04 register.”

“That isn't the same as reporting the altered form.”

“I did not have authority over the Family Allocation Review system.”

Edward paused.

“Did you know the response came from Susan Bell?”

Marianne shook her head.

“No. I was not told the name.”

“Did you recognise the handwriting?”

“I recognised the way the statement was written. But I cannot identify its author.”

Edward looked at the document.

“Then who gave it to you?”

Marianne's eyes settled on the final line of the cover sheet.

**V-17 / PERSONAL DELIVERY.**

She took a breath.

“The woman who brought it to Charles Vale was not Susan Bell.”

Edward waited.

Marianne continued.

“She was someone who had been involved with the original hospital records.”

“Who?”

Marianne shook her head.

“I never knew her full identity.”

“Did you know her by another name?”

“Yes.”

“What name?”

Marianne looked at him.

“Anna.”

Edward did not move.

The name appeared in several earlier records.

Margaret Ellison had once cared for a baby she called Anna.

A woman had also appeared in the restricted correspondence trail under an uncertain identity.

But this was the first time the name had been connected directly to the day-40 response.

“Are you saying the person who brought you the statement was Margaret Ellison?”

“No.”

“Then who was Anna?”

Marianne's expression became troubled.

“I don't know whether that was her real name. It was the name Charles used when he referred to her.”

Edward looked at Jonathan.

The investigation had uncovered another name.

Not a confirmed identity.

Not a family relationship.

Just a name used by Charles Vale for a woman who had carried a conditional response through V-17.

And that woman had known enough to warn him not to confuse consideration with acceptance.

Marianne spoke again.

“There is something else you should know.”

Edward turned back.

“The woman called Anna did not come to V-17 alone.”

“Who was with her?”

Marianne hesitated.

“A man.”

“Do you know his name?”

“No. But Charles called him the originating representative.”

Edward's pen stopped.

“Which originating representative?”

Marianne looked down.

“He never said.”

For the first time, the day-40 response seemed to connect not merely to the proposed receiving family, but to someone involved in the original maternity records.

Edward closed the folder.

“Find every record of a woman referred to as Anna in Charles Vale's correspondence.”

Jonathan nodded.

“And the man?”

“Everything connected to the phrase originating representative.”

Edward glanced at the courier certificate once more.

The woman with the brown leather case had delivered two important packages.

But she had not written the conditional response.

She had only transported it.

The sender remained unknown.

The man who accompanied her remained unknown.

And the name Anna, once again, appeared without a verified identity.

Outside the office, Edward's phone vibrated.

A message from Victoria.

**Has the latest evidence established whether either child was ours?**

He read it.

Then typed his answer.

**Not yet. We have established additional records of proposed arrangements and the people who transported them. We still do not have a confirmed link between any receiving record and either mother's child.**

He sent the message.

It was not the answer Victoria wanted.

But it was the only answer the evidence allowed.

And somewhere in the old files, the woman called Anna had left behind a statement that might explain why someone had refused to accept a child without first knowing whose child it was.
