# **Chapter 27 — The Page That Was Taken Away**

Edward read the final line of Marcus Morelli's notebook for the fourth time.

**The original plan has failed. The children must remain separated until the person who selected their receiving guardians is found.**

Below it was a single name.

**ELEANOR.**

He placed the notebook on the conference table.

For several seconds, nobody spoke.

The meeting room at Valmont Holdings was usually occupied by people discussing acquisitions, quarterly figures, and negotiations. Today, it contained an eighteen-year-old mystery, several boxes of old documents, and six people who were beginning to understand that the truth had been deliberately divided into pieces.

Richard Valmont stood beside the window.

Sebastian was examining the notebook's binding.

Nicholas had abandoned his usual habit of making jokes. Catherine sat beside Richard, her hands folded tightly in her lap.

Alexander Aurelius remained at the opposite end of the table, with Adrian beside him.

Edward finally broke the silence.

"The missing page is probably important."

"That is an understatement," Sebastian replied.

"Morelli wrote that the two receiving guardians had been confirmed. He also wrote that neither infant was delivered to the person originally named. Then he made a separate note that the receiving guardians might not have known what they were receiving."

Adrian leaned forward.

"Which means there were at least three distinct stages. The original intended recipients, the actual receiving guardians, and the people who arranged the final placement."

"Possibly," Edward said. "We shouldn't assume that all three groups were separate."

Alexander looked at the notebook.

"Morelli had no authority to make those decisions."

"You have said that before," Richard replied.

"And I will continue saying it. I authorised an investigation into the disappearance of a child recorded under my family's name. I did not authorise the transfer of two newborns."

"Then someone used your authority."

"Yes."

Alexander's answer was immediate.

It was also unusually cold.

Adrian glanced at his father.

"There's another possibility. The authority was not simply used without permission. Someone may have created a sequence of documents that made it appear as though the authority had been granted."

"That would explain the unverified signature," Edward said.

"And the missing separation agreement," Sebastian added.

Catherine looked up.

"Can we establish who actually received the children?"

Edward opened a folder.

"That's what we're trying to do."

He placed a photocopy of the notebook entry beside a separate document.

"The original nursery register contains no guardian names. Morelli's notebook says the actual receiving guardians were confirmed on the ninth day, but the surviving page doesn't identify them. The missing page may contain those names."

"Then find it," Richard said.

Edward nodded.

"We're trying."

Richard's expression tightened.

He had spent much of his professional life believing that enough resources, enough competent people, and enough persistence could solve almost any problem.

This particular problem had resisted all three.

"Where was the notebook found?" Alexander asked.

"Among Marcus Morelli's personal correspondence, preserved by Eleanor Vale's estate."

"Could Eleanor have kept the missing page?"

"Possibly. Or Morelli could have removed it before sending the notebook away."

"Why would he remove it?"

Edward considered the question.

"Protection. Fear. An attempt to prevent the identities from being discovered. Or because the page contained information that would implicate him."

Nobody seemed particularly satisfied with any of those possibilities.

Adrian closed the folder.

"We should investigate the missing page independently of the names."

Edward looked at him.

"Explain."

"If we focus only on identifying the two guardians, we might overlook the circumstances surrounding the page's removal. We need to know when it disappeared, who had access to the notebook, and whether it was removed before or after Morelli left the Family Office."

"That means tracing the notebook's history."

"And the correspondence collection."

Margaret Vale, who had joined the meeting through a secure video call, adjusted her glasses.

Margaret had been listening quietly.

"My aunt Eleanor kept records of everything," she continued. "Letters, receipts, appointment books, household accounts. She was not careless with documents."

"Do you remember seeing this notebook before?" Edward asked.

"No. I only found it when I was sorting the correspondence collection. It was inside a box labelled Personal Papers — Unsorted."

"Was the box sealed?"

"No. But the papers inside were tied into separate bundles. The notebook was in one of them, along with three letters."

"Three?"

"Yes. You have only received two copies from me so far. The third was addressed to Eleanor."

Edward straightened.

"What did it say?"

"I haven't read it."

"Why not?"

"Because the envelope was sealed, and it was not addressed to me. I thought it might be relevant to the investigation, so I kept it separate."

A brief silence followed.

"Can you send it to us?" Edward asked.

"Not electronically. The paper needs to be examined properly. I can arrange for it to be delivered to your legal representative."

"Please do."

Margaret nodded.

"I should mention something else."

Edward waited.

"The notebook wasn't the only item in that bundle with signs of tampering."

"What else?"

"A small envelope. Its contents were missing, but the paper had been folded around something rectangular. There was a label on the outside."

"What label?"

Margaret checked her notes.

"Hospital correspondence. Ninth day."

Edward slowly looked down at the notebook.

The ninth day.

Again.

"Do you still have the envelope?"

"Yes."

"Please don't handle it any further."

"I haven't."

The call ended a few minutes later.

Edward remained standing.

"Hospital correspondence," Nicholas repeated. "That's rather specific."

"It could be anything," Sebastian said. "A receipt, a transfer document, a letter, a copy of the register."

"Or the missing page," Catherine suggested.

Edward nodded.

"That's possible."

Alexander looked towards Adrian.

"Have the old Family Office records been searched for a corresponding envelope?"

"Yes. Nothing has been found under Morelli's name."

"Search under Eleanor's."

Adrian made a note.

"Already planned."

Richard turned away from the window.

"Then we have another lead."

It was not the answer any of them wanted.

But it was something.

At the Valmont Estate, Elena was having a considerably less complicated morning.

Or at least, she had intended to.

Her laptop was open on the dining table.

Three university programme brochures lay beside it, along with a notebook, a calculator, and a cup of tea that had been forgotten long enough to become unpleasantly cold.

Isabelle sat opposite her.

She was reading the same brochure for the third time.

"You're still looking at Singapore?"

Elena glanced up.

"Yes."

"You've already read that page."

"I know."

"And the page before it."

"I know."

"Are you expecting the fees to become smaller if you read them repeatedly?"

Elena looked down at the brochure.

"That would be convenient."

Isabelle smiled faintly.

"Unfortunately, I don't think universities operate that way."

"Then they're missing an opportunity."

Elena turned her laptop slightly.

"Look at this."

Isabelle leaned forward.

The screen displayed a university exchange programme with information about academic requirements, accommodation, tuition arrangements, and application deadlines.

"You're applying for this one?"

"Preliminary application. It doesn't guarantee admission."

"How long would you be away?"

"One semester, possibly two if everything goes well."

Isabelle studied the information.

"That's quite a long time."

"Yes."

"Would you be living in university accommodation?"

"Probably. I haven't decided yet."

Elena scrolled down.

"There are several options. Some are cheaper than others, and the university has a student support office for international exchange students."

"You've researched everything."

"I haven't researched everything."

"You have a spreadsheet."

Elena paused.

Then she looked at the second tab of her laptop.

"That's different."

"How?"

"Spreadsheets are useful."

Isabelle laughed.

It was a small sound, but it came more easily than it had during their first few days together.

Elena returned to the application.

"Have you decided what you want to do?"

"Not yet."

"University?"

"Yes. I've been looking at business and economics programmes."

"That suits you."

"Why?"

"You like understanding how things work. Especially organisations."

Isabelle considered that.

"I suppose I do."

She closed her brochure.

"I've also been speaking with your mother about the foundation."

Elena's fingers stopped above the keyboard.

Catherine's foundation.

The organisation had been part of the Valmont family's public identity for years, but Isabelle had recently begun volunteering there.

"What do you think?" Elena asked.

"It's interesting. More complicated than I expected."

"Most things are."

"I thought it would mostly involve attending events and helping with donations."

"And?"

"And there are budgets, regional offices, project assessments, audit reports, funding applications, and about a thousand other things."

Elena nodded.

"That sounds about right."

Isabelle looked at her.

"Did you ever work there?"

"Not officially. Mother occasionally asked me to help organise things for smaller events. I also helped with a few student programmes."

"Did you enjoy it?"

"Some parts."

"Which parts?"

"Planning things. Seeing whether a project actually worked. Finding out why a programme wasn't reaching the people it was supposed to help."

Isabelle looked thoughtful.

"That's the part I'm interested in too."

"Then you should ask Mother if you can spend some time with the programme managers."

"I don't want them to think I'm there because I'm the Valmont daughter."

Elena tilted her head.

"Then ask them to give you ordinary work."

"Ordinary work?"

"Data entry. Reviewing applications. Checking records. Whatever they need."

Isabelle gave her an incredulous look.

"You make it sound like I should volunteer to sort filing cabinets."

"Filing cabinets are important."

"That's not what I meant."

"I know."

Elena smiled.

"Besides, if you understand the ordinary work, you'll have a better idea of what the organisation actually does."

Isabelle looked down at the brochure.

She had spent much of her life imagining what it would mean to belong to the Valmont family.

She had imagined the house, the people, the opportunities, and the security.

She had not spent much time imagining the work behind any of it.

Now she was beginning to understand that being part of a family was not the same as knowing how its world functioned.

"I'll ask her," she said.

"Good."

Elena returned to her laptop.

A moment later, Isabelle spoke again.

"Would you be upset if I applied for an exchange programme too?"

Elena looked up.

"Why would I be?"

"I don't know. You were planning to go first."

"That doesn't mean you can't."

"I thought you might want to do something independently."

"I do."

Elena closed the laptop halfway.

"But independence isn't a competition. You don't have to avoid doing something simply because I might do it too."

Isabelle studied her expression.

"You're surprisingly reasonable."

"I can be unreasonable if you prefer."

"Please don't."

"Then we agree."

They returned to their brochures.

For the next twenty minutes, they compared university programmes and complained about application requirements.

It was an ordinary conversation.

Neither girl mentioned DNA results.

Neither discussed the missing hospital records.

And neither realised that the choices they were making might eventually take them far beyond the boundaries of the families that had raised them.

The sealed envelope arrived at the Valmont Holdings legal office the following afternoon.

Edward was informed immediately.

He went to the office himself.

Margaret had arranged for the document to be transported through a specialist records service. It was placed inside a protective sleeve, accompanied by a short statement confirming that it had remained in her possession since discovery.

Edward waited while the legal archivist inspected the package.

The envelope was old.

Its edges had yellowed, and the flap had been sealed with a thin strip of dark adhesive.

On the front, in handwriting that appeared consistent with Eleanor Vale's correspondence, were the words:

**To be opened only if the children are still being searched for.**

Edward read the sentence twice.

"That sounds rather deliberate," the archivist said.

"It does."

"Do you want me to proceed?"

"Yes. Document everything."

The archivist photographed the envelope from several angles before examining the seal.

It had been opened once and resealed.

Edward leaned closer.

"Can you tell when?"

"Not yet. The adhesive is old, but there are signs that the flap was lifted carefully. Someone may have opened it and then restored it."

"Could it have been Eleanor?"

"Possibly."

The archivist carefully separated the flap.

Inside was a single folded sheet.

There was no signature on the front.

Only a date.

The ninth day after the births.

Edward felt his attention sharpen.

The archivist unfolded the paper.

It contained a short letter.

Edward began reading.

> Eleanor,
>
> I have completed the final arrangements.
>
> The two receiving guardians have been confirmed. They have not been told the original identities of the children, and I have not disclosed the details of the failed arrangement.
>
> The children will leave separately.
>
> Do not attempt to reunite them until the original authority has been established and the person who initiated the change has been identified.
>
> I cannot take responsibility for another decision made without the mothers' knowledge.
>
> Keep the remaining records outside the Family Office.
>
> If anyone asks, the matter is still unresolved.
>
> M.

Edward lowered the page.

The room was silent.

The archivist looked at him.

"Is something wrong?"

"No."

Edward read the letter again.

Then he looked at the date.

It was written on the ninth day.

The day both children left the private nursery.

And the final initial was unmistakable.

"M," Edward said quietly.

The letter was not a confession.

It did not identify the receiving guardians.

It did not explain who selected them.

But it established something important.

Morelli had not merely recorded the final arrangements after the fact.

He had written that he completed them.

He had been directly involved.

Edward placed the letter beside the notebook.

One line in the notebook now seemed particularly significant.

*I will not choose between them again.*

He had not been speaking metaphorically.

He had been involved in a choice.

A choice that had separated two newborn children.

And apparently, he had not wanted to repeat it.

At the Aurelius Estate, Alexander received Edward's preliminary findings that evening.

He read the letter in silence.

Victoria sat beside him.

Adrian stood near the fireplace, while Lucien and Damien had joined the discussion after returning from their respective offices.

Julian was absent.

He had been informed that the meeting concerned the old investigation and had decided, after some thought, that he should not attend unless specifically asked.

He had also left a note on the dining table.

**I am available if anyone needs a younger brother's perspective.**

Lucien had found it amusing.

Alexander had ignored it.

Now the five adults were examining the letter.

"Morelli completed the arrangements," Victoria said.

"According to this document," Adrian replied.

Alexander's expression hardened.

"He was not authorised to do so."

"Then why did he?" Damien asked.

Nobody answered immediately.

Lucien leaned against the back of a chair.

"Perhaps he believed the children were in danger."

"That does not give him the right to decide their identities," Alexander said.

"No," Lucien agreed. "It doesn't."

Victoria looked at the letter again.

"He says he did not tell the guardians who the children were."

"Which means the guardians may have believed they were receiving ordinary infants through an authorised process," Adrian said.

"Or they may have known part of the arrangement," Damien added. "We don't know."

Victoria's fingers rested on the edge of the paper.

"Two families raised children without knowing the truth."

Her voice was quiet.

Alexander looked towards her.

"We don't know that yet."

"We know the records were altered. We know the children were separated. We know neither mother was properly informed."

"Yes."

"And we know someone decided that we should not receive the original records."

Alexander did not disagree.

Victoria turned away.

For eighteen years, she had imagined countless explanations for what had happened to her daughter.

Some had been impossible.

Some had been unbearable.

And now she had discovered that the child might have been alive all along, raised somewhere beyond the reach of the family that had spent years searching.

She had no idea whether her daughter had been happy.

Whether she had been loved.

Whether she had been sick.

Whether she had ever wondered why she looked different from the people around her.

She did not even know what name the girl had been given.

"Alexander," she said.

He waited.

"If we find her, we cannot make her pay for what the adults did."

His expression softened slightly.

"I know."

"She may not want to come here."

"Then we will have to understand why."

"She may not want to meet us."

Alexander looked at the letter.

"Then we will give her time."

Adrian watched his parents.

He had spent much of his life seeing his father as someone who could make decisions with remarkable certainty.

But this was different.

There was no company to acquire, no contract to negotiate, no board to persuade.

There was only a daughter who might be somewhere in the world, living an ordinary life without knowing that five people had spent years waiting for her.

And none of them could demand that she return simply because they had finally found a reason to hope.

Lucien broke the silence.

"We should locate Morelli."

"Agreed," Alexander said.

Damien looked at Adrian.

"His old address is useless. His former property was sold, and the separation agreement is missing."

"Then we follow the people who handled the sale."

Adrian closed the document.

"And the letter he sent to Eleanor suggests he maintained contact with her for at least two years after leaving."

Victoria looked up.

"Which means he may have continued communicating with her."

"Yes."

"Then Eleanor's correspondence may contain more."

Adrian nodded.

"We'll examine the rest of the collection."

Alexander looked at the letter one final time.

"Find him."

That night, Elena was sitting in the small reading room at the Valmont Estate.

She had finished her university application for the day and was reading a book she had borrowed from the family library.

The house was quiet.

Beyond the windows, the garden lights illuminated the paths and trimmed hedges.

Her phone vibrated.

A message from Clara appeared.

**Clara:** Did you finish the application?

**Elena:** Almost.

**Clara:** That means no.

**Elena:** It means almost.

**Clara:** I am sending emergency biscuits tomorrow.

Elena smiled.

**Elena:** I don't need emergency biscuits.

**Clara:** That's what people who need emergency biscuits always say.

Elena placed the phone beside her book.

For a moment, she considered telling Clara about the conversation with Isabelle.

About the possibility of both of them applying for exchange programmes.

About the strange feeling of planning a future while the adults around her were still investigating the past.

But she decided to leave it for tomorrow.

She turned another page.

A little later, Nicholas appeared in the doorway.

"Still awake?"

"Yes."

"You're going to ruin your sleep schedule."

"My sleep schedule is perfectly fine."

"It's eleven."

"That's not late."

"For someone who claims to be perfectly organised, you're surprisingly careless about sleep."

Elena closed the book around one finger.

"Did you come here to discuss my sleeping habits?"

"No. I came to ask whether you wanted some cake."

She considered this.

"What kind?"

"Chocolate."

"Then your visit is justified."

Nicholas grinned.

He held up a small plate.

Elena accepted it.

"Thank you."

He sat across from her.

For a few minutes, they ate in comfortable silence.

Then Nicholas looked at the book.

"Are you still thinking about Singapore?"

"Yes."

"Excited?"

"A little."

"Nervous?"

"A little."

"Good."

Elena raised an eyebrow.

"Good?"

"If you weren't nervous at all, I'd be worried."

She smiled.

Nicholas leaned back.

"You're going to do fine."

"You don't know that."

"I know you."

Elena looked down at her cake.

That answer was simple.

Perhaps too simple.

But she appreciated it.

Nicholas did not ask whether she was worried about the investigation.

He did not remind her of the DNA test.

He did not tell her that the family was trying to find answers.

For tonight, he allowed her to be a girl preparing for university.

And Elena was grateful.

The following morning, Edward received a call from the investigator assigned to locate Marcus Morelli.

The investigator had found something.

Not Morelli himself.

A record.

An old residential lease application submitted under a different name, dated four years after Morelli left the Aurelius Family Office.

The address was in a coastal town several hours from the capital.

The application included an emergency contact.

A woman.

Her name was not Eleanor Vale.

It was **Martha Voss**.

Edward searched the name in the documents already collected.

Nothing.

Then he asked the investigator to check the telephone number.

The number had been disconnected years ago.

But the residential address had one additional record attached to it.

A property maintenance request.

Submitted eleven years earlier.

The person who signed it had written a name at the bottom.

**Marcus Morelli.**

Edward looked at the date.

Eleven years.

Morelli had apparently been alive and using that address long after his departure from the Aurelius Family Office.

"Send someone there," Edward said.

"Already arranged."

"Don't approach the property owner aggressively. We need to know whether he is still there."

"Understood."

Edward ended the call.

He looked once more at the name of the emergency contact.

Martha Voss.

Then he noticed a handwritten annotation beside it, added by the investigator.

**Possible former hospital employee.**

Edward stopped moving.

He picked up the telephone again.

"Cancel the immediate visit," he said.

"Sir?"

"Don't contact the woman yet. I want her employment history checked first."

"Understood."

Edward returned to his desk.

For the first time in several weeks, the investigation had produced a name that did not come from the Aurelius Family Office, the Valmont family, or the legal archive.

A new person.

Someone who might have known Morelli after he disappeared.

Someone who might have known what happened on the ninth day.

And possibly someone who had been at the hospital before the two children were born.

Edward opened a fresh investigation file.

At the top, he typed:

**MARTHA VOSS — CONNECTION TO MARCUS MORELLI.**

Then, beneath it, he added a question.

**What did she know about the children?**
