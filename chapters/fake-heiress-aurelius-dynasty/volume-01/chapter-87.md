# **Chapter 87 — The Person Who Came to Collect It**

Edward called the hospital records department before Susan Bell had finished her second cup of tea the following morning.

The department manager confirmed that the former administrative cabinet was still in storage.

It had been transferred from Susan's office after her retirement, sealed with several other restricted cabinets, and registered under an inactive departmental inventory number.

Nobody had opened it.

At least, nobody had officially opened it.

"Who requested access yesterday?" Edward asked.

The manager consulted the visitor records.

"An external representative."

"Name?"

"Mr. Adrian Holt."

Edward went silent.

"Did he collect anything?"

"No. Access was denied because the cabinet is registered under a former employee's custody record. We require written authorisation from the original custodian."

"Did he provide any documentation?"

"A letter from his legal office. It stated that he was collecting a family document under an existing custody arrangement."

"Which family?"

"The letter did not specify."

Edward looked at Jonathan.

Holt had visited the hospital less than twenty-four hours after Edward questioned him about the missing item.

That was not necessarily proof of wrongdoing.

But it was no longer possible to treat the timing as coincidence without investigating it.

"Please preserve the visitor record, the access request, and any accompanying correspondence," Edward said. "Nobody is to remove or open that cabinet until the custody authority has been established."

The manager agreed.

Edward ended the call.

Jonathan was already standing.

"He knew."

"He knew Susan had something."

"How?"

"Someone may have told him. Or he may have known about the cabinet all along."

"Then why wait eighteen years?"

Edward looked at the file.

"Perhaps he believed the matter was finished."

"And now?"

"Now he believes something in that cabinet could still be claimed."

Susan Bell arrived at the hospital shortly before noon.

She brought her old professional identification card, a copy of her retirement transfer form, and the diary in which she had recorded the envelope.

Edward had asked her to bring nothing else.

The records department had arranged a meeting room away from the public counters.

"I never thought anyone would ask about that cabinet again," Susan said.

"Why did you keep the diary entry?"

"Because I didn't like the instruction."

"What instruction?"

"That the envelope was to be delivered to a receiving family, but the receiving authority had not been confirmed."

Edward opened his notebook.

"Did you know what Part Two contained?"

"No."

"Did you know who the receiving family was?"

"No."

"Did you ask Dr Hale?"

"I did."

"What did he say?"

Susan considered her answer.

"He told me that the document was not to be opened until the originating authority and receiving authority had been verified."

"Did he identify either authority?"

"No. He said the information would be supplied later."

"And was it?"

"Never."

Edward glanced at Jonathan.

"Did you contact him again?"

"Yes. Three days later, I received a telephone call from someone in Mr. Vale's office. He said the document was required urgently and that the verification delay was an administrative formality."

"Who called?"

"I don't remember the name."

"Did you release it?"

"No."

"Why?"

Susan looked directly at him.

"Because I had already seen what happened when people treated administrative forms as if they were proof of a child's identity."

She folded her hands.

"By then, I had refused to approve the permanent name proposed for Child 28-B. I had refused to certify an identity without the original documents. I wasn't going to release another sealed record just because someone important wanted it."

Edward understood why Susan had resisted the requests.

She had not been in possession of the truth.

But she had recognised the danger of pretending that the truth had already been established.

"Where was the envelope kept?" he asked.

"In the cabinet."

"Was it locked?"

"Yes."

"Who had the key?"

"Initially, me. Later, the records department."

"Did you ever receive written instructions to destroy or return it?"

"No."

"Did you ever receive confirmation that the receiving authority had been established?"

"No."

Edward closed his notebook.

"Then we need to inspect the cabinet."

The cabinet stood in a restricted storage room on the hospital's lower administrative floor.

It was an old metal unit, painted grey, with a numbered seal attached to the front.

The department manager checked the inventory.

The seal number matched the retirement transfer record.

Susan examined it carefully.

"I don't recognise this seal."

"Was another one used when you transferred the cabinet?" Edward asked.

She nodded.

"Yes. I used a red paper seal. This is a later departmental seal."

"Was the original seal intact when the department received it?"

"I don't remember."

The manager consulted the transfer sheet.

"The cabinet was received with an intact outer lock. The seal was applied by our department after receipt."

Edward looked at the cabinet.

"Then we need the original transfer record."

The manager retrieved it from the archive.

The document showed that the cabinet had been received with two keys and an inventory of its contents.

One line was especially important.

**Restricted envelope — RC-18/FINAL — retained under former custodian's instruction.**

The cabinet had not merely been stored.

Its contents had been recorded.

Edward asked for the keys.

The manager produced one.

The second was missing.

"Who had the other key?" Jonathan asked.

"The transfer record says it was returned to the department."

"Is there a receipt?"

"No."

Edward looked at Susan.

"Did you keep a spare?"

"No."

The manager checked the seal again.

"We can open it under a supervised inventory procedure. The missing key is not required."

Edward considered the request.

"Before opening, I want photographs of the cabinet, the lock, and the seal. I also want a written record of everyone present."

The manager nodded.

Within twenty minutes, the formal inspection began.

The cabinet contained three shelves.

The first held old personnel files.

The second contained sealed envelopes and administrative correspondence.

The third held a small collection of restricted custody records.

Everything was dusty but orderly.

Susan stood beside the table, watching as the manager checked the contents against the original inventory.

Then he stopped.

"Here."

He carefully lifted a thick envelope.

Its outer surface was yellowed with age.

A faded reference was visible near the upper edge.

**RC-18/FINAL**

Below it was another marking.

**PART TWO — ORIGINAL SEAL INTACT.**

Susan covered her mouth.

"That's it."

Edward stepped closer.

The envelope was sealed with two separate strips.

One carried the old V-17 custody reference.

The other carried a hospital preservation stamp.

Neither appeared broken.

"Can you confirm this is the same envelope you received?" he asked.

Susan examined the handwriting.

"Yes. The reference is mine. I wrote it in my diary."

"Did you write anything else on it?"

"No."

Edward studied the second strip.

There was a handwritten instruction beneath the stamp.

**No release to originating family without receiving verification.**

Jonathan looked at him.

"That's the same condition again."

Edward nodded.

"Yes."

The manager turned the envelope slightly.

A small attachment was visible at the back.

It was a folded sheet, secured beneath the preservation seal.

Edward did not touch it.

"Photograph both sides."

The photographer complied.

The folded sheet bore a different reference.

**AV-18/RECIPIENT SCHEDULE.**

Edward felt his attention sharpen.

"Do we have any record of that reference?"

Jonathan searched the documents on his tablet.

"Not in Annex C. Not in the V-17 inventory. Not in the Holt correspondence."

Edward looked at the sealed envelope.

"Then it may be the missing schedule."

"Or an administrative cover sheet."

"Exactly."

The envelope was placed inside a new transparent evidence container.

The manager signed the custody transfer record.

Susan watched the process in silence.

When it was finished, she spoke.

"Mr. Edward?"

"Yes?"

"Does this mean the children can finally be identified?"

Edward looked at her.

"It means we may have found a document that was supposed to help identify them."

Susan's shoulders lowered slightly.

"That isn't the same thing."

"No," he said. "It isn't."

The next problem was legal authority.

Part Two had been held under a restriction that required receiving verification before release.

The restriction had never been satisfied.

The envelope was now located, but its contents could not simply be opened because the investigation had found it.

Edward's legal team reviewed the available custody documents.

The original instruction required two independent approvals:

- Medical records authority.
- Receiving identity custodian.

The first had been assigned to Dr Samuel Hale.

The second remained unidentified.

The record also required notification to the receiving family before the identity schedule could be released.

No completed notification existed.

"Could we obtain a court order?" Jonathan asked.

"We can apply for one."

"Then why not do it today?"

"Because the application needs to explain who has a legitimate interest in the material and why the original restrictions cannot be followed."

"Surely the biological mothers have that interest."

"Possibly. But the records were deliberately structured around receiving identities. We need to establish whether the restriction refers to the families who physically received the children, the families recorded as intended recipients, or the biological families."

Jonathan rubbed his forehead.

"After eighteen years, we're still arguing over the meaning of the word family."

Edward looked at the envelope.

"That is precisely why we cannot make another assumption."

At the Aurelius Estate, Victoria received Edward's latest report that evening.

She read the first page quickly.

Then she slowed down.

The final section described the discovery of Part Two.

For several seconds, she did not speak.

Alexander was sitting across from her.

"Is it the original?"

"Edward believes so."

"Has it been opened?"

"No."

Victoria placed the report on the table.

"Then we have found the document that everyone kept telling us did not exist."

"We have found a sealed envelope."

"Alexander."

"I know."

She looked at him.

"I am not asking you to promise that it contains our daughter's identity. I am asking you to understand what this means. For eighteen years, we were told that the records were incomplete because the relevant materials were unavailable. Now we know that at least one of those materials existed and was deliberately kept from us."

Alexander's jaw tightened.

"Edward has requested legal access."

"Good."

She picked up the report again.

There was a photograph of the envelope.

Victoria examined the faded writing.

**PART TWO — ORIGINAL SEAL INTACT.**

Her eyes moved to the small attached sheet.

**AV-18/RECIPIENT SCHEDULE.**

"Alexander?"

"Yes?"

"Do you recognise AV?"

He leaned forward.

"No."

She studied the reference again.

"Could it mean Aurelius Valmont?"

"That would be an assumption."

Victoria nodded slowly.

"I know."

She placed the photograph down.

"But somebody used both our family names in the same administrative system."

Alexander looked at the page.

For the first time, the possibility that the two families had been deliberately connected in a document became more concrete.

Yet neither of them knew why.

Meanwhile, Elena sat at her desk with a laptop, two notebooks, and a half-finished cup of tea.

The Singapore programme had sent her an email.

She read it once.

Then again.

**Application Update — Interview Assessment Completed**

She opened the message.

The programme thanked her for attending the interview and confirmed that her application had progressed to the final selection stage.

A decision would be communicated within two weeks.

Elena leaned back.

It wasn't an acceptance.

It wasn't a rejection.

But it was progress.

She opened her project presentation.

The final slide contained a short statement she had written herself:

*Good systems should not require expensive technology to preserve important information. They should make ordinary work easier, more transparent, and recoverable.*

She smiled faintly.

It was still a small project.

But it was something she had built because she wanted to build it.

A knock sounded at the door.

Nicholas entered carrying a plate.

"Food."

Elena looked at it.

"What is that?"

"Something edible."

"That description is not reassuring."

"It's from the kitchen."

"Then why are you presenting it like an experimental substance?"

Nicholas put the plate on her desk.

"Because you have been staring at that laptop for three hours."

"I was working."

"You were forgetting to eat."

Elena took a piece.

"Thank you."

Nicholas looked at the open presentation.

"Any news?"

"Final selection stage."

His expression brightened.

"That's good."

"It means they haven't rejected me yet."

"It means they haven't rejected you yet," he repeated solemnly. "A historic achievement."

Elena threw a small cushion at him.

He caught it.

"Mother will be pleased."

"I haven't told her yet."

"Then I won't tell her."

He paused.

"Can I?"

"No."

"Understood."

He turned toward the door, then stopped.

"Elena?"

"Yes?"

"Whatever happens with the programme, you should be proud of the work."

She looked at him.

For a moment, her usual reply did not come.

Then she smiled.

"I am."

Nicholas nodded and left.

Elena returned to her laptop.

She did not know that another family, somewhere beyond the Valmont world, was waiting for a document that might change the course of her life.

At the hospital, Edward was preparing the application for judicial access when his assistant brought him another message.

It was from the former records department.

The visitor who had attempted to collect Susan Bell's cabinet had not been Adrian Holt alone.

The security footage showed a second person waiting outside the building.

The image was unclear, but the individual had remained near the entrance for nearly forty minutes.

Edward opened the photograph.

A woman stood beside a parked vehicle.

Her face was partially obscured by the reflection on the glass.

But the object in her hand was unmistakable.

A brown leather case.

Edward enlarged the image.

The photograph was too blurred to establish her identity.

Still, he recognised the detail from Arthur Bell's account.

The same kind of case had appeared at V-17.

The same kind of case had been described by the nurse at Westbridge.

And now it had appeared again, outside the hospital where Part Two had been preserved.

Jonathan came to his desk.

"Do you think it's Marianne Cross?"

"I don't know."

"Then who?"

Edward looked at the photograph.

"I think we have been asking who carried the documents."

He closed the image.

"We should start asking who kept arranging for them to be collected."

A new message appeared on his screen.

It was from the hospital's security department.

The woman had returned that morning.

This time, she had left a sealed envelope at reception.

It was addressed to Edward.

There was no sender's name.

Only four words written across the front.

**DO NOT OPEN PART TWO.**

And beneath them, in smaller handwriting:

**You have not found the correct family.**
