# **Chapter 218 — The Authority That Approved the Instruction**

The request for CRA/49's authorisation record was returned at 8:51 a.m.

Not refused.

Not approved.

Returned.

Edward read the reason.

**The designation CRA/49 identifies a temporary decision authority exercised through the Restricted Continuity Review process. It does not identify an individual officeholder.**

Eleanor looked at the screen.

“So we have a decision without a named decision-maker.”

“We have a decision recorded under a temporary authority designation.”

“That's not much better.”

“No.”

The response contained an additional instruction.

To identify the authorising officer, they would need the original delegation schedule and the meeting record for the Day-49 continuity review.

Edward requested both.

He also requested the list of documents presented to the review authority before the instruction was approved.

Eleanor watched him complete the request.

“Are you expecting Dr. Hale's objection to be there?”

“I expect it should have been.”

“And if it wasn't?”

“Then we need to know why.”

At Meridian, Helen Ward opened the Day-49 meeting register.

The review had been conducted by a small governance panel.

Its purpose was to decide whether the consolidated authority descriptions could be used in subsequent administrative summaries.

The meeting had lasted twenty-three minutes.

The attendees were listed by function rather than name.

- Governance Secretariat representative.
- Restricted Appointments Desk representative.
- Records Migration Coordinator.
- Continuity Review Authority.

There was no formal identity review.

No medical representative.

No family representative.

No receiving institution.

Helen opened the agenda.

**Item 3: Continued use of consolidated authority descriptions.**

The supporting papers included the six-reference index, Daniel Mercer's consolidated descriptions, and a short note about the migration schedule.

Dr. Hale's Day-48 objection was not listed.

Helen checked the attachments.

It was absent.

She searched the incoming correspondence register.

The objection had been received by the Governance Secretariat at 11:04 a.m. on Day 48.

The Day-49 meeting had taken place at 2:30 p.m.

There had been more than a day to circulate the objection.

But the meeting pack did not include it.

Helen checked the circulation list.

The objection had been routed to the Restricted Appointments Desk.

It had not been added to the meeting papers.

She wrote a note.

**The issuing administrator's scope objection was received before the Day-49 decision but was not included in the recorded supporting papers.**

Then she searched the decision record.

The approval had been signed electronically.

The signature was valid.

The authorising officer's identity was held in the delegation schedule.

Helen requested access.

At 10:15, Elena's pilot preparation meeting began.

The six applications had not yet been received.

The programme team was still testing the workflow with sample records.

Elena had prepared a simple demonstration of what would happen when a reviewer tried to access an application outside their assigned group.

The system denied access.

Mr. Lim frowned.

“What if the reviewer is covering for someone who is absent?”

“Then an authorised administrator can reassign the application.”

“Can the administrator simply open it?”

“Not without the required permission.”

“Even if the programme coordinator asks?”

“The coordinator would need the relevant authority.”

Mr. Lim smiled.

“You are making our lives difficult.”

“Only where the rules are unclear.”

Dr. Ong looked at the screen.

“Could the system record who authorised a reassignment?”

“Yes. It records the authorising account, the reason, the previous assignment, and the new assignment.”

“Could the administrator change the reason afterward?”

“No. A correction can be added, but the original reason remains in the audit history.”

Dr. Ong nodded.

“That is the sort of control I would want in a live pilot.”

Elena switched to the next demonstration.

A sample application had been marked as received.

Its identity verification was still pending.

A reviewer attempted to move it to the decision stage.

The system blocked the action.

A message appeared:

**Required verification incomplete. Authorised exception required.**

Mr. Lim pointed at the screen.

“Could we allow an exception?”

“Yes, if the programme's rules permit one. But the system would require the identity of the approving authority and the reason.”

“And if nobody has authority to approve an exception?”

“Then the application stays where it is.”

Dr. Ong smiled.

“Good answer.”

Elena returned to her seat.

She was beginning to feel more confident.

The platform was no longer just a collection of features she had designed.

It was becoming a practical system with clear limits.

At 11:42, Helen received access to the delegation schedule.

She opened the relevant page.

The final authorising officer for CRA/49 had been appointed under a temporary governance delegation.

The appointment was valid for a limited period.

Its holder could approve continuity instructions concerning administrative descriptions.

However, the delegation contained a restriction:

**The holder must not approve a consolidated description where an originating authority has disputed its scope, unless the dispute is expressly recorded and the original instrument remains controlling.**

Helen read it again.

The Day-49 instruction had omitted the dispute.

The original instrument had not been declared controlling.

The delegation had been exceeded.

She checked the appointment name.

It was not Daniel Mercer.

It was not Dr. Samuel Hale.

It was not anyone from the Restricted Appointments Desk.

The appointment had been issued to a senior officer in the Governance Secretariat.

**Margaret Ellison.**

Helen checked the effective dates.

The appointment was valid on Day 49.

The electronic signature matched the recorded authorisation.

She had the answer.

But she also had a second question.

Why had the objection not been included in the meeting papers?

The routing record showed that the Restricted Appointments Desk had received it.

The desk's representative had attended the meeting.

The objection had not been circulated.

Helen prepared the disclosure for Edward.

Edward read the report at 1:06 p.m.

He did not react immediately.

Eleanor took the pages.

“Margaret Ellison?”

“Yes.”

“Not Margaret Voss.”

“Correct. Different person.”

She read the delegation restriction.

“Her authority expressly required the dispute to be recorded.”

“And it wasn't.”

“Was she given Dr. Hale's objection?”

“The meeting pack doesn't show it.”

“Does that mean she never saw it?”

“No.”

Edward opened the routing history.

The objection had been received by the Governance Secretariat.

It had been routed to the Restricted Appointments Desk.

The desk's representative had attended the review.

But there was no record confirming whether the objection had been forwarded to Margaret Ellison personally.

Eleanor looked up.

“Then we cannot say she knowingly ignored it.”

“Not yet.”

“But she approved an instruction that failed to meet the conditions of her delegation.”

“Yes.”

“Even if the information was withheld from her.”

“Which would change how we understand her responsibility.”

Edward placed the report beside the Day-49 instruction.

“We need the circulation record and her own account.”

Margaret Ellison agreed to a formal interview three days later.

She arrived with a legal adviser and a folder containing copies of the relevant governance papers.

She was in her early sixties, spoke quietly, and appeared unsurprised by the subject of the meeting.

“I understand you're investigating the consolidated descriptions,” she said.

“Yes,” Edward replied.

“Then I expect you have seen the delegation restrictions.”

“We have.”

“And you have seen the Day-49 instruction.”

“Yes.”

Margaret folded her hands.

“I approved it.”

“Did you review the original authority instruments?”

“I reviewed the consolidated index and the supporting summary.”

“Did you review FAW/06?”

“I reviewed its description.”

“Not the original appointment?”

“No.”

Edward waited.

Margaret continued.

“The review was intended to prevent inconsistent terminology during a system migration. I was not conducting an identity review.”

“Were you aware that Dr. Hale had disputed the description of FAW/06?”

Margaret looked at her adviser.

Then she answered.

“I do not recall seeing his objection before the meeting.”

“Were you aware that your delegation required unresolved scope disputes to be recorded?”

“Yes.”

“Why was the dispute not recorded?”

“Because the supporting papers described the matter as a terminology reconciliation, not a disputed authority question.”

Eleanor made a note.

Edward asked, “Who prepared that description?”

“Daniel Mercer.”

“Did you ask whether the original issuing administrator had been consulted?”

“I was told the relevant departments had been included in the migration review.”

“Was that statement documented?”

“Not in the meeting minutes.”

“Did you ask to see the original appointment?”

“No.”

“Why not?”

Margaret looked down briefly.

“The migration schedule was under pressure. There were hundreds of records to reconcile. I relied on the consolidated descriptions prepared by the migration coordinator.”

Edward did not interrupt.

Margaret continued.

“I should have required the original instruments where the wording affected authority. My delegation was clear.”

“Did you intend to expand the witness's authority?”

“No.”

“Did you intend to allow the witness to verify infant identities?”

“Absolutely not.”

“Did you intend to allow the consolidated descriptions to override the original appointments?”

“No.”

Edward placed the Day-49 instruction in front of her.

“Then what did you understand this sentence to mean?”

He pointed to the instruction.

**Use the consolidated descriptions in subsequent administrative summaries unless the issuing authority submits a formal correction.**

Margaret read it.

“I understood that the descriptions were administrative equivalents, not replacements for the underlying authority.”

“Was that distinction stated in the instruction?”

“No.”

“Was it stated in the meeting minutes?”

“No.”

“Was it stated in the consolidated file?”

Margaret paused.

“No.”

Edward closed his notebook.

“Then the instruction did not preserve that distinction.”

Margaret nodded slowly.

“No. It did not.”

Eleanor asked the next question.

“Did you later learn that Dr. Hale had submitted an objection?”

“Yes.”

“When?”

“After the Day-49 review.”

“Did you ask for the original appointment?”

“Yes.”

“Was the instruction withdrawn?”

“No.”

“Why not?”

Margaret's expression tightened.

“The migration had already begun. I was told that changing the descriptions would disrupt several linked administrative processes.”

“Who told you that?”

“The Restricted Appointments Desk.”

“Did they identify which processes would be disrupted?”

“They provided a list.”

“Did you review it?”

“I reviewed the summary.”

“Did you check whether the processes actually depended on the expanded description?”

“No.”

Eleanor looked at Edward.

There it was again.

A decision made on the basis of a summary, followed by another summary explaining why the first decision should remain in place.

Margaret continued.

“I asked for a correction to be considered after migration.”

“Was it?”

“I don't know.”

“Did you issue a formal withdrawal?”

“No.”

“Did you suspend the instruction?”

“No.”

“Did you notify the affected originating authorities?”

“No.”

The room fell silent.

Edward asked one final question.

“Do you accept that the Day-49 instruction should not have been issued in that form?”

Margaret looked at the document.

“Yes.”

“Do you accept that it was used in later administrative summaries?”

“Yes.”

“Do you accept that those summaries could make limited appointments appear broader than they were?”

“Yes.”

She raised her eyes.

“But I did not know that anyone would use the descriptions to establish a receiving identity or a family relationship.”

Edward nodded.

“We have not concluded that you did.”

Margaret's shoulders lowered slightly.

“But you did approve the instruction,” Eleanor said.

“Yes.”

“And you did not withdraw it after learning that the original issuing administrator had objected.”

“No.”

“That is still your responsibility.”

Margaret did not argue.

“I understand.”

After the interview, Edward and Eleanor reviewed the evidence.

Margaret Ellison had authorised the Day-49 instruction.

She had not reviewed the original FAW/06 appointment.

She had relied on Daniel Mercer's consolidated description.

She had later learned of Dr. Hale's objection.

She had not withdrawn the instruction.

But she had not claimed to possess authority over identity, placement, or family allocation.

Her responsibility was administrative and governance-related.

It was serious enough without inventing anything beyond it.

Eleanor closed the interview notes.

“So Daniel prepared the descriptions, Margaret approved their continued use, and the Restricted Appointments Desk presented the matter as a terminology issue.”

“Yes.”

“Who decided to leave Dr. Hale's objection out of the meeting papers?”

“We don't know.”

“Could it have been Daniel?”

“Possibly.”

“Could it have been the desk?”

“Possibly.”

Edward opened the circulation record again.

There was a small entry beneath the receipt stamp.

**Correspondence disposition: Retain at originating desk.**

No individual name.

No explanation.

The objection had been received.

It had been routed.

And then it had remained at the desk.

Eleanor looked at the entry.

“Someone decided it wasn't necessary to circulate.”

“Or someone treated it as an ordinary correspondence item instead of a formal scope objection.”

“Does that distinction matter?”

“It matters to intent. It doesn't change the fact that the objection never reached the decision papers.”

Edward requested the Restricted Appointments Desk's internal handling record.

That evening, Elena received the final written pilot conditions.

The six-application pilot would begin the following month.

The programme had accepted her proposed authority structure.

A separate officer would confirm delivery.

Reviewers would not approve awards.

Administrators would not alter completed assessments.

Any exception would require a named approving authority and a recorded reason.

Elena read the document carefully.

Then she noticed one missing item.

There was no procedure for what would happen if the programme's own authority structure changed during the pilot.

She sent a message to Ms. Tan.

**Elena:** Could we include a requirement that any change to reviewer or decision authority is recorded before it takes effect? Existing permissions should not automatically transfer to a replacement person.

The response came ten minutes later.

**Ms. Tan:** Agreed. Please add that to the final pilot specification.

Elena added it.

She looked at the completed document.

For the first time, she felt that the project was moving beyond an academic exercise.

It was becoming something real.

Something people might depend on.

She saved the final version.

Then she sent Isabelle a brief update.

**Elena:** The limited pilot has been approved. The foundation meeting next week can go ahead, but I will only be presenting the platform, not offering a commitment to implement it.

Isabelle replied:

**Isabelle:** Understood. I'll make sure the trustees know that before the meeting.

Elena put her phone down.

She was glad Isabelle understood.

She did not want her work to become another family arrangement made on her behalf.

The Restricted Appointments Desk's internal handling record arrived the next morning.

It was a short extract.

The Day-48 objection had been received at 11:04.

At 11:19, a staff member had classified it as:

**Originating administrator correspondence — informational.**

At 11:32, it had been routed to the migration coordination folder.

At 12:06, a note had been added:

**No change to consolidated wording pending completion of continuity review.**

At 1:44, the objection had been marked:

**Retained — no circulation required.**

Edward read the last entry.

Eleanor leaned closer.

“Who marked it?”

The record identified a shared desk account.

No individual operator was recorded.

Edward opened the next page.

A manual handling instruction had been attached to the folder.

It was not signed by Daniel Mercer.

It was not signed by Margaret Ellison.

It was not signed by the governance officer who had received the objection.

It carried a different designation.

**Restricted Appointments Desk — Supervisory Instruction.**

The instruction read:

**Do not circulate originating authority objections during a continuity review unless the objection is accompanied by a formal withdrawal request or a direct challenge to the review authority.**

Edward stared at it.

Dr. Hale had not asked to withdraw the appointment.

He had asked for the inaccurate description to be corrected.

The instruction had created a rule under which his objection would not reach the decision-maker unless he challenged the decision-maker directly.

But the decision-maker had not yet issued the instruction.

Eleanor looked at him.

“That is circular.”

“Yes.”

“Who issued the supervisory instruction?”

Edward turned to the final page.

The name field was blank.

Only an authorisation reference remained.

**SDA/47-02.**

He opened the archive index.

The reference was not a person.

It was a supervisory delegation.

And its effective period began on Day 47.

Edward looked at Eleanor.

“Someone had authority over how objections were handled before Margaret approved the Day-49 instruction.”

Eleanor read the date.

“Two days before.”

He nodded.

The investigation had found the missing step.

But not yet the person behind it.

And the next record would have to establish whether the instruction was an administrative safeguard badly designed—or a deliberate way to keep inconvenient objections away from the people making decisions.
