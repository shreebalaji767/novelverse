# Chapter 163 — The Operator Behind the Shared Account

The first audit report arrived at 08:35.

It was incomplete.

Edward knew that before he reached the final page.

The report identified the three status changes he had requested, but the operator fields contained only the shared account name.

**FA-ADMIN / REVIEW DESK.**

No individual username.

No workstation identifier.

No approval signature.

No named supervisor.

Jonathan placed the report on Edward's desk.

"The archive says the original operator information was not retained in the migrated system."

Edward looked up.

"Not retained or deleted?"

"The report says unavailable."

"That isn't an answer."

"I agree."

Edward turned to the first event.

Day 144.

10:18.

R-17 recorded as having declined appointment.

The second event was the day-150 category mapping revision.

The third was the day-152 responsible custodian entry.

Three consequential changes.

One shared account.

And no individual attribution.

"Find the original system," Edward said.

Jonathan nodded.

"The migration backups?"

"Not just the backups. The access-control configuration, the terminal records, the supervisor logs, everything that existed before the system was transferred."

"What if the records were never retained?"

"Then we establish that independently. We don't accept a summary written by the same office whose actions we're examining."

The original system had been operated from a records facility that no longer handled active correspondence.

Its equipment had been transferred to an archive contractor after the administrative migration.

Edward requested a preservation inspection.

The contractor agreed to provide the original access logs, subject to confirmation of authority.

That confirmation took most of the morning.

At 12:10, a message arrived.

**ACCESS APPROVED — HISTORICAL AUDIT MATERIAL ONLY.**

The archive would provide the available records but would not permit the investigation team to modify the original system.

Edward accepted.

He wanted the records preserved exactly as they were.

The first recovered document was the shared-account access schedule.

It listed six authorised users.

Four had already appeared in the investigation.

- Charles Vale.
- Peter Ashford.
- Martin Keane.
- Daniel Mercer.

Two additional users were listed under restricted administrative roles.

- A senior Family Allocation Review supervisor.
- An external systems auditor.

Their names were held in a separate personnel register.

Edward examined the permissions.

The shared account had been created to process documents that required restricted administrative handling.

It could receive correspondence, update routing fields, maintain preservation statuses and prepare summaries.

It could not independently approve personal representation appointments.

It could not verify identities.

It could not authorise a receiving authority.

And it could not record personal consent.

Edward looked at Jonathan.

"Then the account had no authority to do what the ledger suggests."

"Correct."

"Who could approve changes made through it?"

"Only a named supervisor with a separate authorisation credential."

"Was that credential used?"

Jonathan opened the audit index.

"That's what we're trying to establish."

The original terminal records arrived in two encrypted archive packages.

The first contained access times.

The second contained system events.

Edward opened the day-144 entry.

10:12 — shared account accessed.

10:14 — R-17 correspondence opened.

10:18 — status changed.

10:21 — replacement search initiated.

10:29 — summary distributed.

He stopped.

"The correspondence was opened before the status was changed."

Jonathan leaned closer.

"Yes."

"Can we identify which document was opened?"

"The system event references the day-137 request."

Edward returned to the original letter.

The request had explicitly stated that R-17 did not accept the proposed appointment and did not authorise anyone to treat temporary preservation access as acceptance.

It had also asked for the original procedure.

The day-144 summary had converted that request into a declined appointment.

Edward examined the timing.

"Someone read the objection."

"Or accessed it."

"That's a distinction we'll preserve. But the system records an opening event."

Jonathan nodded.

Edward looked at the next line.

10:18.

The status change had taken four minutes after the document was opened.

That was not an automatic migration event.

Someone had selected the status field and entered the new description.

"Find the supervisor credential."

The supervisor register identified two possible approval routes.

One belonged to Family Allocation Review.

The other belonged to Vale Private Counsel.

Both had been authorised to supervise shared-account operations during the original administrative arrangement.

The day-144 event contained no supervisor approval reference.

The day-150 event did.

The day-152 event did not.

Edward compared them.

"Why does the category mapping revision have a supervisor reference when the other two do not?"

Jonathan checked the event codes.

"The day-150 change was classified as a configuration update. It required a separate approval."

"Who approved it?"

The register showed:

**SUPERVISOR CREDENTIAL: F-02.**

No name appeared in the summary.

Edward asked Jonathan to retrieve the personnel record.

F-02 belonged to Helen Sloane, a senior administrative supervisor at Family Allocation Review.

She had left the organisation two years earlier.

Her former office confirmed that she had been responsible for supervising document classification and system configuration.

Edward contacted her through her current legal representative.

She agreed to an interview.

Helen Sloane arrived with a notebook and a printed copy of the day-150 configuration request.

She looked at the first page.

"I remember approving a mapping change."

"Do you remember what it was supposed to do?"

"Preserve the relationship between temporary custody records and the continuity register."

"Was it supposed to create personal representation authority?"

"No."

Edward showed her the revised wording.

*Where the previous representative is recorded as declined, the current document custodian may be retained as the responsible continuity authority until replacement appointment.*

Helen read it.

"That isn't what I approved."

"Did you see this wording?"

"No."

"Then what did you approve?"

She opened her notebook.

"The request described a change to the custody category. It said the system was losing the responsible person when records moved between offices. I approved retaining the physical custodian reference."

"Did the request mention R-17?"

"No."

"Did it mention personal representation?"

"No."

Edward placed the configuration history before her.

"The system used your credential to approve this mapping."

Helen examined the technical description.

"This is not the same change."

"Was your credential used without your knowledge?"

"I can't say that yet."

"Why not?"

"Because I need to see the original approval screen and the exact configuration package. I signed off on a technical change, not a legal authority."

Edward nodded.

"Would you be willing to provide your original approval records?"

"Yes."

She looked at the revised wording again.

"But I want one thing recorded."

"What?"

"I did not approve the appointment of Charles Vale."

The original approval package was recovered that afternoon.

Helen had been correct.

The request she approved was titled:

**TEMPORARY CUSTODY REFERENCE RETENTION.**

It contained no mention of R-17.

It did not refer to a declined appointment.

It did not authorise personal representation.

It simply instructed the system to retain the identity of the person physically responsible for documents when those documents were transferred between administrative offices.

The approval had been valid for that limited purpose.

But the configuration package later installed in the system contained an additional mapping.

**TEMPORARY CUSTODY → PERSONAL REPRESENTATION CONTINUITY.**

The mapping had been added after Helen's approval.

There was no second approval.

Edward looked at the technical record.

"Who added the additional mapping?"

Jonathan examined the change history.

"The modification was submitted under the shared account."

"Who accessed it?"

"That's not in the summary."

Edward pointed to the time.

"Then we go back to the original terminal log."

The terminal log contained a device reference.

**FR-06 / ADMINISTRATIVE WORKSTATION.**

The workstation had been located in the Family Allocation Review office.

Its access badge record showed three entries within the relevant hour.

One belonged to a junior records clerk.

One belonged to the office's systems technician.

The third was recorded under a temporary supervisor credential.

**S-11.**

Edward looked at Jonathan.

"Who held S-11?"

"The personnel register is restricted."

"Request it."

The response came from the former Family Allocation Review office.

S-11 belonged to a temporary compliance officer named **Sophie Grant**.

She had been assigned to the office during the final phase of the administrative migration.

Her role involved reviewing unresolved records, checking completion status and preparing files for transfer.

She had not been authorised to approve personal representation appointments.

She had, however, been authorised to access the shared administrative account.

Edward requested an interview.

Sophie agreed.

Sophie arrived the following morning.

She was in her early thirties and carried a thin folder.

Edward showed her the day-150 configuration record.

She studied it for several seconds.

"I remember the migration."

"Did you modify this category mapping?"

"I may have worked on the continuity records."

"Did you add the personal representation category?"

"I don't remember the exact wording."

Edward placed the original technical request beside the final configuration.

"Your workstation was used during the modification."

Sophie looked at the terminal reference.

"That was a shared workstation."

"Your badge was recorded entering the room."

"I was assigned to the office."

"Did you use the shared account?"

"Possibly."

Edward waited.

Sophie closed the folder.

"I need to be careful. I processed a large number of migration records. I did not appoint anyone."

"Did you change R-17's status?"

"No."

"Did you change Charles Vale's authority?"

"No."

"Did you prepare the day-144 summary?"

"I may have assisted with the review."

Edward looked at her.

"Assisted how?"

"I checked whether the records were complete enough to transfer."

"Was R-17's appointment complete?"

"No."

"Was the original procedure delivered?"

"No."

"Was the identity verified?"

"No."

"Then why was the status changed to declined?"

Sophie looked at the day-144 entry.

"I don't know."

Edward slid the original R-17 request toward her.

"Did you read this?"

She looked at the document.

"Yes."

The answer came too quickly.

Edward waited.

Sophie corrected herself.

"I mean, I may have seen it."

"Did you read the sentence stating that the person did not accept appointment and did not authorise anyone to act on their behalf?"

Sophie lowered her eyes.

"I remember the request for the procedure."

"That wasn't my question."

She remained silent.

The interview continued for another hour.

Sophie maintained that she had not appointed Charles and had not authorised any personal representation.

She also said she had not independently changed R-17's status.

But she remembered receiving an instruction to clear unresolved entries before migration.

"Who gave you that instruction?" Edward asked.

"It came through the review desk."

"From whom?"

"I don't know."

"Was it written?"

"There was a list."

"Do you still have it?"

Sophie hesitated.

"I might."

Edward asked her to locate it.

She agreed to check her personal archive.

Before leaving, she turned back.

"Mr Valmont?"

"Yes?"

"There's something you should understand about the migration."

Edward waited.

"Some entries were marked complete because the receiving system required a status. If we left them unresolved, the file would not transfer."

"Then they should have remained unresolved."

"I know that now."

"Did you know it then?"

Sophie looked at the door.

"We were told that the continuity categories were temporary."

"Temporary categories still need accurate descriptions."

She nodded.

But she did not offer anything more.

That evening, Jonathan received a message from Sophie.

She had found the list.

It was a spreadsheet printed on paper, with handwritten corrections.

The title was:

**DAY-144 TO DAY-152 — MIGRATION COMPLETION REVIEW.**

The first section listed records requiring action.

R-17 appeared on line seventeen.

The original status was:

**APPOINTMENT NOT COMPLETED — PROCEDURE NOT DELIVERED.**

The requested migration status was:

**REPRESENTATIVE DECLINED — REPLACEMENT REQUIRED.**

A handwritten instruction appeared in the margin.

*Use the final status where the nominee has not confirmed acceptance by the review deadline.*

Edward read it.

"Who wrote this?"

Sophie had added a note.

*I believe this handwriting belongs to the review coordinator. I cannot confirm.*

Jonathan looked at the line.

"Was there a review deadline?"

Edward searched the original correspondence.

There was no evidence that R-17 had been given a deadline to accept.

There was no record that the original procedure had been delivered.

There was no verified notice explaining that failure to respond would be treated as a refusal.

Edward placed the spreadsheet beside the day-137 request.

Someone had created a deadline inside the administrative process.

Then they had used the absence of a response to justify a status the individual had never agreed to.

The final page of the spreadsheet contained a distribution list.

Six offices had received the migration instructions.

One entry stood out.

**V-17 — PERSONAL CUSTODY.**

Beside it was a handwritten note:

*Do not return unresolved R-17 status to originating office. Continuity action to remain within current administrative route.*

Edward read it twice.

"That is an instruction to keep the status inside the same system that created it."

Jonathan nodded.

"And not allow the originating office to review the original objection."

Edward looked at the handwriting.

It was not the same as the anonymous notes found on the day-137 and day-155 correspondence.

The letter forms were different.

The pressure was heavier.

The words were more tightly spaced.

Dr Samuel Reed's preliminary comparison later confirmed that the handwriting did not match the anonymous correspondence writer.

It also did not match Daniel Mercer's handwriting.

The writer remained unidentified.

At home, Elena was preparing a short presentation for her academic programme.

Her platform had reached a stage where she could demonstrate the complete grant application process.

She had added a small warning beside incomplete applications.

**No decision can be inferred from missing information.**

Clara read it over video call.

"That's your favourite sentence now."

"It keeps turning out to be useful."

"You've added a lot of rules."

"They're not rules for the sake of rules. They're safeguards."

Clara pointed to the application history.

"What happens if a reviewer forgets to enter a decision?"

"The application remains under review. The system sends a reminder, but it doesn't automatically mark it declined."

"What if the applicant doesn't respond?"

"Then we record that the information hasn't been received. We don't pretend they made a decision."

Clara smiled.

"You're going to make the most stubborn grant platform in the world."

"Good."

Elena saved the latest version.

A message from Isabelle appeared.

*The foundation's review team wants to test your distinction between an incomplete application and a declined application. Could you prepare a sample workflow?*

Elena typed back.

*Yes. But I want the test to include a case where the applicant never receives the request for additional information.*

Isabelle replied:

*That's probably the most important case.*

Elena returned to her presentation.

She had no idea that, thousands of miles away, an investigation was uncovering an administrative process built around precisely the distinction she was trying to protect.

Edward spent the next morning examining the migration completion list.

The handwritten instruction had not been signed.

But the spreadsheet contained a file reference.

**MIGRATION COORDINATION — M-12.**

Jonathan located the corresponding folder.

It contained a set of instructions issued before the day-144 status changes.

Most were ordinary.

Some concerned document formatting.

Others concerned the transfer of restricted records.

One instruction, dated day 143, was particularly unusual.

*Where an appointment remains incomplete at the point of migration, the current administrative custodian shall be retained as continuity authority until the receiving office completes its review.*

There was no mention of R-17.

No reference to Charles.

No explanation of what constituted an incomplete appointment.

The instruction had been distributed to Family Allocation Review, V-17 and Vale Private Counsel.

Edward examined the approval field.

It carried a reference to a meeting held on day 142.

The meeting minutes were missing.

Only the attendance sheet remained.

Five people had attended.

Helen Sloane.

Daniel Mercer.

Martin Keane.

Sophie Grant.

And one person identified only as:

**EXTERNAL ADVISER — C.V.**

Edward looked at Jonathan.

"Charles Vale?"

"Possibly."

"Is there a signature?"

"No. The attendance sheet only records initials."

Edward studied the entry.

C.V.

Charles Vale had been present at a meeting where the migration continuity instruction was discussed.

That did not establish that he wrote it, approved it or knew how it would be applied.

But it placed him in the room before the day-144 status change.

Edward turned to the meeting's final column.

**FOLLOW-UP RESPONSIBILITY.**

Four names had been entered.

The fifth line was blank.

Yet beside it, someone had written:

*Original procedure to remain under existing custody until personal authority is confirmed.*

Edward recognised the handwriting.

It was the same anonymous writer who had repeatedly insisted that the original procedure must not be bypassed.

He stared at the page.

The anonymous writer had attended the meeting.

They had known the migration plan.

They had known the original procedure was still undelivered.

And they had written an instruction that appeared to oppose the very continuity arrangement that the meeting later produced.

Edward placed the attendance sheet beside the day-144 summary.

"Jonathan."

"Yes?"

"Find out who the fifth attendee was."

Jonathan looked at the blank line.

"There is no name."

"Then find the original meeting invitation."

Edward tapped the handwritten instruction.

"Someone in that room knew the appointment was incomplete before the system recorded it as declined."

He paused.

"And someone else knew exactly how to turn that incomplete status into an active authority."

The two people might have been working together.

Or they might have been trying to stop each other.

For the first time, Edward had a reason to believe the administrative records contained evidence of a disagreement that had begun before the false appointment was created.
