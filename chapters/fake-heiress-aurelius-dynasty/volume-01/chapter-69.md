# **Chapter 69 — The Name That Never Became Permanent**

Edward read the residential review again.

Then he read it a third time.

The document was only four pages long, but its implications were considerably larger than its size suggested.

At the top was a reference number.

**MCC-RR/092-28B.**

Below it were the details of a female child transferred from Greenfield Children's House to Meridian Children's Medical Centre on the ninety-second day after the recorded births.

The child's registered name was blank.

Her temporary reference was 28-B.

Her receiving representative was Margaret Voss.

And under the section marked *Proposed Residential Name*, someone had written:

**Elena.**

The final instruction was equally clear.

**Do not enter this name into the permanent identity register until the originating family confirms.**

Edward placed the document beside the beneficiary schedule.

The dates matched.

The reference matched.

The receiving representative matched.

For the first time, he had a direct connection between the child Margaret had monitored and the proposed name Elena.

But he still did not know whether that child was the Elena currently living at the Valmont Estate.

That distinction was becoming increasingly important.

He called Jonathan.

"Did Meridian provide the supporting documents?"

"Not yet. They have the review, but the original attachment is missing."

"What attachment?"

"The document that supposedly established the proposed name."

"Did they say who submitted it?"

"Charles Vale's office."

Edward looked at the signature.

"Then we need to know what Charles used to support the name."

"I'll request the original submission record."

"And Jonathan?"

"Yes?"

"Ask them whether the child was ever entered into the permanent register under another name."

There was a pause.

"Do you think she was?"

"I don't know."

Edward looked at the final instruction again.

"That's what we're trying to establish."

Meridian Children's Medical Centre had changed considerably over eighteen years.

The original building had been replaced by a larger medical complex, and its administrative departments had been reorganised several times.

However, its archival records had survived.

The institution's current records director, Dr. Helen Morris, had agreed to meet Edward and Jonathan after receiving their formal request.

She greeted them in a small conference room.

"I have reviewed the material you sent," she began. "The residential review appears to be genuine. The receiving stamp is consistent with our old records."

"Does that mean the child was admitted?" Edward asked.

"It means the document was processed by the institution. It does not, by itself, establish that the child was physically present."

Jonathan frowned.

"Then how do you know whether the child was there?"

"Through the admission register, medical observations, nursing records, and discharge documentation."

"Have you found those?"

"We found an admission entry associated with reference 28-B."

Edward leaned forward.

"What does it say?"

Dr. Morris opened a folder.

"Female child. Ninety-two days old. Transferred from Greenfield Children's House. Temporary name not assigned. Medical assessment completed on arrival."

"Was the child examined?"

"Yes."

"Is there a medical record?"

"There are several."

She placed a photocopy on the table.

The first page contained routine medical observations.

The child had been examined shortly after arrival. Her weight, temperature, feeding status, and general condition had been recorded.

There was no mention of a serious illness.

No emergency intervention.

No indication that she required prolonged hospitalisation.

Edward studied the page.

"How long did she remain here?"

"The admission record indicates a short stay."

"How short?"

"Four days."

"Where did she go afterward?"

Dr. Morris turned to the next page.

"That is where the record becomes incomplete."

Jonathan looked up.

"Why?"

"The discharge destination is listed as an external temporary residence. The receiving institution's name is missing from the surviving copy."

"Was there a person listed?"

Dr. Morris examined the page.

"Yes. A representative."

"Who?"

She turned the document toward them.

The name was clear.

**Margaret Voss.**

Jonathan stared at her.

"She was already the receiving representative."

"According to this record, yes."

Edward studied the dates.

Margaret had been responsible for monitoring the child at Greenfield.

The child had been transferred to Meridian.

After four days, the child had been discharged under Margaret's receiving authority.

But that still did not establish where she had gone.

"Did Margaret physically collect the child?" Edward asked.

Dr. Morris shook her head.

"The record does not say."

"Who signed the discharge?"

"The signature is missing from the surviving copy."

"Can you retrieve the original?"

"We are searching for it."

Edward looked at the medical observations.

"Was the proposed name Elena used during the admission?"

"No."

"Was it used in the nursing records?"

"Not in the documents we have found."

"Then the name existed only in the residential review?"

"That is correct."

Edward sat back.

The child had existed as an identifiable medical patient.

The name Elena had existed as an administrative proposal.

The connection between them had been made by someone outside the medical department.

He wanted to know why.

Dr. Morris returned with another folder.

"I also found a note attached to the residential review."

Edward immediately leaned forward.

"May we see it?"

"Yes."

She placed a photocopy on the table.

The note was short.

It had been written by a Meridian administrative officer.

**The proposed name has not been accepted as an identity. The child must continue to be recorded under the existing temporary reference until documentary confirmation is received.**

Below it was a handwritten addition.

**External representative informed.**

The initials were unfamiliar.

Jonathan examined them.

"Can you identify the person who wrote this?"

Dr. Morris checked the archival index.

"Possibly. The initials appear to belong to a records officer named Susan Bell."

"Is she still employed here?"

"No. She retired many years ago."

"Can you contact her?"

"I can try."

Edward pointed to the handwritten addition.

"Who was the external representative?"

"The note doesn't specify."

"Could it have been Margaret Voss?"

"It could have been. She was listed as the receiving representative."

"Could it have been Charles Vale?"

"Also possible. The note does not establish that."

Edward nodded.

He had learned not to force an answer out of an incomplete record.

Instead, he asked another question.

"Did the child have an identification bracelet?"

Dr. Morris checked the medical file.

"Yes. The admission record refers to a temporary hospital band."

"Number?"

"28-B."

"Was that the only identification used?"

"According to the surviving nursing sheets, yes."

Edward looked at Jonathan.

The medical centre had not adopted the proposed name.

The child remained 28-B.

Whatever happened after discharge, the name Elena had not become a confirmed hospital identity.

At the Valmont Estate, Isabelle was in Catherine's study.

She had been reviewing applications for the foundation's scholarship programme.

Catherine sat opposite her, checking a separate set of documents.

"Your comments on the eligibility criteria are useful," Catherine said.

Isabelle looked up.

"I thought the income threshold was too restrictive."

"It is."

"And the academic requirement should account for students who have improved significantly rather than only those who have consistently high grades."

Catherine nodded.

"That is a reasonable adjustment."

Isabelle returned to the document.

"I don't want the programme to become another award for students who already have every advantage."

"Neither do I."

Catherine studied her daughter for a moment.

"Would you like to help with the final review?"

Isabelle hesitated.

"Would that be appropriate?"

"You would not make the decision alone. There will be an independent panel."

"I don't want anyone thinking I received the position because I'm your daughter."

Catherine smiled faintly.

"Then we will make the selection process transparent."

Isabelle considered this.

"All right."

Catherine reached for the next document.

"You have been working very hard."

"I have a lot to catch up on."

"You don't need to catch up with anyone."

Isabelle looked down.

"I know."

But she did not entirely believe it.

She had spent much of her life feeling that every opportunity had to be earned before someone else took it away.

Being welcomed into the Valmont family had not erased that habit.

Nor had it made her certain of her place.

Catherine seemed to understand.

"Isabelle."

"Yes?"

"You are allowed to learn before you become good at something."

Isabelle gave a small smile.

"That sounds like something you would tell Elena."

"It is something I should tell both of you."

For a moment, Isabelle looked toward the study door.

"She's doing her university application."

"I know."

"She seemed nervous."

"She was."

"She didn't say much about it."

Catherine's expression softened.

"Elena has always preferred to handle difficult things quietly."

Isabelle returned to her papers.

"I hope she gets accepted."

"So do I."

There was no competition in Catherine's voice.

Only hope.

That afternoon, Edward received a message from Jonathan.

**Meridian found the original discharge register. The destination was not an institution.**

Edward immediately called him.

"What was it?"

Jonathan read the entry.

"A private residence."

"Whose?"

"That's the problem. The address is incomplete."

"How incomplete?"

"Only the district and building reference remain. The street name and house number are missing."

"Who authorised the discharge?"

"There's a signature."

Edward waited.

Jonathan read the name.

"Margaret Voss."

Edward closed his eyes briefly.

"Does the signature match hers?"

"Meridian is checking."

"Any other names?"

"Yes. There's a witness."

"Who?"

"Charles Vale."

Edward went silent.

The same man who had submitted the proposed name.

The same man who had prepared the financial instruction.

The same man who had concealed the correction to Victoria's false authorisation.

Now his name appeared on the discharge record for Child 28-B.

"Send me the page," Edward said.

"I already have."

Edward opened the attachment.

The handwriting was difficult to read, but the names were clear.

**Receiving representative: Margaret Voss.**

**Witness: Charles Vale.**

**Destination: Private residence — District 4, Building 19.**

The final line contained a note.

**Temporary arrangement. Further identity review required.**

Edward looked at the date.

The child had been discharged on the ninety-sixth day.

The proposed name Elena had been submitted four days earlier.

And the child had left Meridian without a permanent identity.

Margaret Voss did not deny signing the discharge record.

When Edward showed her the document the following morning, she examined it quietly.

"That is my signature."

"Did you collect the child?"

"No."

Edward looked at her.

"Your name is listed as the receiving representative."

"I was responsible for the arrangement. That does not mean I physically collected the child."

"Who did?"

"Charles Vale arranged the transport."

"Did you accompany him?"

"No."

"Then why did you sign?"

"Because I was required to acknowledge the receiving arrangement."

"Without seeing the child?"

Margaret looked uncomfortable.

"I had seen the child before the transfer."

"At Meridian?"

"At Greenfield."

"Did you see the child after the hospital discharge?"

"No."

Edward placed the discharge record on the table.

"Then you signed for a child you did not physically receive."

"I signed for the administrative responsibility."

"That is not what the form says."

Margaret looked at the document.

For several seconds, she said nothing.

Then she replied, "I know."

Edward waited.

"I made a mistake," she continued. "I believed the child would be delivered to the residence listed in the accompanying instruction. I believed Charles had arranged the receiving staff and that the institution would complete the identity verification."

"Why did you trust him?"

"Because he had been responsible for the arrangements from the beginning."

"That doesn't answer the question."

Margaret looked at him.

"Because I thought he was trying to protect the children."

Edward did not interrupt.

"He told me the original records were dangerous. He said the children could become targets of legal disputes if their identities were disclosed before the families were ready. He said temporary names were necessary."

"Did he say the name Elena?"

"Yes."

"What did he tell you about it?"

"That it was a proposed name for the child."

"Which child?"

"He didn't specify."

"Did you ask?"

"Yes."

"What did he say?"

Margaret's expression tightened.

"He said it was better not to know until the originating family confirmed."

Edward looked at her.

"That is the same instruction written on the review."

"Yes."

"Did you believe it?"

"I believed that the child needed protection. I did not believe that withholding identity indefinitely was acceptable."

"Then why did you continue?"

"Because I thought the process would eventually be corrected."

Edward leaned back.

"And when it wasn't?"

Margaret looked at the discharge record.

"By then, I no longer knew where the child was."

The room fell silent.

Edward returned to the Aurelius office with the new documents.

Alexander was already waiting.

Victoria sat beside him, and Adrian had brought the original financial archive.

Edward placed the Meridian discharge record on the table.

"Child 28-B was physically admitted to Meridian on the ninety-second day."

Alexander examined the page.

"Four days later, the child was discharged to a private residence."

"Yes."

"Under Margaret Voss's receiving authority."

"Yes."

"Who collected the child?"

"We don't know. Margaret says she did not."

Edward placed another page beside it.

"Charles Vale witnessed the discharge."

Alexander's jaw tightened.

"Again."

"Yes."

Victoria looked at the document.

"Was the proposed name Elena used?"

"Only as a residential proposal. Meridian did not accept it as a permanent identity."

"Why not?"

"Because the originating family had not confirmed it."

Alexander looked at the date.

"Which family?"

"Unknown."

Adrian examined the discharge destination.

"District 4, Building 19."

"Can we identify the residence?" Alexander asked.

"Not yet. The original address is incomplete."

Damien leaned forward.

"Could District 4 refer to a Northbridge property?"

"Possibly. We're checking."

Lucien looked at the documents.

"So the child left Meridian, and after that we have no confirmed location."

"Correct."

Julian had been listening from the doorway.

He stepped into the room.

"Was the child given another name?"

Edward looked at him.

"We don't know."

"Could she have been Elena?"

"She could have been. But we have no evidence proving that."

Julian looked at the discharge record.

"If someone had called her Elena, would that have made her the same person?"

"No," Edward said gently. "A name is not proof of identity."

Julian nodded slowly.

He understood the words.

He simply disliked them.

That evening, Edward received an unexpected call.

The caller introduced herself as Susan Bell.

She was the retired Meridian records officer whose initials appeared on the residential review.

"I understand you're investigating an old child placement," she said.

"Yes."

"I saw the document reference you sent."

"Do you remember it?"

"I remember the procedure."

Edward sat forward.

"What procedure?"

"The hospital received a request to use a proposed residential name. I refused to enter it permanently because the supporting document was incomplete."

"Who submitted it?"

"Charles Vale."

"Did he explain why the name had been chosen?"

"He said the child was expected to be reunited with a family under that name."

Edward's attention sharpened.

"Which family?"

"He didn't say."

"Did he identify the child?"

"No."

"Did he provide a birth certificate?"

"No."

"Any parental declaration?"

"No."

"Then why was the name proposed at all?"

Susan sighed.

"Because the residential institution wanted a name to use in its daily records. A temporary reference was difficult for staff and caregivers. They wanted something more personal."

Edward considered this.

"Did you believe the request was improper?"

"I believed it was premature."

"Did Charles Vale accept your decision?"

"No."

"What happened?"

"He asked me to enter the name anyway. I refused."

"Did he become angry?"

"Not particularly. He was very polite."

Susan paused.

"That was what made it uncomfortable."

Edward waited.

"He said the hospital was making a simple administrative matter unnecessarily difficult. He said the family had already agreed to the name and that the confirmation would arrive shortly."

"Did it?"

"No."

"Did anyone else contact you?"

"Yes."

"Who?"

Susan hesitated.

"A woman."

"Can you describe her?"

"Middle-aged, perhaps. Very well dressed. She arrived with a man from the legal office."

"Do you remember the man's name?"

"No. But I remember the woman."

"Why?"

"Because she was upset."

"About the proposed name?"

"No. About the child's identity."

Edward leaned closer.

"What did she say?"

Susan was quiet for a moment, as if searching through a memory she had not revisited in years.

"She asked whether the child had been registered as belonging to a particular family."

"Which family?"

Susan gave him a name.

"She asked whether the child had been registered as an Aurelius."

Edward stopped moving.

"Did you tell her?"

"I said no. The child had no confirmed family identity."

"What did she do?"

"She looked relieved."

Edward's fingers tightened around the phone.

"Did she give you her name?"

"Yes."

"Do you remember it?"

"I wrote it down in the old visitor register."

"Can you retrieve that register?"

"I still have a personal copy of the visitor index. The hospital allowed me to retain some of my administrative notes when I retired."

"Can you send me the entry?"

"I'll have to look for it."

"Please do."

Susan hesitated.

"Mr. Valmont?"

"Yes?"

"I remember something else."

"What?"

"The woman said the child should never be given a family name until the correct mother had been found."

Edward looked at the Meridian discharge record.

The proposed name Elena had not been confirmed.

The child's family identity had not been established.

And someone had specifically asked whether the child had been registered as an Aurelius.

"Did she say why?"

"No."

"Did she mention Victoria Aurelius?"

"No."

"Alexander?"

"No."

"Did she say she was related to the child?"

Susan paused.

"I don't remember."

Edward thanked her and ended the call.

The visitor register arrived the next morning.

Susan had found the entry.

It was a photocopy of a single page, dated ninety-three days after the recorded births.

The visitor's name was written in a neat hand.

**Isabella Vale.**

Edward stared at it.

He checked the date.

Then he checked the reference written beside the entry.

**MCC-RR/092-28B.**

Isabella had visited Meridian one day after the residential review was submitted.

The same child whose proposed name was Elena.

The same child Margaret Voss had monitored.

The same child Charles Vale had witnessed leaving the hospital.

Edward read the handwritten purpose of visit.

**To verify whether the child has been assigned an originating family identity.**

Below it was a brief note.

**Visitor informed that no identity has been confirmed.**

Edward looked at the final line.

It had been added in a different hand.

**Visitor requested that the child not be released under the Aurelius name.**

He slowly put the page down.

Isabella Vale had not merely visited Meridian.

She had specifically asked whether the child was being registered as an Aurelius.

And she had requested that the child not be released under that name.

But why would a woman who had been told her own baby had died be concerned about an unidentified child being given the Aurelius family name?

Edward looked again at the visitor's signature.

It was the same Isabella Vale who had signed the ninth-day instruction.

The same woman who had believed she had given birth to Infant A.

And the same woman whose maternity record remained unconnected to any confirmed receiving infant.

The investigation had just found another link.

But it had not yet established what that link meant.

Edward reached for his telephone.

He needed to speak to Isabella Vale.

This time, he would not ask her whether she had seen a photograph of a baby.

He would ask why, eighteen years ago, she had gone to Meridian Children's Medical Centre and requested that an unidentified child not be given the Aurelius name.
