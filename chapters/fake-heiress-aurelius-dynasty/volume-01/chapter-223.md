# **Chapter 223 — The Answer That Was Removed Without Being Changed**

Daniel Mercer did not refuse the interview.

He requested the questions in advance.

Edward agreed.

There was no reason to make the meeting confrontational before the relevant records had been established. Daniel had prepared the consolidated descriptions, but the investigation still needed to distinguish what he had written, what he had authorised, and what others had subsequently done.

At 8:40 a.m., Daniel arrived carrying a thin folder.

He placed it on the table.

“I've brought the original migration guidance.”

Edward glanced at it.

“Your copy?”

“My retained working copy. It may help clarify the wording.”

Eleanor sat opposite him.

“Does it include the Day-14 receiving restriction?”

Daniel opened the folder.

“No. That was not included in my working pack.”

“Did you know it existed?”

“I knew there were outstanding receiving reconciliation matters.”

“That isn't the same thing,” Edward said.

“No. It isn't.”

Edward opened the interview record.

“Let's begin with the classification rule.”

Daniel read the instruction.

**Receiving-route documents that do not contain a formal withdrawal or direct challenge to the migration authority may be classified as non-blocking for continuity purposes.**

He nodded.

“That was prepared during the migration.”

“By you?”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“The migration involved several active processes. Some correspondence concerned administrative corrections that did not require the migration itself to stop.”

“Did you distinguish between a correction to the migration and a restriction on the underlying receiving identity?”

Daniel looked at the wording.

“Not sufficiently.”

Edward placed the Day-14 receiving restriction beside it.

**Original identification material remains outstanding. Do not convert the proposed reference into a confirmed receiving identity.**

“Would you consider this a restriction on the underlying receiving identity?”

“Yes.”

“Would you consider it a direct challenge to the migration authority?”

Daniel paused.

“Not necessarily. It did not challenge the migration authority's power to migrate the administrative records.”

“Did it challenge the accuracy of a description that the migration was carrying forward?”

“Yes.”

“Then why was it classified as non-blocking?”

Daniel looked at the document again.

“The rule was designed to determine whether correspondence should interrupt migration. It was not designed to decide identity.”

“But the classification affected which documents appeared in the summary pack.”

“Yes.”

“Did you know that?”

“I knew the classification would determine routing.”

“Did you know the Day-14 restriction would be absent from the Day-28 summary pack?”

Daniel hesitated.

“I cannot say I knew that specific document would be excluded.”

“Did you review the Day-28 pack?”

“I reviewed the consolidated description.”

“Did you compare it with the original receiving response?”

“No.”

“Why not?”

Daniel leaned back.

“The migration process relied on the receiving desk's status information as it had been consolidated. I understood the outstanding reconciliation to be a final documentary step.”

Eleanor spoke.

“But the receiving desk had said identity remained unconfirmed.”

“I understand that now.”

“Did you understand it then?”

Daniel looked at her.

“I knew original identification material was outstanding. I did not appreciate that the later wording would be interpreted as confirmation of identity.”

Edward wrote that down.

“Your wording changed ‘not established’ into ‘recorded for continuity.’”

“It was intended to describe the administrative reference.”

“Then the Day-74 wording changed that into ‘established administrative references.’”

“I did not write that.”

“And the Day-92 wording said ‘confirmed for administrative continuity.’”

“I did not write that either.”

“But those later summaries cited your Day-61 version.”

Daniel nodded.

“Yes.”

“Did you correct the Day-61 wording after learning how it was being used?”

“No formal correction was issued.”

“Why?”

“I believed the phrase ‘pending final documentary reconciliation’ preserved the limitation.”

Eleanor pointed to the original receiving restriction.

“It did not preserve this limitation. It made the outstanding identity question sound like a routine administrative matter.”

Daniel did not answer immediately.

Then he said, “I accept that.”

The interview continued for another hour.

Daniel confirmed that he had prepared the consolidated descriptions under the migration framework.

He confirmed that the descriptions were intended to standardise administrative terminology.

He also confirmed that he had no authority to appoint a final identity examiner, determine biological relationships, or approve a receiving placement.

When asked whether he had deliberately removed the Day-14 restriction, he denied doing so.

The available audit record supported only that the document had been received, opened under a shared account, classified as non-blocking, and excluded from the later summary pack.

It did not identify the individual who performed each action.

Edward did not treat Daniel's denial as conclusive.

He also did not treat the missing operator identity as proof of deliberate concealment.

At the end of the meeting, Daniel made one further statement.

“There was a problem with the migration's completion criteria.”

Edward looked up.

“What problem?”

“The migration could be marked complete when the administrative references had been reconciled, even if the underlying identity review remained unresolved.”

“Who approved that?”

“The continuity review office.”

“Was that your recommendation?”

“I prepared the workflow description. I did not approve the completion criteria.”

“Did you object?”

Daniel shook his head.

“No.”

“Why not?”

“I thought the identity review would continue separately.”

Edward closed the interview record.

“Then we need the completion criteria and the authority under which they were approved.”

Daniel nodded.

“I expected you would ask for those.”

The completion criteria arrived that afternoon.

They were contained in a separate document.

**Migration Completion Standard — Day 45 Revision.**

The standard divided migration into two stages.

**Stage One:** Administrative reference migration.

**Stage Two:** Outstanding source reconciliation.

The first stage could be marked complete once the references had been entered into the receiving system, assigned to the relevant administrative categories, and acknowledged by the destination office.

The second stage was described as a continuing obligation.

At first glance, the distinction appeared reasonable.

A record could be migrated without the underlying identity being verified.

But the completion certificate used different wording.

**Migration completed. Receiving references established in destination system. Outstanding reconciliation retained for subsequent review.**

Eleanor read it.

“That's the same problem.”

Edward nodded.

“Administrative references were established. The certificate doesn't say the identities were established.”

“But later summaries treated those phrases as equivalent.”

“Yes.”

He checked the authorisation.

The standard had been approved by the Restricted Continuity Review under CRA/45.

The approving officer was Margaret Ellison.

Daniel had prepared the operational wording.

The distinction between administrative completion and identity verification had existed in the underlying standard.

But the completion certificate did not preserve that distinction clearly.

Edward added the certificate to the evidence list.

This was no longer simply a matter of one misleading summary.

The system had created several opportunities for a limited administrative result to be interpreted as a substantive conclusion.

Yet the original authority matrix remained unchanged.

No final identity authority had been appointed.

No original comparison had established a receiving identity.

The administrative system had moved ahead of the evidence.

At 5:05 p.m., Dr. Miriam Cole sent her final examination report.

The report contained no new identity conclusion.

It did, however, include a detailed account of the limitations of the original band material.

The incomplete marking could not be reconstructed reliably.

The custody gap could not be resolved from the available archive logs.

The Day-12 shared account entry remained unattributed.

Dr. Cole recommended that the band remain preserved as historical evidence, but that no further identity conclusion be drawn from it unless additional original material became available.

Edward read the recommendation.

He had hoped for a more useful physical comparison.

Instead, the examination had confirmed the limits of the surviving evidence.

He forwarded the report to the source custodians.

The original materials would remain sealed.

No one would be permitted to use the preliminary reference as proof of identity.

The next development came from St Catherine's.

A records officer located an additional page in the original neonatal register.

It had been stored separately from the maternity identification slip.

The page contained the routine neonatal handover entry for the 2:17 a.m. infant.

It included:

- Time of initial assessment.
- Sex recorded at birth.
- Clinical condition.
- Temporary neonatal reference.
- A note that receiving identification remained pending.

The reference was not STC-A.

It was an earlier temporary identifier.

Edward requested a certified copy and the original page's custody record.

When the document arrived, he noticed a handwritten annotation in the margin.

**Receiving identity to remain provisional until original band reconciliation.**

The annotation was dated Day 5.

It preceded the Day-6 amendment.

Eleanor read it.

“So the hospital had already written down that the identity was provisional.”

“Yes.”

“And then the next day, the proposed reference was entered.”

“For administrative continuity.”

“But the original restriction remained.”

Edward nodded.

The record did not show that the hospital had changed its medical conclusion.

It showed that the administrative reference had been added while the identity remained unconfirmed.

That distinction had been lost in later summaries.

At the same time, Elena was preparing for the Valmont Foundation meeting.

She had spent the evening building a simple process map from the documents the trustees had supplied.

The map showed five stages:

1. Application received.
2. Eligibility checked.
3. Independent assessment completed.
4. Award authorised.
5. Payment and receipt recorded.

She had marked three areas requiring clarification.

First, the foundation's written policy did not clearly identify who had authority to approve exceptions.

Second, the reviewer guidelines did not specify how conflicts of interest were recorded.

Third, payment confirmation was being treated as evidence that the recipient had received the grant, even when the bank transaction only established that a payment had been initiated.

She added a note beneath the diagram.

**A completed administrative action must not be treated as proof of a different event.**

She looked at the sentence.

It sounded familiar.

She left it in place.

The following morning, Isabelle called.

“Have you finished the presentation?”

“Almost.”

“Are you nervous?”

“About the meeting?”

“Yes.”

Elena considered the question.

“Not really. I'm more concerned that they'll ask me to fix a problem they haven't agreed exists.”

Isabelle laughed softly.

“That sounds possible.”

“If they want a technical review, I'll do one. If they want me to take responsibility for their entire process, I'll decline.”

“I'll make sure they understand.”

Elena paused.

“Isabelle?”

“Yes?”

“Thank you for arranging the meeting without making it sound like an obligation.”

There was a brief silence.

“I'm trying to do that better,” Isabelle said.

Elena did not offer a grand response.

“Then we're making progress.”

The Aurelius investigation team received the updated historical record later that day.

Adrian reviewed the Day-5 neonatal annotation.

Lucien examined the Day-6 amendment.

Damien compared the original Day-14 receiving restriction with the Day-28 summary.

Alexander listened without interrupting.

Victoria sat beside him.

When Adrian finished, Alexander asked, “Does this establish that the 2:17 a.m. infant was our daughter?”

“No,” Adrian said.

“Does it rule it out?”

“No.”

“Does it connect that infant to Elena?”

“No.”

Alexander nodded slowly.

“Then we have learned something about the records, not about Elena.”

Damien placed the documents in a separate folder.

“The original identification materials are still incomplete. The historical comparison cannot establish parentage.”

Lucien looked at the Day-5 annotation.

“Someone knew the identity was provisional.”

“Yes,” Damien said.

“And later records described it as established.”

“Yes.”

Victoria turned toward Alexander.

“Then we need to know how the original records were handled before we ask anyone to believe a family story.”

Alexander agreed.

“No contact with Elena. Not on the basis of these documents.”

Adrian closed the folder.

“There's another issue. We still need to identify the actual authority that can approve a biological comparison.”

“Then find it,” Alexander said. “But do not turn an administrative investigation into a personal approach.”

At 8:18 p.m., Edward received a final message from Helen Ward at the RPRA.

The authority matrix had been reviewed against the current appointment register.

The conclusion was unchanged.

**No final identity determination authority was ever formally appointed under the original framework.**

But Helen had found something else.

A separate request had been submitted on Day 53.

It proposed a new independent identity review authority.

The request had been marked:

**Deferred — originating family confirmation required.**

Edward opened the attachment.

The proposed examiner's name was sealed.

The request included a list of required original materials and a proposed procedure for biological comparison.

It also included a consent requirement.

The proposed process could not proceed until the relevant living individuals had been identified through lawful means and had independently consented to the appropriate testing.

Edward looked at Eleanor.

“This is the first record that connects the historical identity review to a proposed biological comparison.”

“Does it identify the family?”

“No. The family field is sealed.”

“Does it identify the person to be tested?”

“No.”

“Then what does it give us?”

Edward turned to the last page.

A handwritten note appeared beneath the deferral stamp.

**Do not substitute an administrative receiving reference for a living person's identity. The subject must be independently identified before consent is sought.**

Edward read it again.

The instruction was unmistakable.

They could not use an uncertain historical record to decide whom to test.

They first needed to establish the subject through independent evidence.

And only then could they ask that person for consent.

He placed the request beside Dr. Cole's report.

The historical investigation had reached its limit.

The next stage would require something different.

Not another administrative summary.

Not another reconstructed identity.

A properly authorised biological comparison involving people who had the right to decide whether they wished to participate.

And for the first time, the investigation had a written procedure that required exactly that.
