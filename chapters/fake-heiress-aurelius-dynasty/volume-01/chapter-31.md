# Chapter 31 — The Person Who Completed the Release

Edward read the sentence again.

*I am no longer requesting access on behalf of the Family Office. I am requesting access as the person who completed the ninth-day release.*

The words were typed on a formal application submitted to the Vale Historical Records Repository.

There was no explanation attached.

No supporting statement.

No attempt to justify the request.

Only the signature at the bottom.

**Marcus Morelli.**

Edward placed the document on the conference table.

Across from him, Jonathan Pierce adjusted his glasses.

"He's changing the capacity in which he's applying," Pierce said.

"From representing the Aurelius Family Office to claiming personal involvement."

"Precisely."

Richard Valmont leaned back in his chair.

"Does that give him the right to open Box 114?"

"No."

"Then what has changed?"

Pierce considered the question.

"His previous requests were made through the Aurelius Family Office. The repository rejected them because the restriction explicitly prohibits release to that office. This time, he has identified himself as a person directly involved in the original transaction."

"Is that enough?"

"Not necessarily. The restriction refers to the originating representative, not simply someone who participated in the release."

Alexander Aurelius had remained silent.

His attention was fixed on Morelli's signature.

For eighteen years, Marcus Morelli had been a name in an old personnel file.

A director who had resigned.

A former employee whose departure had been handled confidentially.

A man who had apparently possessed information about the missing child but had never contacted the family.

Now he was claiming responsibility for the final movement of both infants.

Alexander finally spoke.

"Why now?"

Nobody answered immediately.

Adrian sat beside his father, reading the repository's correspondence.

"His first request was six months ago. The second was four months ago. The third was last month."

"And all three were refused," Edward said.

"Yes."

"Yet he returned."

Lucien folded his arms.

"That suggests he wants something inside the box."

"Or he wants us to know he wants something inside the box," Sebastian replied.

Nicholas, who had been listening without participating, looked at the document.

"Could he simply be trying to tell us he's ready to talk?"

"Then he could have contacted us directly," Richard said.

"Perhaps he doesn't trust us."

The observation received several looks.

Nicholas shrugged.

"He's spent eighteen years watching people mishandle this situation. I wouldn't assume he considers any of us particularly trustworthy."

It was an uncomfortable possibility.

Especially because nobody could entirely dismiss it.

Victoria sat beside Alexander.

"Does he know what happened to the children?"

Edward glanced at his notes.

"He claims to have completed their release. That doesn't mean he knows where they went afterward, or who ultimately raised them."

"Then why does he say he completed it?"

"Because he was involved in the procedure."

"That is not the same thing."

"No," Edward agreed. "It isn't."

Alexander looked toward Pierce.

"Can we arrange a meeting?"

"We can request one. But I recommend that we don't approach him as a group."

Richard frowned.

"Why?"

"Because a room containing representatives of both families, their lawyers, and several investigators might look less like a conversation and more like an interrogation."

Edward nodded.

"One representative from each family. Independent legal counsel. No demands for immediate disclosure."

"And the Aurelius Family Office?" Adrian asked.

"Not as the formal requesting party," Pierce answered. "That distinction appears important to Morelli."

Alexander's expression tightened.

"Then I will not attend as chairman."

Victoria turned toward him.

"Alexander—"

"I will attend as the father of the missing child."

He paused.

"If he is willing to speak."

The first response arrived two days later.

It was short.

Marcus Morelli agreed to a meeting.

He proposed a neutral location in the northern coastal town where he currently lived.

He also included three conditions.

First, neither family was to bring private security personnel into the meeting room.

Second, no recording devices were permitted.

Third, the meeting was to begin with one question.

*What happened after the children left the nursery?*

Edward read the final line twice.

"He wants to control the conversation."

"Or establish its boundaries," Pierce said.

"Why that particular question?" Sebastian asked.

"Because he believes it is the question we should have asked first."

Richard looked displeased.

"We have spent weeks examining the hospital records."

"And most of those records concern what happened before the children left," Edward replied.

He placed a photograph of the ninth-day register on the table.

"Morelli is directing our attention to the moment after the release."

Victoria looked at the two entries.

Infant A.

Infant B.

Two receiving guardians.

Two erased names.

Two separate departures.

"What happened after they left?" she whispered.

Nobody answered.

The Valmont mansion was unusually quiet that afternoon.

Elena sat at the long dining table in the smaller family sitting room, surrounded by university documents.

A laptop occupied one side of the table.

A notebook occupied the other.

Three printed pages lay beneath a glass of water.

She was comparing the programme requirements for the Singapore exchange with the academic calendar of her current university.

The problem was not the application itself.

The problem was timing.

The exchange programme began in August.

Her university required confirmation of completed coursework by June.

The accommodation application had to be submitted separately.

And the scholarship committee wanted a financial declaration.

Elena rubbed her forehead.

"Why are there so many forms?"

Nicholas, sitting on the sofa, looked up from his phone.

"Because universities enjoy making students suffer."

"They could at least suffer us with fewer documents."

"That would be too generous."

Elena moved one paper aside.

"I need a letter confirming that I'm financially independent for the programme."

Nicholas glanced at her.

"Are you?"

"Not entirely."

"Then don't claim that you are."

"I know. I'm asking whether they accept a family sponsorship declaration without requiring the sponsor to be directly involved in the university's financial administration."

Nicholas stared at her.

"Could you repeat that in a language I understand?"

Elena smiled faintly.

"They need to know who pays if my scholarship doesn't cover everything."

"Ah."

"And I don't want the Valmont family office making every decision for me."

Nicholas put his phone down.

"That makes sense."

She looked at him, perhaps expecting an argument.

He only continued.

"You can ask them to issue a limited sponsorship declaration. It doesn't mean they get to choose your courses, your accommodation, or what you do every day."

"Would Father agree?"

"Probably."

"Probably?"

"He'll ask seventeen questions, consult Sebastian, and request a financial risk assessment."

Elena laughed.

"That sounds accurate."

Nicholas stood.

"I'll ask him."

"Please don't make it sound like I'm demanding anything."

"I won't."

He reached the doorway, then stopped.

"Elena?"

"Yes?"

"You're allowed to want something that doesn't involve this family."

She looked down at the documents.

"I know."

"And you're allowed to accept help without surrendering your decisions."

Elena considered that.

Then she nodded.

"Thank you."

Nicholas left.

Elena returned to her application.

For several minutes, the only sound in the room was the soft tapping of her keyboard.

Then her phone vibrated.

A message from Clara appeared.

**Clara:** Did you finish the exchange application?

**Elena:** Almost.

**Clara:** Define almost.

**Elena:** I have completed the application, the academic statement, and the programme selection.

**Clara:** That sounds finished.

**Elena:** The financial declaration remains.

**Clara:** So you have completed the application except for the application.

**Elena:** Exactly.

**Clara:** Emergency biscuits may be required.

Elena smiled.

**Elena:** Bring the chocolate ones.

**Clara:** I knew this day would come.

Elena set the phone aside.

Outside the window, the garden staff were trimming the hedges.

Everything looked familiar.

The house.

The garden.

The people moving through the corridors.

Yet the questions surrounding her birth had become a constant presence, like a door left slightly open somewhere in the mansion.

She had stopped expecting every answer to arrive quickly.

Perhaps that was a necessary skill.

Waiting without allowing the waiting to consume everything else.

Isabelle was in Catherine's foundation office.

She had spent the morning reviewing a proposal for a scholarship programme intended for students from smaller towns.

The existing programme awarded financial assistance based primarily on examination results.

Isabelle had suggested adding a separate assessment for students who demonstrated exceptional commitment despite limited educational resources.

Catherine read the revised proposal.

"You've changed the selection criteria."

"I've added another category."

"Why?"

"Because examination results don't always tell us how much opportunity someone has had."

Catherine looked at her daughter.

"That is a reasonable argument. But how would you measure it fairly?"

Isabelle pointed to the second page.

"I thought we could use school recommendations, household circumstances, and an interview. Not just income. Some students have access to expensive coaching even when their official household income is low."

Catherine nodded slowly.

"That would require more administrative work."

"I know."

"And a larger review committee."

"I know that too."

"Then why are you proposing it?"

Isabelle hesitated.

"Because I don't want to spend the year arranging events and attending meetings just so everyone can say I helped the foundation."

Catherine's expression softened.

"I don't expect you to."

"I want to do something useful."

"Then we will find something useful for you to do."

Isabelle looked down at the proposal.

"I don't want people to think I only got this opportunity because I'm your daughter."

"They may think that regardless of what you do."

"I know."

Catherine closed the document.

"You cannot control every assumption people make about you. But you can control the quality of your work."

Isabelle nodded.

She had learned that some doors opened because of her birth.

It did not mean she had to walk through them without earning her place.

Catherine returned the proposal.

"Revise the implementation section. I want you to identify the administrative cost and the potential risks."

Isabelle took the pages.

"All right."

"And Isabelle?"

She looked up.

"Don't make yourself responsible for proving that you deserve to be here."

For a moment, Isabelle said nothing.

Then she gave a small nod.

"I'll try."

The Aurelius private aircraft landed shortly after noon.

The meeting had been arranged in a small conference facility attached to a hotel near the coast.

It was not an extravagant location.

There were no grand entrances, private dining rooms, or elaborate security arrangements.

Morelli had specifically requested somewhere ordinary.

Edward arrived first with Pierce.

Alexander and Adrian followed.

Richard and Sebastian arrived separately.

No one brought a large delegation.

The meeting room contained a rectangular table, six chairs, a water dispenser, and a window overlooking the harbour.

Edward placed the documents in a folder.

Pierce checked the time.

"He said twelve-thirty."

"It is twelve-twenty-eight," Sebastian replied.

Alexander stood near the window.

Outside, fishing boats moved slowly through the harbour.

He had spent eighteen years looking for a daughter.

He had examined old records, commissioned investigations, spoken to former employees, and followed every lead that appeared remotely credible.

Now he was waiting for a man who might know what happened during the first nine days of her life.

A knock came at the door.

Everyone turned.

A middle-aged man entered.

His hair had turned grey around the temples.

He wore a dark coat and carried a thin leather folder.

He was not particularly imposing.

He looked like an experienced administrator who had spent years learning how to remain unnoticed in important rooms.

His eyes moved across the people gathered around the table.

Then they settled on Alexander.

"Mr. Aurelius."

Alexander faced him.

"Marcus."

Morelli gave a brief nod.

"Mr. Valmont."

Richard acknowledged him.

Morelli placed his folder on the table.

"I appreciate that you agreed to the conditions."

"We agreed to meet," Alexander said. "That doesn't mean we agree with your account."

"I wouldn't expect you to."

Morelli sat.

For several seconds, nobody spoke.

Then Edward opened his folder.

"You said you completed the ninth-day release."

"Yes."

"What exactly did you complete?"

Morelli looked at the photograph of the register.

"The hospital's administrative release procedure."

"Did you select the receiving guardians?"

"No."

"Did you know their identities?"

"Yes."

"Did you know where the children were going?"

Morelli looked at him.

"At the time, I was given two destinations."

"By whom?"

He did not answer immediately.

Instead, he looked toward Pierce.

"Before we discuss the destinations, I need to establish something."

"What?" Richard asked.

"Neither child was released according to the original instructions."

"We know that," Edward said.

"Then you know the original instructions named Martin Hale and Charles Vale as receiving representatives."

"Yes."

"And neither of them was supposed to receive the child assigned to them."

Richard leaned forward.

"Charles Vale did receive one infant."

"Physically, yes."

"But he was acting on behalf of someone else."

Morelli nodded.

"That was what he told the hospital staff."

"Who?"

"I cannot tell you yet."

Richard's expression hardened.

"Why not?"

"Because the name was not included in the hospital's release register. It was written in a separate authorisation."

"Where is that authorisation?"

Morelli opened his folder.

He removed a single sheet of paper.

It was a photocopy of a document with several sections blacked out.

The visible heading read:

**NINTH-DAY RECEIVING AUTHORITY**

Below it were two entries.

**INFANT A — RECEIVING GUARDIAN: [REDACTED]**

**INFANT B — RECEIVING GUARDIAN: [REDACTED]**

At the bottom was a line for the person authorising the arrangement.

The name had been obscured.

Edward studied the document.

"Is this the original?"

"No. It is a copy."

"Where is the original?"

"In a separate archive."

"Box 114?"

Morelli looked at him.

"No."

That answer surprised everyone.

"Then where?" Sebastian asked.

"That is one of the reasons I requested access to Box 114."

Pierce frowned.

"Explain."

Morelli folded his hands.

"The receiving authority was created from information held in two separate places. The hospital retained the release register. Vale Private Counsel retained the authorisation. But the original maternal identification pages were stored separately."

"Box 114-B," Edward said.

"Correct."

"Then what is in Box 114?"

Morelli looked at the lawyer.

"Instructions concerning the person who was permitted to receive those pages."

Pierce became very still.

"Not the children?"

"Not directly."

Edward leaned forward.

"Then why does the restriction say the authorised recipient is the mother of the children?"

"Because the maternal pages were never meant to be delivered to either family office."

Alexander's eyes narrowed.

"Who were they meant for?"

Morelli looked at him.

"That is the question I have spent eighteen years trying to answer."

The room fell silent.

Richard spoke first.

"You were the Director of Family Affairs. You had access to the records. You completed the release. And you are telling us you don't know who was supposed to receive the documents?"

"I knew who had been named as the authorised recipient."

"Then tell us."

"I cannot establish whether that person was the mother of either child."

"How is that possible?"

"Because the name was entered through an external instruction. The hospital verified the mothers' identities independently. It did not verify that the person named in the instruction was either mother."

Edward stared at him.

"Someone selected a recipient without establishing the relationship."

"Yes."

"Who approved it?"

Morelli looked down at the document.

"That is what I was supposed to determine."

Alexander's voice became colder.

"You were supposed to determine it?"

"Yes."

"By whose authority?"

Morelli raised his eyes.

"Yours."

Nobody moved.

Alexander slowly straightened.

"I never authorised you to select the receiving guardians."

"I know."

"I never authorised you to arrange the transfer of my daughter."

"I know."

"Then why are you claiming my authority?"

"I'm not."

Morelli placed another paper on the table.

It was an old internal instruction bearing the Aurelius Family Office letterhead.

The signature line contained Alexander's name.

But beneath it was a handwritten note:

**AUTHORITY LIMITED TO INVESTIGATION AND IDENTITY VERIFICATION. NO CHANGE OF GUARDIANSHIP OR PHYSICAL TRANSFER AUTHORISED.**

Alexander read it.

He recognised the wording.

It was consistent with the instruction he had issued after learning that the hospital could not locate the infant registered under the Aurelius name.

But he had never seen this particular copy.

"Where did you get this?"

"It was attached to my original assignment."

"Then why was the approval field altered?"

Morelli did not answer.

Edward examined the document.

The approval section had been copied poorly.

The signature was visible, but the surrounding text was incomplete.

"Was this the document used to justify the nursery arrangement?"

"No."

"Then what was?"

Morelli looked toward the window.

"The authority that permitted the ninth-day release."

"Who signed it?"

"I don't know."

"You're telling us you completed a release without knowing who authorised it?"

"I knew the authorising representative. I did not know who had selected the receiving guardians."

"Those are two different things."

"Yes."

"Then explain the difference."

Morelli looked at Edward.

"Because I was not the person who made the decision."

He paused.

"But I was the person who carried it out."

The meeting continued for another forty minutes.

Morelli answered questions about the nursery, the hospital's procedures, and the separation of the original records.

He confirmed that both infants had been alive and medically stable when they left.

He confirmed that they were discharged separately.

He confirmed that one was received by Charles Vale on behalf of another person.

He also confirmed that the second infant was received by a woman whose identity had been recorded in the separate authority.

But he refused to provide the two names.

Not because he claimed they were legally protected.

Not because he lacked access.

He simply refused.

"You are asking us to trust you," Alexander said.

"No."

Morelli closed his folder.

"I'm asking you to verify what I tell you."

"Then give us something we can verify."

Morelli considered the request.

Finally, he removed a small envelope.

It was old, yellowed around the edges, and sealed with a narrow strip of paper.

The front contained a handwritten date.

The ninth day after the births.

Below it was a short note.

**For the person who eventually asks what happened after the release.**

Edward reached for it.

Morelli placed his hand over the envelope.

"Not yet."

Edward withdrew his hand.

"What is it?"

"A copy of the final instruction I received before the children left the hospital."

"From whom?"

Morelli looked toward Alexander.

"From the person who selected the receiving guardians."

Richard's chair shifted.

"Then you know who selected them."

"Yes."

"Who?"

Morelli's expression changed.

For the first time since entering the room, he appeared genuinely uncomfortable.

"I know who sent the instruction."

He paused.

"I do not know whether that person made the decision independently."

"Who sent it?" Alexander asked.

Morelli looked at the sealed envelope.

Then he answered.

"Charles Vale."

The name seemed to settle heavily over the table.

Edward immediately looked at Richard.

Richard's expression had become unreadable.

"Charles Vale selected the receiving guardians?"

"He delivered the instruction."

"That is not what I asked."

"No," Morelli said. "It isn't."

Alexander's voice was controlled.

"Was Charles acting for someone else?"

"Yes."

"Who?"

Morelli shook his head.

"That name is in the document."

"Then open it."

"I cannot."

"Why?"

"Because the instruction was not addressed to me."

He turned the envelope over.

There was a second line beneath the seal.

A recipient's name had been written there.

It was almost entirely covered by an old paper label.

Only the first letter remained visible.

**M.**

Edward stared at it.

Marcus Morelli.

Martin Hale.

Margaret.

Martha.

There were too many possibilities.

Pierce leaned closer.

"Who was the recipient?"

Morelli withdrew his hand from the envelope.

"I was instructed to deliver it to the person named on the label after the release had been completed."

"And did you?"

"No."

"Why not?"

"Because the person was no longer there."

"Where had they gone?"

Morelli looked at Alexander.

"That is what I was trying to find out when I left the Family Office."

For a moment, Alexander seemed unable to respond.

Then he asked the question that had been waiting behind every other question.

"Marcus, did you know which child was mine?"

Morelli looked at him.

"I knew which child was recorded under your name."

"That is not the same thing."

"No."

"Did you know which child was biologically mine?"

Morelli's face tightened.

"No."

Alexander looked away.

It was not the answer he wanted.

But it was the first answer that had not attempted to disguise uncertainty.

Edward looked at the envelope again.

"Will you give this to us?"

"Not today."

"Then why bring it?"

"Because I want you to know that it exists."

Morelli stood.

"There is one more thing."

He looked directly at Edward.

"The person who selected the receiving guardians did not have the authority to decide which child belonged to which family."

Edward frowned.

"Then why were they allowed to select the guardians?"

"Because the original arrangement had already failed."

"What failed?"

Morelli's expression became distant.

"The first person who was supposed to receive the children never arrived."

He collected his folder.

"And when that happened, someone made a decision that changed both their lives."

The meeting ended without the envelope being opened.

Outside, the harbour remained busy.

Fishing boats returned with their afternoon catch. A delivery van stopped near the hotel entrance. A group of tourists walked past the building, discussing where they would have lunch.

Ordinary life continued.

Inside a parked car, Edward reviewed his notes.

Richard sat beside him.

"Charles Vale," Richard said.

"Yes."

"Why would he select the receiving guardians?"

"We don't know that he did. Morelli said he delivered the instruction."

"Who wrote it?"

"We need the document."

Richard looked out the window.

"Charles is dead. Eleanor is dead. Whitmore is dead. Hale has already told us what he knows."

"And Morelli is still alive."

"Yet he won't tell us the names."

Edward closed his notebook.

"He's not refusing everything."

"He's giving us just enough to keep us interested."

"Perhaps."

Richard turned toward him.

"Edward, I want to know who raised Isabelle's biological sister. I want to know whether Elena was ever meant to be raised by us. And I want to know why nobody told either mother what happened."

"I want those answers too."

"Then we cannot allow another person to decide what we are permitted to know."

Edward looked toward the hotel entrance.

Morelli had disappeared.

"Maybe," he said quietly, "that is exactly what happened eighteen years ago."

That evening, the Aurelius family gathered in the private sitting room of their residence.

Alexander had returned with Adrian.

Victoria was waiting.

Lucien sat on the arm of a sofa, while Damien stood near the window.

Julian was at the table with a notebook.

He looked up as his father entered.

"Did you find her?"

The question was immediate.

Alexander paused.

"No."

Julian's expression fell.

"But we found someone who may know what happened."

"Marcus Morelli?"

Alexander looked at him.

"Yes."

Julian closed his notebook.

"Did he know where she went?"

"He knew where both children went after leaving the hospital."

Victoria stood.

"Alexander?"

"He confirmed that the infants were released separately. One was received by Charles Vale on behalf of another person. The other was received by an unidentified woman."

"Did he know which was ours?"

"No."

Victoria slowly sat down.

For a moment, she looked toward the staircase.

The bedroom at the end of the east corridor remained untouched.

Its shelves still held books chosen before her daughter was born.

Its wardrobe still contained a few carefully preserved items.

She had kept it because abandoning the room had felt like accepting that her daughter would never return.

Now she wondered whether she had preserved a room for a child who had spent eighteen years living somewhere else, unaware that it existed.

"Did he say anything about the mothers?" she asked.

"He said the person who selected the receiving guardians had no authority to determine the children's biological identities."

Victoria closed her eyes.

"Then someone separated two babies without knowing which belonged to whom."

"That appears to be what happened."

Lucien's usual humour had disappeared.

"That's not a mistake. That's an entire chain of decisions."

"Yes," Adrian said.

"And every person involved seems to have known only part of it."

Damien looked toward Alexander.

"Morelli may have been following instructions from someone else."

"That's likely."

"Then he may not be the person we should be looking for."

Alexander nodded.

"He is not the only person we need to find."

Julian opened his notebook again.

He looked at the list of things he intended to do with his sister.

Some entries were simple.

Visit the aquarium.

Teach her the family board game.

Show her the old observatory.

Other entries were more ambitious.

Take her to the northern mountains.

Let her choose a new family holiday destination.

Help her build a library.

He had recently added a new item.

**Ask her what she wants to do, instead of deciding everything beforehand.**

He stared at it for a moment.

Then he added another.

**Find out whether she likes the sea.**

Victoria noticed.

"What are you writing?"

"Things to do with her."

"How many now?"

"Forty-three."

Lucien looked at the notebook.

"At this rate, she'll need a second lifetime to finish the list."

Julian frowned.

"She can choose which ones she wants."

"Good."

Julian looked toward his father.

"Do you think she likes books?"

Alexander was quiet.

"I don't know."

"Maybe we should find out."

"Yes," Victoria said gently. "But not by asking everyone who might know her."

Julian nodded.

He understood.

He wanted to meet his sister.

He wanted to know what she liked, what annoyed her, what made her laugh, and whether she would enjoy having four older brothers.

But he was beginning to understand that wanting someone in the family did not mean he could decide when they should arrive.

He closed the notebook.

"Then I'll wait."

At the Valmont mansion, Elena finally submitted her exchange application.

The confirmation appeared on the screen.

APPLICATION SUBMITTED

She leaned back in her chair.

For the first time that afternoon, she allowed herself to smile.

It was only an application.

Nothing had been approved yet.

There were interviews, financial documents, and academic reviews still to come.

But she had completed the first step.

A message arrived from Isabelle.

**Isabelle:** Did you submit yours?

**Elena:** Just now.

**Isabelle:** Good. I submitted my preliminary application too.

**Elena:** Singapore?

**Isabelle:** Still deciding. I selected two programmes.

**Elena:** That's sensible.

**Isabelle:** I also discovered that the application portal was designed by someone who hates students.

Elena laughed.

**Elena:** I had the same experience.

**Isabelle:** Perhaps we should start a support group.

**Elena:** Only if there are biscuits.

**Isabelle:** Clara has already claimed that territory.

Elena smiled at the screen.

Their conversations had become easier over the past few days.

Not effortless.

There were still moments when an ordinary question about childhood memories became unexpectedly complicated.

But they had started discussing things that belonged to their present rather than their shared past.

University.

Work.

Books.

Travel.

Their individual ambitions.

For now, that was enough.

Elena closed her laptop and went downstairs.

She had no idea that somewhere across the country, two powerful families were trying to understand who had received her on the ninth day of her life.

She did not know that a man named Marcus Morelli had recently spoken about the release of two infants.

And she certainly did not know that the sealed envelope he had brought to the meeting contained a name that might eventually connect her to the people who had spent eighteen years searching for her.

For the moment, she was simply Elena.

And tomorrow, she had an appointment at the university.

Later that night, Edward received a message from the document examiner.

The subject line read:

**PRELIMINARY REVIEW — NINTH-DAY RECEIVING AUTHORITY**

He opened the attachment.

The examiner had compared the photocopy Morelli brought to the meeting with the surviving hospital register and the handwriting impressions preserved beneath the erased labels.

The result was inconclusive.

But one detail stood out.

The two receiving-guardian names had not been written by the same person.

One appeared to have been entered by Charles Vale.

The other had been entered by someone else.

Beneath the second entry was a short notation.

**RECEIVING GUARDIAN TO BE INFORMED AFTER DELIVERY.**

Edward read it twice.

Then he opened the final page of the examiner's report.

A faint impression had been detected beneath the blacked-out section of the receiving authority.

It appeared to be a handwritten instruction.

The examiner had reconstructed only part of it:

*Do not tell the receiving guardian which child has been assigned until—*

The remainder was unreadable.

Edward stared at the unfinished sentence.

The receiving guardians had not been told which children they were taking.

Perhaps they had not even known that the two infants were involved in the same arrangement.

He picked up his phone and called Pierce.

"There's something else."

"What?"

"The receiving guardians may not have known the identities of the children they were taking."

Silence.

Then Pierce asked, "Are you certain?"

"No. It's a preliminary finding."

Edward looked again at the document.

"But if it's correct, we've been asking the wrong question."

"Which is?"

Edward lowered his voice.

"We've been trying to find out who selected the guardians."

He paused.

"We also need to find out who was deliberately kept from knowing what they had received."

And somewhere in the records of the ninth day, beneath the names that had been erased, that answer was still waiting.
