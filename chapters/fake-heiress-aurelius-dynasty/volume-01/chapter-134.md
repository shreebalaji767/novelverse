# **Chapter 134 — The Key That Was Prepared but Never Released**

Charles Vale's instruction was dated day 42.

Edward read it again.

*Verification key is not to be released until the proposed receiving authority confirms its appointment.*

Beneath the sentence appeared a second instruction.

*The personal correspondence shall remain sealed pending completion of the receiving identity process.*

The document was signed by Charles.

There was no reference to an appointment granting him authority to control P-04's personal communication.

There was no separate approval from the originating family representative.

And there was no confirmation from the proposed receiving authority.

“It's circular,” Jonathan said.

Edward nodded.

“The key was withheld because the receiving authority wasn't appointed. But the receiving authority couldn't be appointed without completing the identity process.”

“And the identity process required the principal's response.”

“Which couldn't be obtained without the key.”

Jonathan looked at the instruction.

“Was this deliberate?”

“We don't know. But we know Charles was informed that the process was incomplete, and he chose to maintain the restriction.”

Edward placed the document in a separate folder.

He wanted the original release history.

A prepared key could have been withheld for legitimate security reasons.

But a key that remained withheld after the intended recipient had asked to see the communication was something else.

Meridian's records manager, Rachel Morton, provided the preparation register later that morning.

The verification key had been generated on day 42 by the Personal Instructions Division.

It consisted of two parts.

The first was a sealed identity reference.

The second was a matching confirmation code intended for the delivery officer.

Neither part was supposed to contain the principal's name.

Together, however, they would allow the officer to establish that the person at the destination matched the protected identity record.

Edward examined the preparation details.

**Verification reference:** PO-04/VK-42.

**Prepared by:** Personal Instructions Division.

**Checked by:** Eleanor Vale.

**Release authority:** Pending.

**Intended delivery:** Day 43.

**Final dispatch:** Withheld.

“Eleanor checked it?” Jonathan asked.

Rachel nodded.

“Her initials are on the preparation record.”

“Did she approve the release?”

“No. The release field is blank.”

Edward studied the document.

“Can we ask her why she checked it?”

Rachel permitted them to take a preservation copy.

Eleanor Vale looked at the preparation record for several minutes.

“Yes,” she said. “I checked this.”

“Why?”

“To confirm that the two parts of the verification reference corresponded to the same protected identity record.”

“Did you know it was intended for Nora Whitcomb?”

“I knew a delivery was being arranged. I didn't know the final courier until later.”

“Did you authorise the release?”

“No.”

“Why not?”

“Because the release authority was supposed to be confirmed separately. I had no authority to release a protected identity reference on my own.”

“Who did?”

Eleanor looked at the document.

“The personal correspondence custodian, once formally appointed.”

“Was one appointed?”

“No.”

Edward placed Charles's day-42 instruction beside the preparation record.

“Did you know Charles had ordered the key withheld?”

“I learned that after the delivery failed.”

“Did you challenge it?”

“Yes.”

“What did you say?”

“That the delivery could not reasonably be treated as an unsuccessful principal response when the verification reference had not been supplied.”

“Did he agree?”

“No.”

Eleanor opened a drawer and removed a copy of her correspondence.

It was dated day 44.

*The attempted delivery must remain classified as incomplete. No response has been obtained from P-04. The verification key was prepared but not released. The recipient's identity was therefore not established.*

Edward read the paragraph.

“Did Charles reply?”

“He sent a short response.”

Eleanor handed him the next sheet.

*The delivery was attempted in accordance with the available administrative instructions. The absence of a verified response does not prevent temporary administrative continuity.*

Edward frowned.

“He is treating the failed attempt as sufficient to continue the process.”

“Yes.”

“Did you accept that?”

“No. I wrote back that temporary administrative continuity could not substitute for the principal's response.”

She placed another document on the table.

It was dated day 45.

*The principal has not refused the proposed arrangement. The principal has not accepted it. No personal position has been established.*

Edward looked up.

“You wrote this?”

“Yes.”

“Did anyone acknowledge it?”

“No.”

“Did the instruction change?”

“No.”

Eleanor folded her hands.

“I was not trying to prevent the arrangement from continuing temporarily. I was trying to prevent an incomplete process from becoming a decision.”

At the Aurelius Estate, Victoria listened to the update.

“So the person at the destination may have been P-04,” she said.

“Yes,” Edward replied. “But we cannot confirm that.”

“And the person asked to see the communication?”

“According to Nora's contemporaneous note.”

Victoria looked down.

“Then someone may have been willing to answer, but was never given the opportunity.”

Alexander remained silent.

He had spent years reviewing the same collection of records, searching for a definitive answer about the missing child.

Now another question had emerged.

Who had been denied the opportunity to speak?

“Could the destination have been the child's temporary residence?” he asked.

“We don't know. The address was protected.”

“Was it connected to V-17?”

“Not according to the dispatch record.”

Victoria turned towards Edward.

“Then we need the address.”

Edward nodded.

“We are tracing it through the courier's original assignment records.”

The destination address was not listed in the main delivery report.

It appeared in a separate sealed schedule.

Meridian had preserved the schedule because the original courier assignment required the address to remain separate from the protected identity reference.

The schedule contained a building code:

**P-04 / DESTINATION — C-19.**

No street address.

No named recipient.

Only a reference to an external property register.

Jonathan searched the archive index.

“C-19 appears in the day-34 personal representation chart.”

Edward moved closer.

“What does it refer to?”

“An external contact location. Not a residence classification.”

“Who controlled it?”

“The record says Cross Administrative Services.”

Edward looked at him.

“Marianne?”

“Possibly her office. But the property itself was managed by a separate company.”

They requested the underlying register.

It took several hours.

When the record arrived, the address was visible.

A small residential building on the outskirts of the city.

It had been used as a temporary correspondence contact location by several organisations.

The registered property manager was a company that had since been dissolved.

The lease period covered days 18 to 61.

Edward examined the listed access contacts.

There were three.

**Charles Vale.**

**Marianne Cross.**

**A. Mercer.**

Jonathan looked up.

“Anna Mercer-Smith.”

Edward nodded slowly.

Anna's name had appeared in several stages of the investigation.

She had worked at St Catherine's.

She had transported protected correspondence.

She had drafted the conditional response at Margaret Voss's request.

And now her name appeared as an access contact for the location where Nora had attempted to deliver P-04's correspondence.

But it did not prove she had lived there.

Nor did it prove she was P-04.

Anna Mercer-Smith agreed to meet them again.

When Edward showed her the property register, she looked at it for a long time.

“I recognise C-19,” she said.

“What was it?”

“A temporary correspondence office.”

“Did you have access?”

“Yes.”

“Did you live there?”

“No.”

“Did you meet anyone there on day 43?”

Anna hesitated.

“I remember being asked to wait at the property that morning.”

“By whom?”

“Marianne Cross.”

“What were you told?”

“That a courier might arrive with a sealed communication and that I should not accept it unless the verification procedure was completed.”

“Did Nora Whitcomb arrive?”

“Yes.”

“Did you meet her?”

“I spoke to her briefly.”

Edward placed Nora's delivery report before her.

“Was the person who asked to see the letter you?”

Anna looked at the note.

“No.”

“Who was it?”

“I don't know.”

“Did you see that person?”

“Yes.”

“Can you describe them?”

Anna considered the question.

“I remember someone arriving shortly before Nora. They were not introduced to me. I was told not to ask their name.”

“Did you know why they were there?”

“No.”

“Did they identify themselves as P-04?”

“No.”

“Did they speak to Nora?”

“Yes.”

Anna looked at the note again.

“I remember the question they asked.”

“What was it?”

“They wanted to know whether the letter explained why they were being contacted.”

Edward felt his attention sharpen.

“And Nora said?”

“She couldn't discuss the contents.”

“Did the person leave?”

“Yes.”

“Did you speak to them afterwards?”

“No. Marianne told me to remain inside until the courier had left.”

“Did Marianne explain who the visitor was?”

“No.”

Anna returned the paper.

“I should make something clear. I was never told that the visitor was P-04. I was not authorised to identify the principal.”

Edward nodded.

“Did you ever see that person again?”

“No.”

Marianne Cross was less forthcoming.

She confirmed that C-19 had been used as a temporary correspondence point.

She also confirmed that Anna had been assigned there on day 43.

But when Edward asked about the unidentified visitor, she became cautious.

“I remember the delivery attempt,” she said.

“Who was the visitor?”

“I cannot confirm that.”

“Did you arrange for the person to attend?”

“I arranged for the correspondence process.”

“Did you tell the person to come?”

“I was not authorised to contact P-04 directly.”

“Then how did the person know to attend?”

Marianne paused.

“That question needs to be answered from the original contact register.”

“Do you have it?”

“No. The register was held by the property manager.”

“Where is it now?”

“The company was dissolved. Its records were transferred to a successor administrator.”

Edward leaned forward.

“Did you know the verification key had been withheld?”

“I knew the key was not released.”

“Then why was anyone asked to attend?”

Marianne looked uncomfortable.

“I was told that the person might be able to establish identity through an existing reference.”

“Which reference?”

“I wasn't given one.”

“Did you tell the visitor that the courier could not hand over the letter?”

“No. I was not present when Nora spoke to them.”

“Did you record the visit as a refusal?”

“No.”

“Did anyone?”

Marianne looked at him.

“Not in my register.”

Edward watched her carefully.

“Who told you to arrange the meeting?”

Marianne opened her folder and removed a small instruction slip.

It was dated day 42.

The sender was not named.

The subject read:

**P-04 — PERSONAL CORRESPONDENCE DELIVERY COORDINATION.**

The instruction said:

*Please ensure the protected contact is available at C-19 on day 43. No identity information is to be disclosed to the intermediary. The courier will complete the verification process.*

Edward read it twice.

The visitor had not simply appeared unexpectedly.

Someone had arranged for the person to be available.

But that person had not been given the information necessary to verify the delivery.

“Who sent this?”

Marianne turned the slip over.

There was a routing reference on the reverse.

**ORIGINATING OFFICE / PERSONAL REPRESENTATION.**

No signature.

No individual sender.

Back at the office, Jonathan compared the day-42 coordination slip with Charles's instruction withholding the verification key.

Both had been issued on the same day.

The coordination slip asked for the protected contact to be available.

The withholding instruction prevented the courier from confirming the contact's identity.

The two instructions had been processed through different offices.

One arranged the meeting.

The other made a successful delivery impossible.

“Could that have been a mistake?” Jonathan asked.

“It could have been,” Edward said.

“But they came from the same originating office reference.”

“Which still doesn't tell us who issued them.”

Jonathan opened the property company's successor archive.

A record had been recovered from its old access register.

**Day 43 — C-19**

**Visitor:** Restricted.

**Purpose:** Personal correspondence verification.

**Contact initiated by:** External office.

**Attendance confirmed:** Yes.

**Identity verification:** Not completed.

**Follow-up requested:** Yes.

Edward read the last line.

“Follow-up requested?”

Jonathan nodded.

“Who requested it?”

“The visitor.”

“Was the request transmitted?”

Jonathan opened the next entry.

**Day 44 — Follow-up instruction received.**

**Status:** Suspended pending originating office authorisation.

The instruction contained a short message.

*The visitor remains willing to receive the correspondence. A new appointment should be arranged with the verification reference available.*

Edward stopped reading.

For several seconds, he said nothing.

The visitor had not refused.

They had asked for another attempt.

And the request had been suspended.

“Who suspended it?” Jonathan asked.

Edward turned to the final field.

**Authorising office:** Originating Office / Personal Representation.

**Authorising reference:** PO-04/CONTROL-44.

There was no signature.

No name.

Only a second line.

*No further contact is required until the proposed receiving arrangement is resolved.*

Edward looked at the day-43 report.

The original communication had never been delivered.

The visitor had requested another attempt.

The request had been suspended.

And the principal's response remained outstanding.

He closed the file.

“Find the authorising reference.”

Jonathan nodded.

Edward looked again at the phrase *No further contact is required*.

Someone had decided that a person who had not received the letter did not need another opportunity to read it.

And the instruction had been issued in the name of an office whose authority had never been formally established.
