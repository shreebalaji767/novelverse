# **Chapter 81 — The Number That Followed Two Children**

Edward read the Northbridge register again.

Then a third time.

**Previous receiving reference: IV-204.**

The entry was written in blue ink, beneath the main identification details for STC-A.

It was not a reference to a comparison request.

It was not a note that the two records had been examined together.

It appeared in a column specifically intended to record the identification under which an infant had previously been received.

Edward placed the photograph beside the sixth-day transfer receipt.

The first document identified an infant as IV-204.

The second identified an infant as STC-A, while recording IV-204 as its previous receiving reference.

There were several possible explanations.

The same infant might have been transferred under two different administrative identities.

Two separate infants might have been confused during a transfer.

Or someone might have copied the wrong reference into the register.

None of those possibilities could yet be excluded.

But the implications were serious.

He called Jonathan again.

"Don't let anyone alter or handle the original register until we've arranged an independent preservation examination."

"I've already requested that."

"Good. Ask them to photograph the entire page, including the entries before and after STC-A."

"Why the surrounding entries?"

"Because I want to know whether this was a routine notation or an exception."

Jonathan paused.

"Do you think someone deliberately connected the two children?"

"I think someone connected the two records. We still don't know why."

Edward ended the call.

For the first time in several days, he felt that the investigation had moved beyond discovering missing paperwork.

They were beginning to understand how the paperwork had been used.

Unfortunately, that did not mean they were any closer to knowing who Elena was.

The original Northbridge register arrived under controlled custody the following afternoon.

Edward examined it with an independent document specialist, who photographed the relevant pages and checked the binding, ink, paper condition and visible alterations.

The specialist did not attempt to determine the identity of either infant.

His task was narrower.

He wanted to establish whether the disputed entry was part of the original register or had been added later.

After several hours, he delivered his preliminary observations.

"The entry appears consistent with the surrounding handwriting and ink," he said. "I cannot identify any obvious physical sign that it was inserted after the page was completed."

"Does that mean it was written on the ninth day?"

"Not necessarily. It means the entry does not presently appear to be a later addition. Establishing the exact date would require further examination, and even then there may be limits."

Edward nodded.

"Could it have been copied from another document?"

"Certainly. The handwriting may be original to the register, while the information itself may have originated elsewhere."

"Can you identify the writer?"

"Not from this examination."

Edward thanked him.

Once the specialist had left, Jonathan placed the remaining photographs on the desk.

The surrounding entries revealed something else.

Northbridge had received several infants during the same period.

Most were recorded using a single originating reference.

Some had a temporary hospital number added.

Only two entries contained a previous receiving reference.

One was STC-A.

The other belonged to an infant transferred from a different clinic several days later.

Jonathan pointed to the second entry.

"That looks like a routine transfer from another facility."

"Exactly."

"So the practice itself wasn't unusual."

"No. But the wording matters."

Edward circled the STC-A entry.

"Here, IV-204 is not described as a comparison reference. It is described as a previous receiving reference."

"Could that mean the infant was previously admitted under IV-204?"

"It could."

"And if so, the two references might belong to the same child."

"Yes."

Jonathan studied the page.

"But we have a separate sixth-day receipt for IV-204, and a separate ninth-day receipt for STC-A."

"Which is why we need the original transport logs."

Edward stood.

"Get me every record showing the movement of the two infants between the sixth and ninth days."

At Meridian, the records officer had already begun searching the transport archive.

He called Edward that evening.

"We found a vehicle dispatch sheet."

"Does it identify the infants?"

"It identifies the transport cases. There are two entries."

Edward opened his notebook.

"Read them."

"Sixth day, 08:40. Case IV-204. Destination Northbridge Clinical Receiving Unit."

"And the second?"

"Ninth day, 11:15. Case STC-A. Same destination."

"Same transport vehicle?"

"No. Different vehicles."

"Different medical teams?"

"Yes."

"Different drivers?"

"Yes."

Edward paused.

"Who authorised the second transfer?"

The officer checked the record.

"Gabriel Vale."

"Who authorised the first?"

"Also Gabriel Vale."

Edward wrote that down.

"Was there any reference connecting the two dispatches?"

"Yes. The ninth-day dispatch sheet contains a notation referring to the earlier IV-204 case."

"What does it say?"

The officer read slowly.

"Previous receiving reference to be retained until originating identity review is completed."

Edward closed his eyes for a moment.

There it was again.

The number had been deliberately carried forward.

But the wording did not explain whether the same infant was involved.

"Does the dispatch sheet identify the child by name?"

"No. Both are recorded as female infants."

"Any physical description?"

"Weight and medical condition."

"Are those consistent?"

The officer hesitated.

"Not entirely."

Edward straightened.

"Explain."

"The sixth-day case was recorded at two kilograms and eight hundred grams."

"And the ninth-day case?"

"Two kilograms and seven hundred grams."

"Could that be the same infant?"

"Certainly. But the medical notes are different."

"How?"

"The first record describes mild feeding difficulty. The second describes routine feeding and stable temperature."

"Is that inconsistent with recovery over three days?"

"No. It could be perfectly consistent."

Edward did not interrupt.

The officer continued.

"There is also a difference in the receiving band numbers."

"Read them."

"IV-204 was replaced at Northbridge with a temporary band numbered NB-061."

"And STC-A?"

"That infant received NB-094."

Edward looked at the two numbers in his notebook.

"Was NB-061 removed before the ninth-day transfer?"

"I can't find a removal entry."

"Was NB-094 issued before the ninth-day transfer?"

"No. It was issued on arrival."

Edward was silent.

If the two references belonged to the same child, the first receiving band should have remained part of the custody trail, or its removal should have been documented.

If they belonged to different children, the second infant should not have inherited the first infant's receiving reference without an explanation.

"Send me copies of the original dispatch sheets and the band register."

"I will."

Edward ended the call.

Jonathan had been listening.

"So the ninth-day infant could have been the sixth-day infant."

"Yes."

"But we don't know."

"No."

"And the records don't prove that two different children were present."

"Not by themselves."

Jonathan leaned back.

"Then why did the hospital create two separate receiving cases?"

Edward looked at the papers.

"That is what we're going to find out."

Elena had a different problem.

Her presentation was due in three days, and she had reached the stage where she disliked every sentence she had written.

She sat at the dining table in the Valmont estate, surrounded by printed pages, handwritten notes and a laptop.

Her proposed project was straightforward.

A simple digital record-keeping system for small businesses.

It would allow a shopkeeper to record daily sales, track expenses, monitor stock and generate basic summaries without needing expensive software.

She had deliberately avoided making it too complicated.

Her first version had included customer profiles, supplier histories, automated reports, inventory forecasts and several other features.

After testing it with a sample business, she had removed nearly half of them.

The system was supposed to help a small shop owner.

It was not supposed to become another job.

Nicholas walked into the dining room carrying a glass of water.

He looked at the papers.

"Are you building a business?"

"No."

"Writing a business plan?"

"No."

"Preparing to take over the family company?"

Elena looked up.

"Definitely not."

"Then why does the dining table look like a corporate emergency?"

"Because I'm trying to make a simple record system."

Nicholas pulled out a chair.

"How simple?"

"Simple enough that someone who has never used accounting software can understand it."

He glanced at her screen.

"What does this button do?"

"Records a sale."

"And that one?"

"Records an expense."

"What happens if someone presses the wrong one?"

Elena looked at him.

"They can correct the entry."

"Can they delete it?"

"No. Corrections are recorded separately."

Nicholas raised his eyebrows.

"That's rather serious for a little shop."

"People need to know where their money went. If someone can silently delete an entry, the records become unreliable."

He nodded thoughtfully.

"Could it work for a family?"

"Of course. You could use the same basic structure for household expenses."

"Could it track how much I spend on food?"

"It could."

"Could it track how much Julian spends on unnecessary things?"

Elena smiled.

"That would require a separate investigation."

Nicholas laughed.

She returned to her presentation.

He watched her for a moment.

"You're enjoying this."

"I am."

"More than you expected?"

Elena considered the question.

"Yes. I thought I would enjoy making the software. I didn't expect to enjoy figuring out what people actually need."

Nicholas nodded.

"That's probably more important."

She looked at him.

"You're being surprisingly sensible."

"I have moments."

"Rare ones."

"Don't tell Sebastian. He'll expect me to repeat them."

Elena laughed.

Nicholas stood to leave, then stopped.

"Have you thought about what happens if they select you?"

"Then I'll go to Singapore for the programme."

"And if they don't?"

"I'll find another programme."

He studied her.

"You've already made up your mind."

"I want to try."

Nicholas smiled.

"Then try properly."

He left her to her work.

Elena looked at the presentation again.

For the first time that afternoon, she did not feel dissatisfied with it.

At the Aurelius estate, Victoria was reading a report from the family archive when Alexander entered the room.

She looked up.

"Have you heard from Edward?"

"Not today."

"He requested the original custody material."

Alexander placed a folder on the table.

"I authorised the release of the copies. The originals will remain under independent supervision."

Victoria closed her report.

"Do you think he has found something?"

"He has found another discrepancy."

"That isn't the same thing."

"No."

Alexander sat opposite her.

For eighteen years, the family had searched for a daughter whose disappearance had left behind more questions than answers.

Every promising document had eventually produced another uncertainty.

Every apparent confirmation had required another examination.

Victoria had learned not to celebrate too early.

But she had also learned that caution could become an excuse for doing nothing.

"Alexander," she said, "I want to speak to Eleanor."

He looked at her.

"Why?"

"Because she wrote that instruction about keeping the St Catherine's card separate from the material I received."

"Edward has already spoken to her."

"I know. But I want to understand why she thought my documents were dangerous to combine with the card."

"She said they were unverified."

"That is what everyone says."

Victoria glanced towards the cabinet.

Inside it were the preserved envelopes from the original investigation.

The letter from Marianne Cross.

The empty identification pouch.

The separate administrative card.

For years, Victoria had believed that one of those items might eventually explain what happened to her daughter.

Now she wondered whether the way they had been stored had concealed a more important detail.

"Did Eleanor ever tell you that the band itself was missing?" Alexander asked.

"She never spoke to me directly."

"Did you receive anything else from her?"

Victoria thought for a moment.

"Not from her."

She stood and opened the cabinet.

There were several sealed archival folders.

She removed the one containing the original correspondence from the seventeenth day.

"Wait."

Alexander approached.

Victoria examined the folder's contents.

The letter from Marianne was there.

The envelope was there.

The empty pouch was there.

But beneath the pouch lay a thin sheet of paper that she had not seen during the previous review.

It was folded twice and had been placed behind the archival backing.

Victoria carefully unfolded it.

The paper contained a short handwritten note.

**For the originating family representative only.**

**Do not attach this material to the infant identification card.**

There was no signature.

Only a reference.

**EFO-SL/07 — Annex C.**

Victoria read it again.

Alexander took the paper.

"Was this inside the original envelope?"

"I don't remember seeing it."

"Could it have been placed there during archiving?"

"Possibly."

He examined the fold marks.

The paper had been folded to fit inside the pouch.

It was not part of the letter from Marianne.

Nor was it a page from the St Catherine's card.

Victoria looked at the words.

"Why would someone send a separate instruction to the originating family representative?"

Alexander did not answer.

He turned the paper over.

There was a faint impression on the reverse side, as if another document had once been written above it.

Only a few words were legible.

**Maternal reference retained separately.**

Below that:

**Do not disclose receiving identity.**

Victoria felt a chill pass through her.

"Alexander."

He looked at her.

"This was not simply a warning about the band."

"No."

"It was an instruction about withholding an identity."

Alexander placed the note carefully on the table.

"Then we need to establish who sent it."

Victoria looked towards the cabinet.

For the first time, the possibility occurred to her that the material she had preserved for eighteen years might not have been incomplete by accident.

Someone had deliberately separated it.

And someone had wanted the original family to receive only part of the evidence.

Edward received the Aurelius family's message the following morning.

Victoria had found an additional note in the original archival pouch.

He requested a high-resolution copy.

When it arrived, he examined the reference.

**EFO-SL/07 — Annex C.**

He searched the investigation index.

The reference appeared in several places.

The original transfer instructions.

The restricted identity register.

The correspondence between Gabriel and Charles.

The external liaison records.

But Annex C had never appeared in the documents previously supplied to him.

Edward called the records officer responsible for the recovered archive.

"I need the complete EFO-SL/07 annex register."

"We've already supplied the main register."

"Not the annex register. I need every listed annex, including those marked missing, withdrawn or restricted."

The officer hesitated.

"That archive is incomplete."

"Then give me the incomplete register."

"I'll check."

Edward ended the call.

He looked again at the note.

**Maternal reference retained separately.**

**Do not disclose receiving identity.**

The instruction could have been intended to protect a child's identity.

It could also have been used to conceal the child's movements from the family whose identity was supposed to be protected.

And there was another troubling possibility.

If the maternal reference had been retained separately, the receiving identity might have been assigned without the originating mother's knowledge.

Edward wrote a new question in his notebook.

**Who was the originating family representative?**

The answer should have been simple.

Victoria Aurelius.

But the wording suggested that the person receiving the note might not have been the person whose child was being discussed.

He looked at the reference again.

Then his phone rang.

It was Jonathan.

"We found the Annex C register."

"Where?"

"In the private archive associated with Charles Vale's legal office."

"What's listed?"

Jonathan took a breath.

"Three separate entries."

Edward waited.

"Annex A concerns financial authorisation. Annex B concerns temporary custody. Annex C concerns maternal identity and receiving identity."

"Who signed Annex C?"

"That's the problem."

Jonathan paused.

"The register lists two originating mothers."

Edward stood.

"Which two?"

"Victoria Aurelius."

A pause.

"And Isabella Vale."

Edward looked at the note in his hand.

Two mothers.

Two separate identity records.

Two infants whose references had crossed through the same receiving network.

And an instruction warning that maternal identity must not be attached to receiving identity.

"Is the actual Annex C available?" he asked.

"Not yet. The register says it was divided into two sealed parts."

"Where?"

Jonathan was silent for a moment.

"Part One was retained by the originating medical institution."

"And Part Two?"

Jonathan read the final line.

"Part Two was delivered to the person designated as the receiving identity custodian."

Edward looked at the note again.

**Do not disclose receiving identity.**

"Who was that person?" he asked.

Jonathan read the name.

"Margaret Voss."

Edward slowly lowered the phone.

Margaret Voss had been responsible for monitoring the restricted identity records.

She had received the original Category 17 register.

She had witnessed the correction to Victoria's signature.

She had signed administrative custody documents.

And now her name appeared as the designated custodian of a sealed file containing the maternal and receiving identities of both mothers.

Edward looked at the empty space on his desk where the original register should have been.

For eighteen years, the investigation had followed the children through hospitals, clinics, private offices and temporary residences.

But perhaps the most important document had never been a transfer record.

It had been the one document specifically created to connect the mothers to the children.

And Margaret Voss had been entrusted with it.
