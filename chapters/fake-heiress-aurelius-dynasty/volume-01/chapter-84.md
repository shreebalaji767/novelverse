# **Chapter 84 — The Person Who Was Supposed to Notify Them**

The initials R.V. appeared in three different places.

On the Annex C release instruction.

In the restricted identity register.

And in a payment authorisation recovered from Charles Vale's legal archive.

Edward had initially assumed they referred to a person's initials.

But the wording on the release instruction had made that interpretation unlikely.

**Notification authority: R.V.**

The designation appeared to describe a responsibility rather than an individual.

Someone had been appointed to verify the receiving identity and notify the relevant family before the sealed records could be released.

The problem was that the appointment register had disappeared.

Edward stood beside his desk, looking at the three references.

"Whoever held that authority was supposed to complete the process," he said.

Jonathan sat opposite him.

"Could it have been a medical officer?"

"Possibly."

"Or someone from the Family Office?"

"Also possible."

"Then why is the name missing?"

"Because the register may have been kept separately."

Jonathan opened his notebook.

"The payment authorisation might help. If someone was paid to perform the notification, there should be a beneficiary."

Edward nodded.

"Start with the payment."

The financial records were recovered from an old archive maintained by Charles Vale's legal company.

They were not complete.

Several invoices had been transferred to another accounting office, and the original supporting schedules were missing.

But one payment entry survived.

**Reference: EFO-SL/07-RV.**

**Description: Receiving verification and family notification.**

**Payment status: Released.**

**Authorised by: Charles Vale.**

**Beneficiary: Restricted.**

Edward read the entry.

"Restricted beneficiary."

Jonathan leaned closer.

"That usually means the name was recorded in a separate schedule."

"Then we need the schedule."

"The archive index lists it as Schedule R."

"Where?"

"Not in the main financial archive."

Edward looked at him.

"Where was it transferred?"

Jonathan checked the index.

"Private custody register."

"Who held that?"

"Margaret Voss."

Edward closed the folder.

"Of course."

Margaret Voss received Edward's request without surprise.

"I expected you would eventually ask about the notification authority," she said.

Edward sat opposite her.

"You know what R.V. means?"

"Receiving Verification."

"Who held the authority?"

Margaret looked at the documents.

"It was not a single person."

Edward paused.

"Explain."

"R.V. was a restricted administrative function. It required two separate approvals. One from the medical records authority and one from the receiving identity custodian."

"Who were those people?"

"The medical records authority was supposed to be Dr. Samuel Hale."

"And the receiving identity custodian?"

"That depended on the child."

"Who made the appointment?"

"Charles Vale submitted the designation."

"Did you approve it?"

"No. I recorded it."

Edward placed the payment authorisation on the table.

"Why was a payment released?"

"Because the verification process involved external medical and administrative work."

"Was the payment made to Dr. Hale?"

Margaret shook her head.

"I don't know."

"Was it made to the receiving identity custodian?"

"I don't know."

"Then who was paid?"

Margaret opened her handbag and removed a small folder.

"I kept a copy of the schedule."

Edward looked at her.

"Why?"

"Because the beneficiary was not identified in the main financial register. I thought it might become important."

She placed a folded sheet on the table.

The heading read:

**SCHEDULE R — RESTRICTED BENEFICIARY**

Below it was a reference number.

**RV-02.**

The beneficiary's name had been covered by a rectangular black mark.

Edward examined the page.

"Is that how you received it?"

"No. The name was visible."

"Who covered it?"

"I don't know. I made this copy after the original was returned to Charles's office."

"Do you remember the name?"

Margaret hesitated.

"I remember that it was not a medical officer."

"Who was it?"

She looked at Edward.

"Marianne Cross."

Edward did not immediately react.

He read the schedule again.

Marianne Cross had delivered records.

She had signed for an infant at Northbridge.

She had collected documents from Meridian.

She had acted under Eleanor Vale's name on at least one occasion.

And now she appeared as the beneficiary of the payment for receiving verification and family notification.

"Was Marianne appointed as the receiving identity custodian?" he asked.

Margaret shook her head.

"No. She was appointed as the administrative liaison."

"Then why was she paid?"

"That was the description on the schedule."

"Did she have authority to notify the families?"

"Not by herself."

"Who did?"

"The medical records authority and the receiving identity custodian were supposed to complete the verification. Marianne was responsible for delivering the notification once both approvals had been obtained."

Edward looked at the payment record.

"Was the payment released before the verification was completed?"

Margaret examined the dates.

"Yes."

"How long before?"

"At least several days."

"Was that permitted?"

"No."

Edward looked at her.

"Then why did you record it?"

"Because the payment had already been authorised by Charles."

"Did you raise an objection?"

"I did."

"What happened?"

"Charles said the payment was an advance for administrative work."

Edward leaned forward.

"Did Marianne ever complete the notification?"

Margaret shook her head.

"Not according to the register."

"Did she submit a report?"

"No."

"Did she return the money?"

"I don't know."

Edward placed the schedule inside a protective folder.

"Then we need to ask her."

Marianne Cross arrived at Edward's office the following afternoon.

She examined the payment schedule without speaking.

"Do you recognise this?" Edward asked.

"Yes."

"You were the beneficiary."

"I was paid for administrative work."

"Receiving verification and family notification."

"That was the service description."

"Did you notify either family?"

"No."

"Why not?"

"Because the verification was never completed."

Edward placed the Annex C release instruction beside the schedule.

"Who told you that?"

"Dr. Hale."

"Did he tell you which part of the verification was incomplete?"

"He said the maternal records had been received, but the receiving identity schedule was still sealed."

Edward nodded.

"Did you ask for it?"

"Yes."

"Who refused?"

"Charles."

"Did you tell him that the notification could not proceed without it?"

"Yes."

"What did he say?"

Marianne looked down.

"He said the families would be informed when the arrangements were finalised."

"Did you believe him?"

"I believed that he intended to complete the process."

Edward studied her.

"Did you know the children had already been placed?"

"I knew that temporary placements had been arranged."

"Did you know where they were?"

"Not consistently."

"Did you know which child belonged to which mother?"

"No."

"Then how could you have notified the families?"

Marianne looked at him.

"I couldn't."

"Yet you accepted payment for the work."

"I accepted payment for the administrative work I had already performed."

"Which work?"

"Collecting records, delivering documents, coordinating transfers and maintaining the correspondence."

"Did you perform the family notification?"

"No."

"Did you return the portion of the payment allocated to that service?"

Marianne hesitated.

"I don't remember."

Edward placed the schedule before her.

"Then I suggest you find out."

She looked at him.

"Mr. Valmont, I understand why this appears suspicious. But I was never the person deciding which child belonged to which mother."

"Who was?"

"I don't know."

"Who had access to the receiving identity schedule?"

"Margaret Voss and Adrian Holt."

"Who had authority to release it?"

"Charles."

"Did you ever see Part Two of Annex C?"

"No."

"Did you ever see the receiving identities written beside the maternal records?"

Marianne shook her head.

"No."

Edward watched her.

"Then tell me something. Why did you sign for the IV-204 infant on the sixth day?"

Marianne's expression tightened.

"Because Gabriel told me the medical receiving officer was unavailable and that the child needed to be accepted into temporary custody."

"Did you know that the receiving record would later be connected to STC-A?"

"No."

"Did you know the same reference would appear in the ninth-day register?"

"No."

"Did you ever ask why the previous receiving reference was carried forward?"

"I asked Gabriel."

"What did he say?"

"He said the reference had to remain attached until the originating identity review was complete."

Edward leaned back.

"That is the same explanation you gave us before."

"Because it is the truth."

"Did Gabriel tell you that the two references belonged to the same child?"

Marianne was silent.

Then she said, "He told me that the records were connected."

"That is not the same thing."

"No."

"What did you understand by connected?"

"That one record had to be examined against the other before anyone could assign a final identity."

Edward made a note.

"Did you ever see both infants together?"

Marianne looked at him.

"Yes."

"When?"

"At Northbridge."

"On which day?"

"The ninth day."

"Was one of them IV-204?"

"I cannot identify them now."

"Was one of them STC-A?"

"Both were recorded under separate references at different points."

Edward stopped writing.

"Both?"

Marianne looked uncomfortable.

"I remember seeing two female infants in the receiving ward. I was not told that either was the child associated with the other reference."

"Did you know whether both had been transferred from St Catherine's?"

"No."

"Did you know whether one had come from Isabella Vale's clinic?"

"No."

Edward closed his notebook.

"Then we need the original ward register."

Marianne nodded.

"I understand."

At Northbridge, the ward register was found in a separate medical archive.

It contained daily entries for infants admitted under temporary custody.

Edward reviewed the pages with the hospital's records officer.

There were two entries on the ninth day.

The first:

**STC-A — Female infant.**

**Receiving representative: Marianne Cross.**

**Medical custodian: Dr. Samuel Hale.**

The second:

**NB-061 — Female infant.**

**Previous reference: IV-204.**

**Medical custodian: Dr. Samuel Hale.**

Edward read both entries carefully.

The two infants had been present in the same ward.

They had separate temporary identification numbers.

But the second entry carried the previous reference IV-204.

The records officer pointed to a note in the margin.

"There's a clarification."

Edward leaned closer.

**NB-061 to remain under temporary identification pending originating reconciliation.**

"Who wrote that?"

The officer checked the signature.

"Dr. Hale."

Edward looked at the next entry.

On the tenth day, NB-061 had been moved to a different section of the facility.

STC-A had remained in the original ward.

The movement records were signed by two different nurses.

The entries appeared ordinary.

But the receiving identity schedule had never been completed.

Edward requested the nursing notes.

They were retrieved from a separate box.

One nurse remembered the two infants because they had arrived within three days of each other and had initially been placed in the same nursery.

She did not remember their faces.

She remembered the identification bands.

"One had a temporary band with a number beginning NB," she said during the interview.

"And the other?"

"Another temporary band. I don't remember the number."

"Did either infant have an original hospital band?"

"I don't think so."

"Did anyone ask you to change the bands?"

"No. We followed the medical records instructions."

"Did you ever see someone remove an identification band?"

The nurse considered the question.

"Yes."

Edward looked up.

"From which infant?"

"I don't know."

"Who removed it?"

"A records officer."

"Was it Dr. Hale?"

"No. A woman."

"Do you remember her?"

The nurse shook her head.

"Not her name. She was carrying a brown leather case."

Edward looked at Jonathan.

The description was familiar.

The older woman with grey hair and glasses who had collected the M-204 band at Westbridge.

But the nurse could not identify her.

And the brown leather case was not enough to establish that the woman was the same person.

"Did she take the infant?"

"No. She took the band."

"Which band?"

"I don't remember."

Edward thanked her.

Another detail had emerged.

Someone had removed an identification band from an infant in the Northbridge ward.

But the nurse could not say which infant it belonged to.

Back at the Valmont estate, Elena was preparing for her interview.

She had decided to practise the presentation without looking at the slides.

Sebastian had agreed to listen.

He sat across from her in the study, with a notebook and a cup of coffee.

"Start."

Elena took a breath.

"Small businesses often maintain financial records using notebooks, spreadsheets or several disconnected applications. This can create problems when information is incomplete, duplicated or difficult to retrieve."

Sebastian nodded.

"Continue."

"My proposed system keeps the essential records in one place. It allows the user to record daily sales, expenses and stock movements. It also provides a basic summary of the business's financial activity."

"How does it prevent mistakes?"

"Each transaction has a date, category and reference. If the user makes a correction, the original entry is retained in the history."

"What happens if the device is lost?"

"The user can export a backup and restore it on another device."

"Where is the data stored?"

"Locally in the browser."

Sebastian looked at her.

"Does that mean it isn't available across several devices?"

"Not automatically. That would require a server or another synchronisation method."

"Then why not add one?"

"Because I want the basic version to work without an account, a subscription or an external service."

Sebastian nodded.

"Fair enough."

Elena moved to the next slide.

"Another important feature is that the system should not force users to understand complicated accounting terms. A shopkeeper should be able to record a sale without first learning an entire accounting system."

Sebastian closed his notebook.

"Good."

Elena looked at him.

"That's all?"

"Yes."

"You didn't ask anything difficult."

"I did. You answered."

She smiled.

"Thank you."

He stood.

"One more thing."

"What?"

"Don't apologise for keeping the project simple."

Elena looked at him.

"Why would I apologise?"

"Because people sometimes mistake complicated features for better work."

She thought about that.

"That's true."

Sebastian collected his notebook.

"Then go and show them what you made."

Elena returned to her laptop.

For the first time, she felt ready.

The next morning, Edward received a call from Dr. Samuel Hale's former medical assistant.

She had been contacted after the Northbridge ward register was located.

"I remember the Annex C material," she said.

Edward opened his notebook.

"Which part?"

"Part One."

"Do you remember receiving it?"

"No. I remember Dr. Hale asking for it."

"Why?"

"He said the maternal records were incomplete and that he needed to compare them with the original neonatal records."

"Did he receive the documents?"

"Yes."

"Did he complete the comparison?"

She hesitated.

"Not fully."

"Why?"

"Because the receiving identity schedule was missing."

Edward closed his eyes briefly.

"Did he request Part Two?"

"Several times."

"Who refused?"

"Someone from the Family Office."

"Do you remember the name?"

"No."

"Did Dr. Hale ever speak to Charles Vale?"

"Yes."

"About the file?"

"Yes."

"What did he say?"

The former assistant paused.

"He said he couldn't complete the review unless he knew which receiving record belonged to which infant."

Edward wrote that down.

"Did Charles respond?"

"He told Dr. Hale that the receiving identities would be supplied after the medical review."

Edward stared at the page.

"That is backwards."

"That's what Dr. Hale said."

"Did he refuse to proceed?"

"Yes."

"Did he return Part One?"

"No. He kept it sealed."

Edward leaned forward.

"Where is it now?"

"I don't know."

"Did he ever tell you?"

"He said it had to remain available for the originating authority."

"Did he say who that authority was?"

The woman hesitated.

Then she answered.

"Victoria Aurelius."

Edward ended the call and sat motionless.

Part One had been released under Victoria's general authority.

Dr. Hale had received it.

He had refused to complete the comparison without Part Two.

And he had apparently retained the maternal records for the originating authority.

The release receipt said the material was to be returned only after maternal identity reconciliation.

But the medical assistant's recollection suggested that the reconciliation had never occurred.

Edward opened the archive register again.

There was a final custody notation.

**Part One — retained pending return to originating authority.**

The next line contained a date.

**Return scheduled: Day 61.**

The final field was blank.

No receiving signature.

No return acknowledgement.

Edward picked up his telephone.

"Jonathan."

"Yes?"

"Find out whether Dr. Hale is still alive."

A pause.

"Why?"

"Because we need to know what happened to Annex C, Part One."

"Didn't we already establish that it was transferred to him?"

"We established that he received it."

Edward looked at the blank return field.

"We haven't established that he ever gave it back."

Three days later, Elena completed her interview.

She presented her system, demonstrated the transaction history and explained why she had chosen a local-first design.

The interviewers asked about data security, backup recovery and the limitations of offline storage.

She answered honestly.

When asked what she would improve if given more time, she did not promise a complicated new platform.

"I would test it with more small businesses," she said. "I would observe how people actually use it, identify where they become confused, and improve those parts first."

One interviewer smiled.

"Why not add more features?"

"Because a feature is not useful simply because it exists. If it makes the system harder to use, it may create more problems than it solves."

She left the interview room feeling lighter.

The result would take time.

But she had done what she wanted to do.

She had presented her own work.

On the way home, she stopped at a small bookshop and bought a book about practical database design.

She had no idea that, in another part of the city, an eighteen-year-old's identity was still being debated through files that were older than her earliest memories.

That evening, Jonathan called Edward.

"We found Dr. Hale."

"Where?"

"He retired several years ago. He lives outside the city."

"Is he willing to meet?"

"His solicitor says he will."

Edward looked at the custody register.

"Ask him about Part One."

"I will."

"And Jonathan?"

"Yes?"

"Don't ask him only where he put the file."

"What else?"

"Ask him whether he ever read it."

The meeting took place two days later.

Dr. Samuel Hale was in his late sixties.

He spoke slowly, but his memory remained sharp.

Edward placed the Annex C release receipt before him.

Hale examined it.

"I remember this."

"Did you receive Part One?"

"Yes."

"Did you open it?"

Hale looked up.

"Eventually."

Edward waited.

"It contained two maternal records and supporting hospital documentation. I was asked to conduct an identity reconciliation."

"Did you complete it?"

"No."

"Why?"

"Because the receiving identity schedule was missing."

"Did you request it?"

"Repeatedly."

"From whom?"

"Charles Vale."

"Did he refuse?"

"He said it would be released when the medical comparison was complete."

Edward stared at him.

"How were you supposed to complete the comparison without the receiving identities?"

Hale gave a tired smile.

"That was precisely my objection."

"Did you believe the children had been incorrectly identified?"

Hale considered the question.

"I believed the available records could not establish their identities."

"Did you suspect that two infants had been confused?"

"I considered it."

"Did you ever find evidence that they were?"

"No."

"Did you ever find evidence that they were not?"

Hale looked at him.

"Not enough."

Edward opened his notebook.

"Where is Part One now?"

Hale looked away.

For several seconds, he did not answer.

Then he said, "I returned it."

"To whom?"

"To Victoria Aurelius."

Edward stared at him.

"When?"

"On the sixty-first day."

"Do you have a receipt?"

Hale nodded.

"I kept a copy."

He opened a drawer and removed a document.

Edward took it.

The receipt confirmed that Part One had been returned.

But the receiving signature was not Victoria's.

It was another name.

**Adrian Holt.**

Edward looked up.

"Why did you return it to Holt?"

"Because he presented written authority to collect the material on behalf of the Family Office."

"Did you verify that authority with Victoria?"

"No."

"Did you verify it with Alexander Aurelius?"

"No."

"Then why did you release it?"

Hale looked troubled.

"The document carried the Family Office seal."

Edward examined the receipt.

The seal was genuine.

The signature was genuine.

But there was no evidence that the Aurelius family had personally approved the transfer.

He turned the paper over.

A short handwritten instruction appeared on the reverse.

**Part One to be retained with Part Two until receiving identity reconciliation is complete.**

Edward read it again.

The maternal records had been returned from Hale.

But they had not been returned to Victoria.

They had been transferred to Adrian Holt, who had been holding Part Two.

The two halves of Annex C had finally been brought together.

Not for the mothers.

Not for the medical review.

For the custodian.

Edward looked at Hale.

"Did you know Holt was already holding Part Two?"

"No."

"Did you know the two parts would be combined?"

"I assumed they would be preserved together."

"Did you ever receive confirmation that the reconciliation was completed?"

"No."

Edward placed the receipt inside his folder.

Another transfer.

Another genuine signature.

Another action taken under authority that had not been independently verified.

And this time, the missing material might still exist.

Back in his office, Edward called Jonathan.

"Find Adrian Holt."

"We already have his previous address."

"Not that. I need to know where he transferred Annex C after the sixty-first day."

"I'll check the custody index."

Edward looked at the two entries in his notebook.

Part One.

Part Two.

Both had been entrusted to Holt.

For the first time, the complete Annex C file might have existed in a single location.

And someone had been responsible for keeping it there.

Edward turned to the final page of the release receipt.

There was a small reference beneath Holt's signature.

**Archive destination: V-17.**

He searched the recovered index.

V-17 did not appear among the Family Office storage facilities.

It appeared under a different heading.

**Private Residential Custody.**

Edward read the location.

Then he stopped.

It was not a hospital.

It was not a legal office.

It was not a records archive.

It was a residential property.

A property registered to Charles Vale.

And the custody record contained one final instruction:

**No access permitted without the receiving family's written consent.**

Edward slowly closed the folder.

Someone had moved the complete identity file into a private residence and placed it behind a requirement for consent from the very families who had never been told it existed.

The next step was no longer simply to find a missing document.

It was to discover whether Charles Vale had kept the answers in his own home.

And whether he had ever intended to let anyone read them.
