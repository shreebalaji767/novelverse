# **Chapter 90 — The Address That Had No Family Name**

Edward placed the old instruction beside the anonymous warning.

The handwriting was unmistakably similar.

Both documents carried the same narrow lettering, the same unusually long crossbar on the letter *t*, and the same habit of pressing heavily on the final word of each sentence.

Marianne Cross had written the original instruction.

And someone had apparently written the warning left at the hospital.

"R-04," Jonathan said, examining the notation. "What does the R stand for?"

Eleanor Vale shook her head.

"I don't know."

"You saw it before?"

"Several times. Marianne used to refer to it when discussing restricted correspondence."

"Did she ever explain it?"

"No. I assumed it was an internal reference."

Edward turned to her.

"Did she have access to the original recipient schedule?"

"She had access to the notification instructions. She was not authorised to alter the recipient schedule."

"Could she have seen the names?"

"Possibly. But she was not supposed to retain them."

"Did she?"

Eleanor looked at the documents.

"I cannot tell you."

Jonathan leaned forward.

"Mrs. Vale, you have told us several times that you wanted the identities kept separate. Yet you never mentioned that Marianne was handling the notifications."

"I told you she was an administrative liaison."

"You didn't tell us she had written the instruction that stopped the process."

Eleanor's expression tightened.

"Because I did not know she had written it until I found the original correspondence."

Edward looked at her.

"When did you find it?"

"Three days ago."

"Why didn't you contact us immediately?"

"I wanted to verify the handwriting."

Edward glanced at Jonathan.

The answer was reasonable, but it also meant Eleanor had known something important before this meeting.

"Where is Marianne now?" he asked.

"She lives outside the city. I haven't spoken to her in several years."

"Do you have her current contact details?"

"I have an old address."

Edward wrote it down.

"Please give it to us."

Eleanor hesitated.

Then she removed a small card from her folder.

There was a handwritten address on the front.

On the back was the same code.

**R-04.**

Marianne Cross did not answer the first telephone call.

She did not answer the second either.

On the third attempt, someone picked up.

"Hello?"

"Mrs. Cross?"

"Who is calling?"

"My name is Edward Valmont. I'm investigating the St Catherine's and Northbridge custody records."

There was silence.

Then Marianne said, "I have nothing more to add."

"We have recovered a document bearing your handwriting."

"I have already explained my involvement."

"This document refers to R-04."

The silence lasted longer this time.

"Where did you get that reference?"

"From an instruction you wrote."

"I didn't write any instruction involving a final recipient."

Edward looked at the document in front of him.

"Perhaps you could explain what R-04 means."

"No."

"Why not?"

"Because you don't understand what you're looking at."

"Then help us understand."

Marianne exhaled.

"I am not discussing this over the telephone."

"Will you meet us?"

Another pause.

"Tomorrow. No recording devices. No other representatives."

Edward considered the request.

"I can meet you with a witness."

"Then don't come."

The call ended.

Jonathan looked at him.

"She wants to control the meeting."

"She wants to control what she says."

"Same thing."

"Not necessarily."

Edward saved the call record.

"Arrange a meeting. We'll make the conditions clear beforehand."

The following afternoon, Marianne Cross arrived at a small private conference room.

She was older than the woman described in the Westbridge records, but her posture was still straight and her expression guarded.

She placed a brown leather case on the table.

Edward noticed it immediately.

"Is that the case you used during the original transfers?"

Marianne looked at him.

"Yes."

"Arthur Bell remembers a woman carrying a similar case into V-17."

"I visited V-17."

"When?"

"Several times."

"Did you deliver documents there?"

"Yes."

"Did you collect them?"

"Sometimes."

Edward placed the old instruction on the table.

"Did you write this?"

Marianne examined it.

"Yes."

"Did you write the warning left at the hospital?"

She looked at the second document.

Her expression changed.

"Where did you get this?"

"At reception."

Marianne read the words.

**DO NOT OPEN PART TWO.**

**You have not found the correct family.**

She put the paper down.

"I didn't write this."

"The handwriting appears consistent with yours."

"Then someone copied it."

"Who?"

"I don't know."

Edward waited.

Marianne opened her case and removed a pair of reading glasses.

She examined the warning more closely.

"The spacing is wrong."

Jonathan frowned.

"What?"

"The original instructions were written by hand, but I used a particular format. The person who copied this has imitated the lettering, not the way I arranged the words."

Edward studied the paper.

"Are you certain?"

"Yes."

"Then someone has access to your old documents."

Marianne removed her glasses.

"Or someone has access to the same instructions I used."

Edward returned to the first document.

"Let's discuss R-04."

Marianne's expression hardened.

"That reference should not be in your possession."

"Why?"

"Because it was not a receiving family designation."

"What was it?"

"A protected contact address."

"Whose address?"

"An administrative receiving office."

"Which office?"

Marianne looked toward the window.

"Not a hospital. Not a residence. It was an independent correspondence facility."

Jonathan leaned forward.

"Who operated it?"

"I don't know the individual owner. It was maintained by an external administrative service."

"Vale Private Counsel?"

"Not exactly. They used it."

"Who else?"

"Several organisations."

Edward wrote down her answer.

"Was it used to receive documents for the children?"

"Yes."

"Which children?"

"That is what I was trying to establish."

Edward stopped writing.

"Explain."

Marianne folded her hands.

"At the time, the arrangement involved two originating maternity records and several temporary receiving records. My role was to deliver notifications once the receiving identities had been verified."

"Yet you were given R-04."

"Because all unresolved correspondence was supposed to go there."

"Why?"

"To prevent an incomplete notification from being delivered to the wrong person."

Jonathan looked at Edward.

"That sounds sensible."

"It would have been," Marianne replied, "if the process had worked."

Edward turned toward her.

"What went wrong?"

Marianne stared at the table.

"Charles Vale wanted the receiving arrangements completed quickly. He believed the children could be placed first and the paperwork corrected afterward."

"Who disagreed?"

"I did."

"Eleanor?"

"Yes."

"Margaret Voss?"

"She disagreed too."

"Dr Hale?"

Marianne hesitated.

"Dr Hale wanted the original medical material before making any identity determination."

Edward nodded.

That was consistent with the earlier records.

"And Gabriel Vale?"

"Gabriel wanted the transfer documents completed. He was less concerned about the final identity schedule."

"Why?"

"I don't know."

"Did you tell him the records were incomplete?"

"Repeatedly."

"Did he listen?"

"Sometimes."

Marianne looked at Edward.

"Mr. Valmont, you keep asking who was responsible for the missing identities. The truth is that several people were responsible for different parts of the process. Nobody had the complete authority. But everyone believed somebody else would finish the work."

Jonathan frowned.

"That doesn't explain why the children were transferred."

"No. It doesn't."

Edward returned to the address code.

"Who received the documents at R-04?"

Marianne shook her head.

"I never met the person."

"Then how did you know the address was valid?"

"I received acknowledgements."

"Signed by whom?"

"An administrative custodian."

"Name?"

"I don't remember."

"Do you have the acknowledgements?"

Marianne looked at her case.

For a moment, Edward thought she might refuse.

Then she opened the inner compartment.

"I kept copies."

The documents were held together with a faded elastic band.

There were six acknowledgement slips.

Each contained a date, a reference number, and a signature.

The first three were ordinary administrative receipts.

The fourth was marked:

**STC-A — RECEIVED FOR ORIGINATING VERIFICATION.**

The fifth:

**IV-204 — RECEIVED FOR MATERNAL REFERENCE REVIEW.**

The sixth:

**ANNEX C — RECIPIENT SCHEDULE PENDING.**

Edward examined the last slip.

It had been signed by an individual identified only as:

**R-04 CUSTODIAN.**

"Is this the person who received the original recipient schedule?"

Marianne nodded.

"Yes."

"Did you ever see the schedule?"

"No."

"Did you know whether it contained the names of the receiving families?"

"No. I only knew that the schedule had been sent."

Edward placed the slip beside the original V-17 records.

"Why was the schedule sent to R-04?"

"Because the receiving authority had not been confirmed."

"But if it was not confirmed, why send the schedule?"

"To obtain confirmation."

Jonathan stared at her.

"That makes no sense."

Marianne looked at him.

"It was not the schedule that needed confirmation. It was the identity of the person authorised to receive it."

Edward understood.

The records had been sent to an address where an unidentified custodian was supposed to confirm who could receive the information.

But the custodian's identity had never been established in the documents they had recovered.

"Who authorised the use of R-04?" he asked.

"Charles Vale."

"Did he know the custodian?"

"I assume so."

"Did he ever tell you who it was?"

"No."

"Did he receive a copy of the acknowledgement?"

Marianne nodded.

"Every time."

Edward wrote that down.

Charles had known the address.

He had known the acknowledgements were arriving.

And he had continued to control the process without completing the final designation.

"Why didn't you report this?"

Marianne looked at him.

"I did."

"To whom?"

"To Eleanor. To Dr Hale. To Gabriel. And finally to Charles himself."

"What did he say?"

"He told me to continue with the administrative work."

"Did he threaten you?"

"No."

"Did he order you to destroy anything?"

"No."

"Then why did you stop?"

Marianne's answer came quietly.

"Because I was being asked to notify families about children whose identities had not been verified. I refused."

Edward watched her carefully.

"Did you ever deliver a notification?"

"One."

Jonathan sat forward.

"To whom?"

Marianne did not answer immediately.

Then she said:

"To a family representative whose identity I was told had already been confirmed."

Edward's expression changed.

"Which family?"

"I don't know."

"Was it the Aurelius family?"

"I cannot say."

"The Valmont family?"

"I cannot say."

"Then what did you deliver?"

Marianne opened the case again.

She removed a folded receipt.

It contained a date and a signature.

But the recipient's name had been covered by a black strip.

Edward looked at it.

"Who concealed the name?"

"I didn't."

"Who did?"

"Charles Vale."

"Why?"

"He said the recipient's identity was confidential."

Edward turned the paper over.

There was a small note on the reverse.

**Notification delivered. Identity confirmation outstanding.**

Jonathan stared at it.

"How can notification be delivered before identity confirmation?"

Marianne looked exhausted.

"That is what I asked him."

"What did he say?"

"He told me the family had been informed, and the confirmation could be completed afterward."

Edward placed the receipt down.

"Who signed it?"

Marianne looked at the signature.

"I don't know."

The meeting continued for another hour.

Marianne provided what she could remember about the notification process, the R-04 address, and the correspondence she had retained.

But she could not identify the custodian.

She could not identify the family that had received the notification.

And she could not confirm that the notification had concerned either of the two newborns.

Before leaving, she looked at Edward.

"Do you still intend to open Part Two?"

"Only after we obtain proper authority."

"Then I need you to understand something."

"What?"

"The recipient schedule was never supposed to be used as a list of families who had already received children."

Edward waited.

"It was a list of proposed receiving identities. Some were temporary. Some were conditional. Some may never have become valid."

"Are you saying the children might have been placed with families who were never officially designated?"

Marianne looked down.

"I am saying the paperwork does not establish who was supposed to receive them."

Edward closed his notebook.

"Then why did you write that we had not found the correct family?"

"I didn't."

"Someone did."

"Then someone knows that the recipient schedule is not the answer you think it is."

She picked up her case.

"Don't assume that the people listed in Part Two were the people who actually raised the children."

That evening, Edward reviewed the six acknowledgement slips.

The final receipt had a date that did not match the chronology in the other records.

The Annex C recipient schedule had supposedly been sent to R-04 after the original custody process began.

But the acknowledgement was dated several weeks earlier.

Edward checked it again.

Then he checked the reference number.

The slip was genuine, according to the paper and stamp examination.

Yet the date placed it before the creation of Annex C.

Jonathan leaned over his shoulder.

"Could the date have been written incorrectly?"

"Possibly."

"Could it refer to another file?"

"The reference says Annex C."

"Then someone prepared the receiving schedule before the formal file existed."

Edward opened the old correspondence index.

The earliest reference to Annex C was dated the twenty-eighth day after the births.

The R-04 acknowledgement was dated the nineteenth day.

Nine days earlier.

He turned to the final page of Marianne's notes.

There was a handwritten line.

**Initial recipient schedule prepared before maternal comparison was suspended.**

Edward stared at it.

The proposed receiving identities had existed before the comparison process was stopped.

Someone had begun deciding where the children would go before the maternal records had been reconciled.

And now they had found the first indication that the process might have started even earlier.

At the Aurelius Estate, Victoria was reading a message from the Singapore programme coordinator.

It was not addressed to her, of course.

It had been forwarded by Edward as part of the ordinary investigation update, because Elena's academic progress had become a small but welcome part of the family's reports.

Victoria read the brief note confirming that Elena had accepted the programme offer.

She smiled.

"She is going."

Alexander looked up.

"Yes."

"For a semester."

"Yes."

Victoria placed the message on the desk.

"I hope she enjoys it."

Alexander returned to his work.

"So do I."

Neither of them said what both were thinking.

They still did not know whether Elena was their daughter.

They did not know whether the records would ever prove it.

And they had no right to interfere with a young woman's plans simply because they hoped she might belong to them.

For now, they could only wait.

Near midnight, Edward received an email from the successor archive organisation.

It contained the preservation confirmation for R-04.

The facility had been closed many years earlier.

Its physical records had been transferred to a secure storage provider.

The account had been maintained under a trust.

The trust's beneficiary was listed as:

**THE RECEIVING FAMILY — TO BE DESIGNATED.**

Edward read the line twice.

Below it was the name of the person who had created the account.

He leaned closer to the screen.

The name was not Charles Vale.

It was not Adrian Holt.

It was not Marianne Cross.

It was a name he had encountered only once before, buried in the earliest St Catherine's correspondence.

**Dr. Samuel Hale.**

Edward opened the original medical transfer file.

There, on the sixth-day record, was Hale's signature.

Beside it was a short handwritten instruction.

**Do not release the recipient schedule until the originating representative has been identified.**

Edward stared at the screen.

Hale had been responsible for medical verification.

He had also created the trust that held the records intended to identify the receiving family.

And the trust had never been assigned a beneficiary.

Edward picked up his telephone.

"Jonathan."

"What is it?"

"We need to find Dr Samuel Hale."

"Again?"

"No."

Edward looked at the trust record.

"This time, we need to find out why he created a family trust with no family named as its beneficiary."

And beneath the beneficiary designation, in a field that had remained untouched for eighteen years, was one final instruction:

**Beneficiary to be entered only after the child is correctly identified.**
