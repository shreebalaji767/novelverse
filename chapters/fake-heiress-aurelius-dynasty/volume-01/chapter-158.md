# Chapter 158 — The Fourth Person on the Access Register

The external representative's identity was held in a separate personnel schedule.

Edward requested it through the preservation authority.

The response arrived the following morning.

It contained four names.

Charles Vale.

Peter Ashford.

Martin Keane.

And a fourth person whose appointment had been recorded under a restricted identifier.

**R-17 — EXTERNAL PERSONAL CUSTODY REPRESENTATIVE.**

The schedule included an appointment date, a security clearance reference and a temporary access authorisation.

It did not include a name.

Edward read the final entry.

*Identity disclosure restricted pending independent verification of personal-channel authority.*

He placed the schedule on the table.

"Someone was granted access to the system without their identity being available to the people investigating the system."

Jonathan examined the authorisation.

"That person had access to PC-04?"

"According to this, yes."

"Could they approve a status change?"

"No. Their access was classified as preservation and correspondence handling."

"Could they enter instructions?"

"Not under the formal authority."

Edward looked at the word *formal*.

"Then we need the actual access logs."

The original PC-04 access logs were stored in the migration archive.

Martin Keane had already explained that the system used shared terminal accounts during the transition. Individual users were expected to authenticate through a separate access card, but not every action had been recorded with a personal identifier.

The archive officer brought three sets of logs.

Day 61.

Day 133.

Day 134.

Edward began with day 61.

At 09:42, the PC-04 terminal had been accessed.

At 10:16, the verification-key destination was changed.

At 10:21, the amendment was saved.

At 10:28, the terminal was closed.

The operator field contained only PC-04.

"Was there a physical access-card record?" Edward asked.

The officer searched the supplementary log.

"Yes. Two cards were registered during that period."

"Whose?"

"Peter Ashford and R-17."

Edward looked at Jonathan.

"Can we tell which card was used?"

The officer checked the entry.

"The card number is partially masked."

"Can it be recovered from the original security archive?"

"Possibly. The migration archive retained a protected copy."

"Request it."

The day-133 log was more complicated.

At 09:14, PC-04 had been accessed.

At 09:38, the original response verification request was opened.

At 10:26, the request was refused.

At 10:41, Helen Ward's channel-register access was suspended.

At 11:03, the personal response was transferred to the sealed preservation compartment.

At 11:17, the terminal was closed.

The sequence suggested that the refusal and access suspension had occurred within the same session.

Edward studied the timestamps.

"Could one person have done all of this?"

"Yes," Jonathan said.

"Could two people have shared the session?"

"Also possible."

The access card field was again incomplete.

But a separate building-security record showed that three individuals had entered the restricted records corridor that morning.

Charles Vale.

Peter Ashford.

And R-17.

Martin Keane had entered later, after the terminal was closed.

Edward examined the times.

"Who arrived first?"

"R-17, at 08:52."

"Who left last?"

"Charles Vale, at 12:06."

"Did anyone sign the access register?"

"Only Charles."

Edward leaned back.

"That establishes presence. Not authorship."

Jonathan nodded.

"Agreed."

Peter Ashford was the first person they interviewed.

He arrived with his old access authorisation and a folder of migration notes.

Edward showed him the day-133 log.

"Did you use PC-04 that morning?"

Peter examined the entry.

"I don't remember."

"You had access."

"Yes."

"Did you refuse the original response verification request?"

"No."

"Did you suspend Helen Ward's access?"

"I didn't issue that instruction."

"Did you transfer the personal response?"

"I may have processed the preservation transfer."

"Did you open the envelope?"

"No."

Edward placed the day-61 log before him.

"Did you change the verification-key destination?"

Peter looked at the record for a long time.

"I don't remember making that amendment."

"Could you have?"

"I had technical access. But the destination change required a restricted administrative function."

"Did you have that function?"

"During migration, yes."

"Under whose instruction?"

Peter hesitated.

"Some tasks were submitted through Charles's office."

"Was this one?"

"I can't say."

Edward watched him carefully.

"Do you remember R-17?"

Peter nodded.

"I remember the identifier. I wasn't told the person's name."

"What did they do?"

"They handled personal correspondence and custody instructions."

"Did they have authority to appoint the verifier?"

"No."

"Could they authorise the key release?"

"No."

"Then what was their role?"

Peter considered the question.

"To make sure documents concerning the protected personal channel were preserved without being sent to the wrong office."

Edward showed him the day-134 suspension notice.

Peter read the handwritten instruction.

"Do you recognise the writing?"

"No."

"Did you enter it?"

"No."

"Did you see it at the time?"

"I don't remember."

Edward closed the folder.

Peter had access.

He had technical capability.

But nothing yet proved he had made the disputed decisions.

Martin Keane's interview was shorter.

He confirmed that the migration system had retained the PC-04 shared account because the original software could not be transferred immediately.

"Did you know R-17 had access?" Edward asked.

"Yes."

"Did you know their identity?"

"No."

"Could you see their access-card details?"

"Only the restricted identifier."

"Did you ever receive a request to disable their access?"

Martin thought for a moment.

"There was a temporary suspension request."

"When?"

"Day 134, I believe."

"Who submitted it?"

"The Personal Custody office."

"Was it completed?"

"No. The request was returned because the identity of the authorised representative had not been independently confirmed."

Edward looked at Jonathan.

The same problem again.

A person had been given access under a protected identifier.

Then the office had tried to suspend that access without verifying who the person was.

"Was the access ever restored?"

Martin checked his notes.

"Not under the original card."

"What happened?"

"A replacement credential was issued under a different reference."

"Which one?"

Martin searched the migration log.

**R-17B.**

Edward looked at the entry.

"Who requested the replacement?"

"Personal Custody."

"Who approved it?"

"The approval field was blank."

Martin added, "I did not issue the credential. I only processed the system update."

Edward closed his notebook.

The access history was becoming more complicated, not less.

At V-17, Arthur Bell located a personnel envelope associated with R-17.

It had been kept in the same restricted cabinet as the PI-07 correspondence.

The envelope contained an appointment summary.

**ROLE:** External Personal Custody Representative.

**APPOINTMENT PURPOSE:** Preserve restricted personal-channel correspondence and maintain separation between personal responses and administrative records.

**AUTHORITY LIMITATIONS:**

- No authority to determine biological identity.
- No authority to appoint or withdraw the OC-19 verifier.
- No authority to release the PR-06 verification key.
- No authority to accept or refuse family allocation.
- No authority to speak for the protected principal without separately verified authorisation.

Edward read the limitations.

"Who appointed this person?"

Arthur examined the final page.

"Charles Vale's office submitted the nomination."

"Who approved it?"

"The appointment field is marked provisional."

"Was there an independent confirmation?"

"No."

"Was the person informed of the restrictions?"

"The acknowledgement field is blank."

Edward looked at the document.

The external representative had been given access to preserve the personal channel.

But the appointment itself had never been completed.

"Then R-17 was another provisional role."

"Yes."

"Was the person ever formally appointed?"

Arthur shook his head.

"Not in the records I have."

Edward turned the page.

A handwritten note appeared on the reverse.

*Temporary access shall not be treated as acceptance of the representative appointment.*

The handwriting was consistent with the anonymous writer.

Edward looked at Jonathan.

"Another incomplete appointment."

Jonathan nodded.

"And another person whose role may have been misunderstood."

The following afternoon, Eleanor Vale agreed to review the appointment summary.

She read it slowly.

"I remember the R-17 proposal."

"Did you know the person?"

"No. I knew the identifier and the restricted role description."

"Did you approve the appointment?"

"No."

"Why not?"

"Because the person had not received the original procedure, and the privacy conditions had not been independently confirmed."

Edward placed the access register before her.

"Yet the person was given temporary system access."

"Yes."

"Who authorised that?"

"Charles requested temporary preservation access."

"Was that within his authority?"

"He could arrange physical preservation. He could not independently appoint a personal-channel representative."

"Did you object?"

"I did."

"Is there a record?"

Eleanor nodded.

"Day 62. I sent an objection to V-17."

Edward asked Jonathan to retrieve it.

The letter was located in the Personal Custody correspondence file.

It stated:

*Temporary preservation access may be granted only for the handling of sealed material. It does not permit the temporary representative to enter, amend, suspend, or approve personal-channel status records.*

Beneath it was a second sentence.

*Any action taken through shared administrative credentials must be separately attributed to the individual responsible.*

Edward looked up.

"Did you receive a response?"

"No."

"Did you know the access remained active?"

"I learned later that the credentials had been retained."

"Did you know they were replaced?"

"No."

Eleanor read the R-17B entry.

Her expression tightened.

"That should never have happened without a verified appointment."

"Could R-17 have made the day-133 changes?"

"Possibly, if the person had access to PC-04. But the records do not establish that."

"Could the person have been trying to protect the sender?"

"Yes."

"Could they have been obstructing verification?"

"Also possible."

Edward nodded.

Eleanor looked at him.

"The important thing is that no one should be allowed to use an incomplete appointment to justify actions outside the role."

At the Aurelius Estate, Victoria listened to Edward's update.

"So the person had access to preserve documents but not to alter the records."

"Correct," Edward said.

"Was the access used improperly?"

"We don't know."

"Could this person have been the one who wrote the anonymous instructions?"

"Possibly, but the handwriting is not enough to identify them."

Victoria looked at the day-134 access suspension.

"Someone tried to suspend the representative's access, but that request also failed verification."

"Yes."

"Then the system was unable to confirm who was allowed to act, who was allowed to stop acting, or who was allowed to speak."

Edward nodded.

"That is a fair description."

Alexander looked through the documents.

"Did Charles know the appointment was provisional?"

"The records show he received the appointment summary."

"Then he knew the restrictions."

"Yes."

Alexander set the papers down.

"Knowing the restrictions is not the same as following them."

Victoria remained silent.

She did not want to assume that every failure had been deliberate.

But she also did not want another incomplete process to be mistaken for a valid decision.

Edward and Jonathan returned to Meridian to examine the physical access-card archive.

The original card records had been stored separately from the migration logs.

The archive officer produced two sealed entries.

**CARD R-17:** Issued day 59, temporary preservation access.

**CARD R-17B:** Issued day 135, replacement credential.

The first card had been deactivated on day 134.

The second had been activated the following morning.

Edward examined the authorisation.

The replacement had been approved under a shared administrative credential.

No individual signature appeared.

"Was the replacement card physically collected?"

The officer checked the receipt.

"Yes."

"By whom?"

"An authorised courier."

"Name?"

"Restricted."

Edward looked at Jonathan.

"Could the courier register identify the recipient?"

"It should."

The officer searched the collection record.

A name appeared.

**NORA WHITCOMB.**

Edward looked up.

"Nora collected the replacement card?"

"Yes."

"Was she told who would receive it?"

"No. She was instructed to deliver it to the Personal Custody office."

"Did you meet the recipient?"

"I handed it to someone in the restricted reception area."

"Did you see their face?"

"Yes."

"Could you identify them?"

"No. I wasn't given a name, and I did not know them personally."

"Did they sign?"

"They acknowledged the envelope using a code."

"Which code?"

"R-17B."

"Did they say anything?"

Nora thought for a moment.

"They asked whether the card would allow them to open the sealed personal response."

Edward leaned forward.

"What did you tell them?"

"I said I had no information about the card's permissions."

"Did they ask anything else?"

"Yes. They asked whether the original procedure had been delivered."

"What did you say?"

"That I had no delivery confirmation."

Nora paused.

"They said, 'Then the card cannot solve the problem.'"

Edward looked at her.

"Did you record that?"

"Yes."

She produced her contemporaneous courier note.

The sentence was there.

*Recipient queried whether replacement credential permitted access to sealed response. Courier unable to confirm. Recipient stated that credential could not resolve absence of original procedure.*

Edward read it carefully.

The person receiving the card appeared to understand that access to the records did not establish authority to read them.

That was important.

But it still did not identify the person.

Dr Sen compared the courier note with the other correspondence.

The phrase *original procedure* appeared in several C-19 requests.

It also appeared in the day-132 response inventory and the day-133 verification request.

The wording was consistent.

But Dr Sen warned against drawing a conclusion from that alone.

"These documents may have been prepared by different people using the same procedural terminology."

"Could the sender of the C-19 letters have been R-17?"

"Possibly."

"Could R-17 have been the proposed verifier?"

"Possibly."

"Could R-17 have been P-04?"

"Nothing establishes that."

Edward nodded.

The records had created several roles that could overlap in theory.

But they could not be merged simply because they shared a channel or a concern.

The last record Jonathan found was a day-136 access review.

It showed that R-17B had been used to enter the Personal Custody register.

The operator had opened three files.

**PR-06 / KEY CONTROL**

**OC-19 / NOMINATION**

**C-19 / PERSONAL CORRESPONDENCE**

No changes had been made.

The session lasted eleven minutes.

A note had been entered afterward.

*Access undertaken for preservation review only. No document opened. No personal response examined.*

The note was signed using R-17B.

Edward read it.

"At least someone recorded the limits."

Jonathan nodded.

"But we still don't know whether the person followed them."

"Correct."

Edward looked at the access history.

The credential had been used to examine the very files whose control was disputed.

Yet the record claimed no document had been opened.

"Can the system establish which pages were viewed?"

"Only the file-access events. Not every page."

"Then we cannot confirm the statement."

"No."

Edward closed the register.

The fourth person on the access schedule had become another unresolved identity.

They had been given temporary access.

Their appointment was provisional.

Their identity remained sealed.

Their actions could not be reliably attributed.

And they might have been involved in the same personal correspondence process that had never been properly verified.

That night, Edward received a message from Margaret Voss.

It contained a photograph of a document she had discovered in her own preservation file.

The heading read:

**R-17 / TEMPORARY ACCESS REVIEW — DAY 137**

The first paragraph confirmed that the external representative's access was to remain limited to sealed preservation materials.

The second paragraph stated:

*The representative shall not be required to acknowledge the substance of any personal communication until the original procedure has been delivered and the scope of the role independently confirmed.*

Edward read the sentence twice.

The third paragraph was more troubling.

*No further access shall be granted to the representative until the identity of the protected principal has been established.*

He stared at the wording.

The representative's access had been suspended because the principal's identity was not established.

But the representative's role was supposed to help preserve the channel through which the principal might communicate.

Once again, the process had made its own completion impossible.

At the bottom of the document, someone had added a final note.

*The representative has requested that their identity remain undisclosed until the original procedure is provided.*

Edward looked at the date.

Day 137.

The day after R-17B had been used to examine the records.

Someone had made a request.

Not for access.

Not for appointment.

For anonymity until the procedure was delivered.

Edward turned to Jonathan's earlier report.

The day-132 response had come from C-19.

The day-133 verification request had been refused.

The day-134 access suspension had been issued.

The replacement credential had been delivered.

And now, on day 137, the external representative had asked to remain unidentified until receiving the very procedure that had never been delivered.

Edward placed the photograph beside the sealed response inventory.

The fourth person might be the proposed verifier.

They might be an intermediary.

They might even be the person who had been trying to protect P-04.

But one thing was now clear.

Someone had repeatedly asked for the original procedure before agreeing to be identified.

And the system had repeatedly refused to provide it.

The next document he needed was the original day-137 request.

Because it might finally explain why the person behind R-17 had chosen to remain hidden.
