# **Chapter 28 — The Woman Listed as an Emergency Contact**

Edward had learned something important during the investigation.

A name in an old document was not necessarily a clue.

Sometimes it was simply a name.

The difficulty was determining which kind it was before someone built an entire theory around it.

Martha Voss had appeared in a residential lease application connected to Marcus Morelli. She had been listed as his emergency contact, and an investigator had added a handwritten note suggesting that she might once have worked at the hospital where Elena and Isabelle had been born.

It was an interesting connection.

It was not yet evidence of anything.

Edward intended to keep it that way.

He sat in his office with three files open.

The first contained Morelli's employment history.

The second contained the hospital's surviving staff records.

The third contained the information gathered about Martha Voss.

He began with the oldest record.

Martha Voss had worked at St. Catherine's Medical Centre for eleven years, beginning eighteen years before the present investigation.

Her position was listed as patient administration assistant.

She had left the hospital three years after the births.

There was no disciplinary action.

No dismissal.

No unusual payment.

No recorded connection to the Aurelius or Valmont families.

Edward read the employment summary again.

"Patient administration," he murmured.

That department would have had access to admission records, patient identification documents, and administrative transfer forms.

But not necessarily the authority to make decisions about newborn placements.

He called the investigator.

"Find out whether she worked in maternity administration."

"Her personnel record doesn't specify a department transfer."

"Then look for departmental rosters."

"We've requested them."

"Good. Also establish whether she was still employed when the two infants were discharged."

"She was. Her employment ended three years later."

Edward wrote that down.

"Has anyone contacted her?"

"No."

"Keep it that way until we know more."

He ended the call.

A few minutes later, his brother Sebastian entered without knocking.

"You're still working on Morelli?"

"Yes."

"Father wants an update."

"He can have one when I have something reliable."

Sebastian looked at the files.

"Martha Voss?"

"An emergency contact on Morelli's lease application. Former hospital employee."

"Which department?"

"Unknown."

"Could she have been one of the receiving guardians?"

Edward looked up.

"Possibly. But we have no evidence of that."

"Morelli's notebook says two receiving guardians were confirmed. It doesn't say they were family members."

"Exactly."

Sebastian pulled out a chair.

"Then we should consider whether the receiving guardians were ordinary people."

Edward nodded.

"That possibility has been considered."

"People who weren't connected to either family. People who might have believed they were helping a family in difficulty."

"Or people who were deliberately selected because they had no connection to either family."

Sebastian looked at the file again.

"Which would make them difficult to trace."

"Yes."

For a moment, neither spoke.

Then Sebastian asked, "What about the separation agreement?"

"Still missing."

"Charles Vale's office?"

"Nothing new."

"Could Morelli have kept a copy?"

"His personal correspondence may contain one. Margaret is checking the remaining papers."

Sebastian stood.

"Keep me informed."

Edward nodded.

As Sebastian reached the door, Edward spoke again.

"One more thing."

Sebastian turned.

"Don't discuss Martha Voss with the others yet."

"Why?"

"Because I don't want a name becoming a conclusion before we've established what it means."

Sebastian considered that.

"Fair enough."

He left.

Edward returned to the records.

The mystery was no longer simply about two children.

It was about the adults who had moved them, the people who had received them, and the reasons those adults had believed the arrangements necessary.

And somewhere among them was someone who had made a decision that had lasted eighteen years.

At breakfast, Catherine was trying to persuade Richard to attend a charity foundation meeting.

Richard was trying to persuade Catherine that he had already attended three such meetings that month.

Neither was making much progress.

"You cannot keep sending Sebastian in your place," Catherine said.

"I don't send him. He volunteers."

"He volunteers because you keep asking him."

"That is how volunteering works."

Nicholas nearly laughed into his tea.

Catherine gave him a look.

He immediately became interested in his breakfast.

Elena entered the dining room carrying a folder.

"Good morning."

"Good morning," Catherine replied.

Nicholas looked at the folder.

"University?"

"Yes."

"You're carrying it everywhere now."

"It's important."

"Is it physically attached to you?"

Elena sat down.

"Would you prefer that I leave it outside?"

"No. I was simply observing."

Isabelle arrived a moment later.

She was carrying her own folder.

Nicholas looked from one girl to the other.

"Oh."

Elena glanced at him.

"What?"

"Nothing. The university department appears to have taken over breakfast."

Isabelle sat beside Catherine.

"I've been reviewing the exchange programmes."

"Have you decided which one?" Catherine asked.

"Not yet. There are two possibilities."

"Would you like me to arrange a meeting with the university advisers?"

Isabelle hesitated.

"I'd prefer to speak with them myself first."

Catherine nodded.

"Of course."

Richard folded his newspaper.

"That is reasonable. You should understand the programme before committing to it."

Isabelle looked slightly relieved.

Elena opened her folder.

"I found another option last night. It has a better research component."

"Which university?" Isabelle asked.

Elena showed her the page.

They began discussing the course structure.

Richard watched them for a moment.

The two girls were becoming more comfortable around each other.

Not perfectly.

There were still awkward pauses, unspoken comparisons, and occasional moments when one of them would become conscious of the other's place in the family.

But they were learning to talk without every conversation becoming a reminder of what had happened.

Catherine seemed to notice it too.

She looked at Elena.

"Have you decided whether you want to continue helping with the foundation's student programme?"

Elena looked up.

"I haven't decided."

"There's no pressure. I only thought you might enjoy it."

"I might. But I don't want to commit if I'm going abroad."

"Then you can decide later."

Isabelle glanced between them.

"I was thinking of asking whether I could help with the programme assessment."

Catherine smiled.

"I would be happy to arrange that."

"Not as a special project," Isabelle added. "I'd like to work with the regular team."

"Then that's what we'll do."

Elena returned to her folder.

For once, the conversation had not been about which daughter belonged where.

It had been about what both girls wanted to do.

And that was a small but meaningful improvement.

At the Aurelius Estate, Julian was reorganising his list.

He had originally written thirty-nine activities he wanted to do with his future younger sister.

After several discussions with Lucien, he had decided that some of the activities might need adjustment.

For example:

**Item 4: Take her to the family amusement park.**

Lucien had pointed out that the family did not own an amusement park.

Julian had replied that this was a problem that could be solved.

**Item 12: Buy her an entire bookstore.**

Adrian had objected that she might prefer choosing her own books.

Julian had reluctantly changed it to:

**Item 12: Take her to a bookstore and allow her to choose as many books as she wants.**

Damien had asked whether there was a limit.

Julian had written:

**No limit, subject to reasonable transport capacity.**

Now he was sitting at the breakfast table, adding a new item.

**Item 40: Ask her what she actually wants to do.**

He looked at it for a long time.

Then he drew a box around it.

Victoria entered the room.

"What are you writing?"

Julian quickly covered the page.

"Nothing."

"Nothing seems to require a lot of concentration."

"It's a private project."

Victoria smiled.

"Is it about your sister?"

Julian looked down.

"Maybe."

She sat beside him.

"Have you thought about what she might be like?"

"Yes."

"What do you imagine?"

Julian considered the question.

"I don't know. Maybe quiet. Maybe loud. Maybe she likes books. Maybe she hates books. Maybe she doesn't like any of us."

Victoria's expression softened.

"Why would you think that?"

"Because she doesn't know us."

He looked at the list.

"And we don't know her."

Victoria gently touched his shoulder.

"That's true."

"Everyone keeps talking about finding her. But what if she doesn't want to be found?"

Victoria took a moment before answering.

"Then we will have to respect that."

Julian frowned.

"But she's our family."

"Yes. And being family doesn't mean we get to decide everything for her."

He thought about that.

Then he looked at item forty.

"Should I add another one?"

"What?"

"Ask her if she wants to be here."

Victoria smiled, though her eyes were slightly wet.

"That would be a good place to start."

Julian wrote it down.

**Item 41: Ask whether she wants to spend time with us.**

He put the pen down.

For the first time, his list seemed less like a plan and more like a collection of possibilities.

Martha Voss lived in a quiet residential neighbourhood on the eastern side of the city.

The investigator found her current address through public property records and confirmed that she still lived there.

She was sixty-three years old.

She had retired from hospital administration twelve years earlier.

She had no known criminal history, no financial connection to either family, and no obvious relationship with anyone currently involved in the investigation.

The investigator sent Edward a photograph taken from a public event several years earlier.

Martha was standing beside a group of former hospital employees.

She was an ordinary-looking woman with short grey hair and a composed expression.

Nothing about the photograph suggested she had been involved in a matter that had affected two families for eighteen years.

Edward studied the image.

"Did you identify her department?"

"Not yet. But we found something."

"What?"

"An old staff photograph from the maternity administration office. She's in it."

"During the relevant period?"

"Yes. The photograph was taken eighteen years ago, approximately four months before the births."

Edward leaned back.

"That's useful."

"There's more. Her name appears on the visitor authorisation list for the private nursery."

Edward sat forward again.

"What kind of authorisation?"

"Temporary administrative access. She was permitted to enter the nursery to verify patient documentation."

"On which dates?"

The investigator checked his notes.

"On three occasions."

"Dates?"

"The first was two days after the births. The second was the seventh day. The third was the ninth day."

Edward looked at the photograph again.

The seventh day.

That was when Eleanor Vale had visited the hospital and met both mothers separately.

The ninth day.

That was when the children had left the private nursery.

"Was she present during the final release?"

"We don't know. The visitor list only confirms that she had authorisation to enter."

"Find the entry records."

"We're trying. The hospital archive is incomplete."

Edward looked at the image for several more seconds.

"Did she know Marcus Morelli?"

"We haven't established that."

"But she was listed as his emergency contact years later."

"Correct."

Edward closed the photograph.

"Arrange a meeting."

"With Morelli?"

"With Martha Voss."

"Should we tell her why?"

"Tell her we're reviewing historical hospital administration records and would like to ask about her work at the time."

"Understood."

Edward paused.

"And don't mention the children by name."

Martha Voss received the request two days later.

She read the letter at her kitchen table.

The first time, she thought it was a routine records inquiry.

The second time, she noticed the name of the legal representative.

The third time, she placed the letter down and went to the cupboard.

There was a small wooden box on the top shelf.

She had not opened it in years.

Inside were old employment papers, photographs, a few letters, and a hospital identification card that had long since expired.

She took out the card.

**MARTHA VOSS — PATIENT ADMINISTRATION**

She turned it over.

There was a faded handwritten number on the back.

A department extension.

She sat down again.

The letter remained on the table.

She looked at the date.

Then she looked at the old identification card.

For several minutes, she did nothing.

Finally, she picked up her telephone.

She scrolled through her contacts until she found a number she had not called in years.

The call rang four times.

A man answered.

"Hello?"

Martha closed her eyes briefly.

"It's me."

There was a pause.

"Martha?"

"Yes."

"Is something wrong?"

She looked at the letter.

"Someone has started asking questions about the hospital."

Silence.

Then the man spoke again.

"Who?"

"Valmont's legal office."

The line remained quiet.

"They haven't asked anything specific yet," Martha continued. "But they want to meet me."

"Did you agree?"

"Not yet."

Another pause.

"Don't meet them."

Martha's expression changed.

"That isn't a decision you can make for me."

"I know."

"Then don't tell me what to do."

The man exhaled.

"Did they mention the ninth day?"

"No."

"Did they mention the private nursery?"

"They asked about my employment records."

"Then they haven't found everything."

Martha looked towards the wooden box.

"Perhaps they should."

The man said nothing.

She continued.

"I kept the copy."

This time, the silence was longer.

"Which copy?"

"The release register."

"Martha—"

"I kept it because I was told to destroy it. I never did."

The man lowered his voice.

"You shouldn't have kept that."

"I know."

"Does anyone else know?"

"No."

Martha looked at the old identification card.

"Not until now."

The man was quiet for several seconds.

Then he asked a question she had not expected.

"Does the document still have both signatures?"

Martha's hand tightened around the telephone.

"Yes."

"Both?"

"Yes."

A long silence followed.

Then the man said, very quietly:

"Then you must not give it to them yet."

Martha looked at the letter from the Valmont legal office.

For eighteen years, she had kept the document because she believed that one day someone would need to know what had happened.

Now that someone had finally come looking, she was being told to wait again.

"Why?" she asked.

The man did not answer immediately.

When he finally spoke, his voice had changed.

"Because one of those signatures belongs to someone who was never supposed to be involved."

Martha stared at the wooden box.

"Who?"

The call ended.

Edward received no confirmation from Martha that afternoon.

Instead, he received a short message from the investigator.

**Martha Voss has requested additional time before agreeing to an interview. She has not refused.**

Edward read it twice.

Then his telephone rang.

It was the investigator.

"There's something else."

"What?"

"We've confirmed that Martha Voss contacted someone shortly after receiving our letter."

"Who?"

"We're tracing the number. It appears to belong to an inactive mobile account registered under an old business address."

"Can we identify the subscriber?"

"Not yet."

"There's also a message she sent after the call."

"What message?"

"To the same number. We only have the carrier's timestamp and recipient information, not the content."

Edward waited.

The investigator continued.

"She sent it at 4:18 p.m."

"And?"

"The account was reactivated at 4:21 p.m."

Edward frowned.

"Three minutes later?"

"Yes."

"By whom?"

"We don't know."

Edward ended the call.

He walked to the cabinet containing the copies of the original documents.

He removed Morelli's notebook.

Then the ninth-day letter.

Then the photocopy of the visitor register.

He arranged them on his desk.

Morelli had completed the final arrangements.

Eleanor had kept the remaining records outside the Family Office.

Martha had retained a copy of the release register.

And someone had just reactivated an old telephone account three minutes after Martha contacted it.

Edward looked at the handwritten initials on Morelli's notebook.

**A.V.**

**C.V.**

For the first time, he wondered whether those initials were not simply abbreviations for the two families.

Perhaps they belonged to the people who had actually received the children.

And perhaps one of those people had been waiting eighteen years for the investigation to begin.

He picked up his telephone.

"Find the owner of that reactivated account," he instructed.

"Understood."

"And Edward?"

He paused.

"Yes?"

"Don't approach them alone."

Edward looked at the documents.

"Agreed."

He ended the call.

The investigation had finally reached someone who possessed a document from the ninth day.

But Martha Voss was not simply a witness waiting to be questioned.

She was afraid.

And someone had just reminded her that the past was not finished with them.
