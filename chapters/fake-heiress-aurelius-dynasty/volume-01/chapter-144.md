# **Chapter 144 — The Person Who Asked for the Missing Procedure**

Edward read the day-136 entry again.

*The sender has requested confirmation that the original procedure will be delivered before any further response is required.*

It was the first indication that someone had not merely been waiting for information.

Someone had been waiting for a particular document.

The original PR-06 communication procedure.

And they had made further cooperation conditional upon receiving it.

Edward placed the scan beside the day-133 collection request.

“Why would someone ask for the procedure to be delivered when another person had already tried to collect its control copy?”

Jonathan examined both documents.

“Perhaps they were different people.”

“Or perhaps the same person was involved in both events.”

“The collection request was submitted through Cross Administrative Services. The follow-up communication came through the protected personal correspondence channel.”

“Could the same intermediary have handled both?”

“Possibly. But the correspondence index doesn't identify the sender.”

Edward looked at the status line.

“Find the original communication, not another summary.”

Jonathan nodded.

“I've already requested the physical correspondence register.”

“Good. And find out whether the request was answered.”

Meridian's restricted correspondence room contained a separate register for documents that could not be attached to ordinary case files.

The day-136 entry appeared between two unrelated preservation notices.

Its reference was **PC-136/04**.

The register described it as a personal communication received through an authorised protected channel.

There were four fields.

**Sender identification:** Restricted.

**Recipient identification:** Pending.

**Document category:** Request for procedural information.

**Required action:** Release original PR-06 procedure.

The final field contained a handwritten note.

*No further personal response should be requested until the original procedure has been supplied.*

Edward read it aloud.

Jonathan turned the page.

“There's a response entry.”

It was dated day 137.

**RESPONSE ISSUED: ADMINISTRATIVE HOLD CONTINUES.**

**REASON: PR-06 ACTIVE.**

**DELIVERY OF ORIGINAL PROCEDURE: NOT AUTHORISED.**

Edward frowned.

“Who approved that?”

“The approval field is blank.”

“Who issued the response?”

“V-17 / Personal Custody.”

“Was it delivered?”

“There's no delivery confirmation.”

Edward sat back.

“So the person asked for the procedure, and someone replied that the procedure could not be delivered because the administrative arrangement that prevented its delivery was still active.”

Jonathan gave a tired smile.

“It's circular.”

“It's worse than circular. The person was asked to respond under a procedure they had never received.”

He looked at the original entry.

“Whoever submitted this request understood that problem.”

Dr Mira Sen arrived later that morning with the original correspondence envelope.

The seal was intact.

The envelope bore the same restricted personal correspondence classification as the PA-132 package.

Edward examined the outside.

There was no sender's name.

Only a small reference:

**C-19 / FOLLOW-UP COMMUNICATION**

He looked at Jonathan.

“C-19 again.”

The same temporary correspondence location where an unidentified adult had once declined to identify themselves without knowing who was requesting their information.

Dr Sen opened the examination record.

“The envelope was received at Meridian on day 136. Its seal is original. There is no evidence it was opened after receipt.”

“Can we examine the contents?”

“Not yet. The custody instruction requires independent identity verification.”

Edward looked at the envelope.

“Can we at least establish whether it contains the original procedure?”

“No. The inventory lists one folded document, but the description is deliberately limited.”

“Can we identify the writer?”

“Only if the document is examined.”

“And that requires the principal's identity to be confirmed.”

Dr Sen nodded.

Edward looked at the C-19 reference again.

“Then we need the original C-19 correspondence register.”

Cross Administrative Services had retained a copy of the C-19 access record.

Marianne Cross agreed to review it with them.

The first relevant entry was dated day 43.

An unidentified visitor had declined to provide their identity because the courier could not explain who had requested the correspondence or why.

The second entry was dated day 44.

The visitor was recorded as willing to receive information after independent verification.

The follow-up was suspended.

The third entry was day 136.

**PERSONAL CORRESPONDENCE REQUEST — PROCEDURAL INFORMATION**

**Visitor identification:** Not recorded.

**Communication received:** Yes.

**Request:** Delivery of original PR-06 procedure.

**Response:** Pending.

Edward looked at Marianne.

“Was this the same visitor from day 43?”

“I can't establish that.”

“Did anyone attend in person?”

“The access record does not say.”

“Who accepted the communication?”

“An administrative clerk.”

“Name?”

“Daniel Price.”

Jonathan wrote it down.

“Can we speak with him?”

Marianne checked her contact list.

“He left the company several years ago. But I may still have his forwarding details.”

Edward waited while she made a call.

After a few minutes, she returned.

“He agreed to speak with you.”

Daniel Price was living outside the city and working as an independent records consultant.

He remembered C-19 immediately.

“It wasn't a normal office,” he said. “It was a temporary correspondence point. We weren't supposed to ask visitors for information beyond what was required to receive their documents.”

“Did you meet the person who submitted the day-136 communication?” Edward asked.

Daniel shook his head.

“No.”

“Did you speak to them?”

“Through a courier.”

“Do you remember the courier?”

“A woman.”

“Name?”

“I don't know.”

“Did she carry a brown leather case?”

Daniel paused.

“Possibly. A lot of people used cases and document bags.”

“Did she say who had sent the communication?”

“She said it came from the protected personal correspondence channel.”

“Did she identify the sender?”

“No.”

“Was there an instruction about how to handle it?”

Daniel thought for a moment.

“She told me not to ask the sender to provide another response until the original procedure had been delivered.”

Edward leaned forward.

“Those were her exact words?”

“Not exactly. She said the person had already been asked to respond without receiving the document that explained how they were supposed to respond.”

Daniel looked at Jonathan.

“I remember because I thought it was an unusual complaint.”

“Did you agree with it?”

“I thought it was reasonable.”

“Did you record that?”

“I wrote a note in the intake register.”

“Can we see it?”

Daniel nodded.

“I kept a copy of the relevant pages when I left.”

He opened a folder and removed a photocopy.

The original entry had been typed.

A handwritten note appeared beneath it.

*The sender has not been given the original communication procedure. Further requests for personal acknowledgement may be premature.*

Edward examined the writing.

It was not the same handwriting as the anonymous restriction.

“Did you write this?”

“Yes.”

“Did anyone tell you to add it?”

“No. I added it because the instructions didn't make sense.”

“Did you send it to anyone?”

“Marianne's office.”

Marianne examined the copy.

“I don't remember receiving this.”

Daniel pointed to the routing stamp.

“It was sent.”

The stamp showed:

**RECEIVED — PERSONAL CUSTODY — DAY 137**

Edward looked at Marianne.

“Was it processed?”

She checked the old correspondence index.

“It was entered as a routine intake note.”

“Was anyone required to respond?”

“No.”

“Why not?”

“Because the sender's identity was restricted and no verified recipient had been appointed.”

Edward looked at the two documents.

The person's request had been received.

The concern had been recorded.

The procedure still had not been released.

And the system had classified the entire matter as routine correspondence.

“Did you ever see the person who was supposed to receive the procedure?” Edward asked.

Daniel shook his head.

“No.”

“Did you know whether the sender was P-04?”

“No.”

“Did you know whether the sender was a representative?”

“No.”

“Then what did you know?”

Daniel looked at the photocopy.

“Only that somebody was asking for the rules before agreeing to anything.”

At the Aurelius residence, Victoria received a copy of the day-136 correspondence summary.

She read the wording in silence.

Alexander sat opposite her.

“Do you think it was the principal?” he asked.

“I don't know.”

“Could it have been the person who visited C-19?”

“Possibly.”

“Or the representative.”

Victoria nodded.

She placed the paper down.

“What troubles me is the timing.”

“Which part?”

“The request came after the original procedure had been prepared and after the principal's acknowledgement form had been created. Someone had already been asked to respond. Yet the document explaining the process was still being withheld.”

Alexander looked at the date.

“Do you believe the person refused?”

“No.”

“Why?”

“Because they asked for the procedure.”

Victoria's voice remained calm.

“They did not say they would never respond. They asked to understand what they were being asked to respond to.”

Alexander looked at her.

“That is not a refusal.”

“No. It is a request for information.”

He closed the folder.

“We should not make assumptions about who sent it.”

“I agree.”

Victoria glanced at the day-136 entry once more.

“Still, I hope they understood that someone was trying to find them.”

Alexander did not answer immediately.

There was no way to know whether the protected principal had ever heard about the search.

There was no way to know whether the correspondence had reached them.

And there was no way to know whether they still wanted to be found.

In Singapore, Elena's project review had gone better than expected.

Professor Lin had examined the revised workflow and identified two remaining weaknesses.

The first concerned documents submitted after a review had already begun.

The second concerned changes to an application's status after a decision had been recorded.

Elena had added a version history, but the professor wanted the system to preserve the reason for every change.

“Suppose an application is marked incomplete,” he said. “A week later, someone changes it to rejected. What should the record show?”

“The original status, the new status, the person who changed it and the authority under which they made the change.”

“And if the person has no authority?”

“The change should be rejected.”

“Good. And what if the authority is valid but the underlying review is incomplete?”

Elena considered this.

“Then the system should allow only the actions permitted by that authority. It shouldn't let the reviewer convert an incomplete review into a completed decision.”

Professor Lin nodded.

“That's an important distinction.”

Elena wrote it down.

She had begun to realise that the hardest part of building a reliable system wasn't deciding what should happen when everything went correctly.

It was deciding what the system should do when something was missing.

When a person had not been identified.

When an approval had not been given.

When a decision had not been made.

She wanted the system to make those gaps visible rather than conceal them behind neat status labels.

After the meeting, Clara joined her for lunch.

“You're smiling,” Clara observed.

“I got useful feedback.”

“Did your professor finally stop finding problems?”

“No. He found two more.”

Clara laughed.

“Then why are you smiling?”

“Because now I know what to fix.”

They continued discussing the project while walking back across campus.

For Elena, that was a satisfying kind of progress.

Not everything was solved.

But she could see the next step.

Edward's next lead came from an unexpected place.

The C-19 communication register had a separate field for the delivery channel.

Most entries used ordinary courier references.

The day-136 communication had been entered under:

**PERSONAL DELIVERY — ROUTE 06**

Jonathan traced the reference through the Cross Administrative Services archive.

It led to a courier instruction prepared on day 135.

The instruction did not contain a sender's name.

It contained a single requirement:

**The communication must be delivered to the person who requested the original procedure. No substitute recipient is permitted.**

Edward read it twice.

“Who prepared this?”

“The author field is blank.”

“Was it delivered?”

“The dispatch record says completed.”

“Where?”

“C-19.”

“Who signed?”

Jonathan enlarged the scan.

There was a small acknowledgement mark.

Not a name.

Not a signature.

Just two handwritten initials.

**A.M.**

Edward went still.

“Anna Mercer?”

Jonathan looked up.

“Possibly.”

“Do we have a comparison?”

“Not yet.”

Edward examined the mark.

Anna Mercer-Smith had served as a temporary records liaison at St Catherine's. She had delivered documents, maintained correspondence and refused to certify the identity continuity of the infant transfer.

She had also helped draft the conditional response.

But none of that established that she was the person behind the day-136 communication.

“Contact her,” Edward said.

Jonathan nodded.

“Do you want to tell her about the initials?”

“No. First ask whether she handled Route 06.”

He looked again at the courier instruction.

*The communication must be delivered to the person who requested the original procedure.*

The person had asked for the procedure.

The procedure had not been delivered.

Someone had nevertheless prepared a second communication intended specifically for that person.

And the delivery record carried Anna's initials.

Edward placed the document in a separate folder.

There was still no confirmed identity for P-04.

But the investigation had found something it had not possessed before.

A possible link between the protected principal's correspondence and a person who had worked at the hospital where the original records began.

The next meeting with Anna would have to be handled carefully.

Because if she had been the courier, she might know who had asked for the missing procedure.

And if she had not been, someone else had been using her initials.
