# **Chapter 173 — The Request That Was Refused**

Edward printed the day-94 access request.

He placed it beside the day-89 retention authority.

The two documents were separated by five days.

One preserved the unresolved governance objections outside the external programme transfer.

The other attempted to obtain them.

**REQUESTING OFFICE:** Vale Private Counsel\
**REQUEST DATE:** Day 94\
**REQUESTED RECORD:** GOV-EXC-04\
**PURPOSE:** Consolidated programme review\
**REQUESTED ACCESS:** Full inspection and reproduction

The refusal was brief.

**RELEASE REFUSED — AUTHORITY NOT ESTABLISHED.**

Below it was a second sentence.

*The requesting office has not demonstrated authority to access records excluded from the day-90 transfer.*

Edward read the wording again.

The refusal had not said the documents were confidential.

It had not said the requesting office was prohibited from ever seeing them.

It had said Vale had not established its authority.

That distinction mattered.

He called Helen Ward.

“Do we have the original refusal?”

“I believe so.”

“I need the complete request, including attachments.”

“I'll check.”

“Also, I need to know who made the decision.”

“That's more complicated.”

“Why?”

“The refusal was issued through the restricted governance desk. The operational copy does not display the reviewing officer.”

Edward looked at the signature field.

There was no signature.

Only the identifier:

**GOV-CHAIR-02.**

“Then I need the historical credential record.”

“I've already requested it.”

“Good.”

Helen paused.

“Edward, there is something else in the refusal.”

“What?”

“The final paragraph.”

Edward looked down.

He had not noticed it.

*Any subsequent request must identify the specific authority under which the external programme custodian claims access to the excluded records. General programme continuity shall not be accepted as sufficient authority.*

He read it aloud.

Helen was silent.

“That's unusually specific,” Edward said.

“Yes.”

“Someone anticipated that Vale might rely on the programme's general administrative role.”

“Or someone had already seen an earlier attempt to use that argument.”

Edward looked at the day-94 date.

“Can we establish whether this was the first request?”

“I'll check the request register.”

“Please do.”

He ended the call.

The question was no longer simply why Vale had been refused.

It was what had happened before the refusal.

At Vale Private Counsel, Charles Vale received a copy of Edward's latest request through the records department.

He read the subject line.

**HISTORICAL ACCESS REQUEST — GOV-EXC-04**

He placed the paper on his desk.

Daniel Mercer stood opposite him.

“Edward is asking about the day-94 refusal,” Daniel said.

“Why?”

“He has the day-90 transfer inventory.”

Charles looked at him.

“Who gave him that?”

“The Governance Secretariat.”

“Did they release the excluded records?”

“No.”

“Then he has only the transfer inventory.”

“Yes.”

Charles returned to the request.

“Does he know about the day-94 application?”

“He has the refusal record.”

Charles looked up sharply.

“Where did that come from?”

“I don't know.”

“Has he requested the original correspondence?”

“Not yet.”

Charles closed the file.

“Prepare the day-94 request history.”

Daniel hesitated.

“Do you want the internal legal review included?”

Charles considered this.

“Preserve it. Don't alter anything.”

“Should we send it?”

“Not until the disclosure scope is established.”

Daniel nodded.

Charles opened a second folder.

The title read:

**EXECUTIVE CONTINUITY PROGRAMME — EXTERNAL CUSTODY**

He turned to the transfer inventory.

The exclusions were clearly marked.

He had signed the receiving acknowledgement on day 90.

He knew the four categories had not been included.

What he did not know was why the Governance Secretariat had refused the later request.

The distinction was important.

He had accepted the programme's operational records.

He had not accepted authority over every record associated with the programme.

And he had never been appointed to make personal decisions for P-04.

He looked again at the refusal.

Then he called the legal review office.

“I want the original basis for the day-94 request,” he said.

“Understood.”

“Not the summary. The submission itself.”

“Yes, Mr Vale.”

“And I want the correspondence explaining why the requesting office believed it had authority.”

The call ended.

Charles remained at his desk.

For once, the question he wanted answered was the same one Edward was asking.

Who had believed Vale possessed authority over records it had never received?

At the Valmont estate, Edward's request for the historical credential assignment received an answer shortly before lunch.

Helen Ward called him directly.

“The record is available.”

“Who held GOV-CHAIR-02 on day 89?”

“The credential was assigned to the sitting Governance Chair.”

“Name?”

“Dr Miriam Ashford.”

Edward opened his notebook.

“Was she the chair who approved the retention authority?”

“The assignment register confirms she held the credential. The approval log confirms the credential was used.”

“Does that prove she personally operated it?”

“No. The credential was protected, but the system allowed supervised access.”

“Was there a supervised session?”

Helen checked.

“Yes.”

“Who opened it?”

“That's recorded separately.”

Edward closed his eyes briefly.

“Of course it is.”

“I know.”

“Can we get that record?”

“I've submitted the request.”

“Was Dr Ashford the Governance Chair throughout the relevant period?”

“Yes. Her appointment began before day 70 and continued beyond day 90.”

“Was she involved in the day-75 amendment?”

“The central record does not show her approving it.”

“But she approved the day-89 retention authority?”

“The credential record supports that the approval was issued under her authority. We still need the operator record to establish who physically initiated the session.”

Edward made a note.

“Can we interview her?”

“Not yet. The panel will need to determine whether the interview concerns her official decision or a protected personnel matter.”

“Then request the official decision record.”

“I will.”

He thanked her.

After the call, Edward wrote two names on separate lines.

**Jonathan Price — GS-11, secondary review.**

**Miriam Ashford — GOV-CHAIR-02, retention approval.**

The day-75 amendment and the day-89 retention authority had travelled through different approval chains.

One had been treated as valid without a verified appointment.

The other had a recorded governance approval.

The two events suggested a conflict inside the administration itself.

But Edward still needed to understand the people behind the records.

That afternoon, Edward visited Sophie Grant.

She was in the records annex, sorting a collection of old meeting files.

When he explained the day-94 refusal, she stopped working.

“Vale requested the governance objection?”

“Yes.”

“Five days after the transfer?”

“Yes.”

Sophie shook her head.

“I remember a request like that.”

“Do you remember who submitted it?”

“No. But I remember the argument.”

“What argument?”

“That the Executive Continuity Programme was responsible for maintaining the arrangements and therefore needed access to every record connected to those arrangements.”

“Was that the wording?”

“Not exactly. It was something like that.”

“Did anyone object?”

“I did.”

Edward waited.

Sophie placed a file on the shelf.

“I said the programme's responsibility for administrative continuity did not automatically grant it access to personal correspondence or governance objections.”

“Who responded?”

“Martin Keane.”

“What did he say?”

“He said the request was being reviewed by the appropriate authority.”

“Did he agree with you?”

“I couldn't tell.”

“Did he ask you to withdraw the objection?”

“No.”

“Did he say the request should be approved?”

“No.”

Edward opened his notebook.

“Did you see the original request?”

“I saw a draft.”

“Where?”

“In the day-93 review folder.”

“Can you locate it?”

Sophie searched the archive catalogue.

After several minutes, she found a reference.

**EC-93/REVIEW — ACCESS SCOPE DISCUSSION.**

The file had been preserved in the ordinary administrative archive.

She requested it.

The document arrived as a scanned image.

It contained a short internal discussion.

**Proposed position:** The external programme custodian requires access to all records necessary to maintain continuity.

**Objection:** Excluded personal and governance records are not transferred programme assets. Access must be established separately.

**Response:** The custodian's continuity responsibility should be considered when determining access.

The final paragraph had been added later.

*If access is refused, the operational review may remain incomplete.*

Edward read the sentence.

“Who wrote this?”

Sophie examined the document.

“The handwriting isn't mine.”

“Do you recognise it?”

“No.”

“Was this part of the formal request?”

“I don't know. The file says it was a review note.”

Edward looked at the date.

Day 93.

One day before the refusal.

The internal review had already identified the problem.

Vale's responsibility for continuity did not establish authority over the excluded records.

Yet someone had continued to argue that access was necessary.

Edward turned the page.

At the bottom was a routing instruction.

**Forward to programme liaison for response.**

The recipient field contained a name.

**Gabriel Vale.**

Edward stopped.

Gabriel had previously held limited communication authority for P-04.

He had been permitted to receive information, communicate questions, return conditional responses, and preserve correspondence.

He had not been authorised to accept permanent responsibility, approve placement, confirm identity, or appoint a receiving custodian.

And he had never been established as the person who could grant Vale Private Counsel access to the excluded governance records.

Edward looked at Sophie.

“Was Gabriel involved in the day-94 request?”

“I don't know.”

“Did he receive this review note?”

“The routing record says it was sent to him.”

“Was there a response?”

Sophie searched the file.

There was one.

It had been received on day 94, at 09:06.

The refusal had been issued later that morning.

Edward read the response.

*The programme liaison authority does not extend to the release of excluded governance or personal correspondence records. Any access request must be submitted by an independently authorised receiving office.*

There was no signature.

Only a reference:

**G.V. — LIMITED LIAISON RESPONSE.**

Edward looked up.

“Gabriel refused to support the request.”

Sophie nodded slowly.

“He seems to have.”

“Did he have the authority to refuse it?”

“He had the authority to explain the limits of his role.”

“Not to decide whether Vale should receive the records.”

“Correct.”

Edward examined the wording again.

Gabriel had not claimed authority over the excluded material.

He had explicitly denied that his limited liaison role could be used to establish access.

The refusal had followed three hours later.

It was possible that the two events were connected.

It was not proof that Gabriel had caused the refusal.

Edward wrote down the distinction.

At five thirty, Edward received a message from the independent governance panel.

The panel had located the original day-94 submission.

It was available for restricted review the following morning.

The message included one additional note.

*The request was submitted by an authorised representative of the Executive Continuity Programme. The submitting authority and the programme custodian's receiving authority were recorded separately.*

Edward read the sentence twice.

The request had not necessarily come from Charles.

It had been submitted by someone authorised to represent the programme.

And the panel had distinguished that person's submission authority from the custodian's authority.

He called Helen.

“The panel has located the original request.”

“Good.”

“It was submitted by an authorised programme representative.”

“Do they identify the representative?”

“Not in the appointment message.”

“Then you'll find out tomorrow.”

Edward looked at the note again.

“There's another distinction. The submitter's authority and the custodian's authority were recorded separately.”

Helen was quiet.

“That could be important.”

“Yes.”

“Especially if the submitter had authority to request access but not authority to receive the records.”

Edward looked at the day-94 refusal.

“Exactly.”

He ended the call.

The following morning, the review took place in a smaller archive room.

Margaret Ellis sat at the end of the table.

Edward sat opposite her.

The original submission appeared on the screen.

**REQUEST REFERENCE:** EC-94-006.

**DATE:** Day 94.

**SUBMITTING OFFICE:** Executive Continuity Programme.

**SUBMITTING AUTHORITY:** Programme Liaison Office.

**REQUESTED RECEIVING CUSTODIAN:** Vale Private Counsel.

Edward examined the first page.

“Who submitted it?”

Margaret moved to the protected identity field.

**Submitting officer: Marianne Cross.**

Edward recognised the name.

Marianne had acted as an administrative liaison and correspondence intermediary in earlier records.

She was not the protected principal.

She was not the receiving identity custodian.

She was not the independent courier Nora Whitcomb.

And she was not Gabriel Vale.

“Did Marianne have authority to submit requests?”

“Yes,” Margaret said. “The programme liaison appointment permitted her to submit administrative requests and receive procedural responses.”

“Did it permit her to accept the requested records?”

“No.”

“Who was authorised to accept them?”

“The designated external custodian.”

“Charles Vale?”

“The custody acknowledgement identifies him as the receiving officer for the day-90 transfer. It does not automatically establish authority over records excluded from that transfer.”

Edward nodded.

He read the appointment reference.

Marianne's authority was valid from day 31 through day 120.

It covered administrative correspondence, procedural enquiries, and submission of requests on behalf of the programme.

It expressly excluded personal decisions and identity verification.

“Was the request submitted under her own authority?”

“Yes.”

“Was it submitted on behalf of Gabriel?”

“No.”

“Did Gabriel approve it?”

“There is no approval field showing that.”

Edward looked at the second page.

The request included a statement.

*The Executive Continuity Programme requires access to the excluded records to complete its operational review and maintain continuity across all outstanding arrangements.*

Below it was a declaration.

*The requesting office acknowledges that the records may remain subject to independent release conditions.*

Edward looked at Margaret.

“So the request acknowledged that the records were restricted.”

“Yes.”

“Then why did the internal review suggest general continuity was sufficient?”

“The request did not make that claim in absolute terms. It asked the receiving authority to consider the programme's responsibilities.”

Edward returned to the first page.

There was a handwritten annotation beside the requested receiving custodian.

*No transfer of custodial responsibility implied.*

The initials were M.C.

Marianne Cross.

Edward leaned back.

Marianne had submitted the request.

But she had also made clear that the request itself did not transfer responsibility.

He examined the refusal.

The governance desk had rejected the request because Vale had not demonstrated authority to receive the excluded records.

That was consistent with the conditions.

It was also consistent with Gabriel's limited response.

No one had established a valid receiving authority.

The refusal was not evidence of a secret agreement.

It was evidence that the required authority was missing.

Margaret moved to the final page.

“There is one more record associated with the request.”

Edward leaned forward.

It was a short administrative note.

**Day 94 — 11:42**

**Action:** Requesting office notified of refusal.

**Follow-up instruction:** No further request to be submitted until the receiving authority is independently confirmed.

**Originating office:** Governance Secretariat.

**Instruction acknowledged:** Yes.

**Acknowledging officer:** Marianne Cross.

Edward studied the final field.

“Did Marianne comply?”

“The subsequent request register shows no further application from her office during the next thirty days.”

“Was another request submitted by Vale?”

“No.”

“By the programme itself?”

“No.”

Edward looked at the date.

The request had been refused.

The instruction had been acknowledged.

And the access attempt had stopped.

For thirty days, no one tried again.

That was not proof that the dispute had ended.

It did show that the administrative boundary had been recognised.

He closed the document.

Then Margaret opened a separate attachment.

“This was not part of the original request,” she said. “It was retained because it was received through the same channel on day 95.”

The document was a one-page procedural enquiry.

It had been submitted by Marianne Cross.

The subject line read:

**CLARIFICATION — RECEIVING AUTHORITY REQUIREMENT**

The message asked whether the receiving authority had to be a person, an organisation, or an independently appointed representative.

The reply was issued later that day.

*The receiving authority must be separately identified, appointed for the relevant purpose, and capable of accepting the specific records under the applicable restrictions. Programme participation, document custody, and communication liaison do not by themselves establish receiving authority.*

Edward read the reply carefully.

It repeated the same separation that Eleanor had insisted upon.

Administrative participation was not personal representation.

Document custody was not identity authority.

Communication liaison was not receiving authority.

He looked at Margaret.

“Who answered this?”

“The restricted governance desk.”

“Same reviewing officer?”

“The operational copy doesn't identify the person.”

Edward nodded.

He had another request to make.

But before he could speak, Margaret turned to the final section of the attachment.

“There is a handwritten note on the original.”

Edward looked down.

The note was brief.

*Please do not allow the absence of an appointed receiving authority to be interpreted as permission to select one administratively.*

The handwriting was not Marianne's.

The initials were not Eleanor's.

There was no name.

Only a reference in the corner.

**GOV-CHAIR-02 — PERSONAL REVIEW.**

Edward stared at the notation.

The same restricted approval credential had been used for the day-89 retention authority.

Now it appeared on a day-95 instruction warning against administrative selection of a receiving authority.

He looked at Margaret.

“Was this note part of the official response?”

“No. It was attached to the retained original.”

“Was it included in the copy sent to Marianne?”

“No.”

“Was it included in the operational register?”

“No.”

Edward slowly closed his notebook.

Someone had added a warning that the administrative record did not contain.

Someone had insisted that no one should appoint a receiving authority merely because the proper person had not yet been identified.

And that warning had been kept with the original.

He looked once more at the credential reference.

The Governance Chair had not merely preserved an objection.

Someone using the chair's restricted authority had continued to defend the boundary after the day-94 refusal.

The question was no longer whether the governance office had noticed the problem.

It had.

The question was why its warnings had remained in restricted originals while the operational summaries continued to describe the arrangements as complete.
