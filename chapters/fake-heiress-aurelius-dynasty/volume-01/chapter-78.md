# **Chapter 78 — The Card That Never Left the Hospital**

The restricted neonatal custody register had not been destroyed.

That was the first thing Edward learned when he returned to St Catherine's Hospital.

It had not been lost during the hospital's administrative reorganisations, misplaced during the archive transfer, or discarded under an old document-retention policy.

It still existed.

It was simply not stored with the other neonatal records.

And nobody at the central archive had known where to find it.

Mr. Daniel Mercer stood beside the consultation table, holding a printed inventory.

"I've located the reference," he said. "The register was transferred to the hospital's former clinical governance office before the maternity department was reorganised."

"Is that office still operating?" Jonathan asked.

"Not under that name. Its records were transferred to the medical quality and safety department."

"Then we need access."

Mercer nodded.

"I've already contacted them. The current director is reviewing your authorisation."

Edward glanced at the reference.

**NC-17/04 — Restricted Neonatal Custody Register.**

The number appeared nowhere in the documents they had previously examined.

"Why was it restricted?" he asked.

Mercer turned the page.

"According to the archive index, it contained records of original identification materials retained by the originating clinical department. Access required the approval of the department's medical director."

"Not the records administrator?"

"No."

"Not the legal representative?"

"No."

Edward looked at Jonathan.

That was significant.

For weeks, the investigation had uncovered administrative authority being used to control medical records. But this register had been deliberately placed outside the ordinary administrative process.

Someone had understood that the original identification materials were too important to be handled through the same channels.

Or someone had wanted to prevent certain people from accessing them.

The current medical quality director, Dr. Amelia Grant, arrived shortly before eleven.

She was in her early fifties, composed and direct, with a habit of reading every document before discussing it.

After reviewing the authorisation, she agreed to provide access to the surviving register.

"I should make one thing clear," she said. "A custody register establishes who held a particular item. It does not establish that the item was biologically associated with a particular infant."

Edward nodded.

"Understood."

"And if the original material is still present, we will need a qualified examiner to assess its condition before anyone attempts to use it."

"Agreed."

Dr. Grant led them through a corridor into a smaller archival room.

Unlike the general records archive, this room contained locked cabinets and individually sealed storage boxes.

She entered a code and opened one of the cabinets.

"NC-17/04," she said.

A grey register was removed from the shelf.

Its cover was worn, but the pages inside had been preserved carefully.

Edward opened it.

The first entries were routine.

Identification cards retained for correction.

Bands temporarily removed for medical procedures.

Labels transferred after accidental damage.

Each entry included the item number, reason for retention, responsible clinician, date received and date released.

Then he reached the relevant page.

**Day 1 — Original neonatal identification materials retained under restricted custody.**

The first entry listed Infant A.

**Temporary reference: STC-A.**

**Item: Original identification card.**

**Custodian: Dr. Peter Lang.**

**Reason: Identity comparison request pending.**

Edward turned the page.

The next entry was dated the sixth day.

**Additional comparison reference received: IV-204.**

**Item: Maternal identification band and maternity admission record.**

**Custodian: Dr. Peter Lang.**

**Instruction: Do not attach the two originating references. Maintain separate custody until authorised comparison.**

Jonathan leaned closer.

"Both materials were physically at St Catherine's?"

"According to this register," Edward replied.

Dr. Grant examined the entries.

"That would have been unusual. The original band came from another clinic."

"Could it have been delivered here?"

"Yes. If the external clinic authorised the transfer and the receiving department accepted custody."

Edward turned to the next page.

The sixth-day entry included a second notation.

**Materials prepared for external comparison.**

**Dispatch reference: SC-CMP/06.**

**Receiving institution: Northbridge Clinical Review Unit.**

Then, beneath it:

**Original STC-A card withheld.**

**Reason: Clinical authorisation not completed.**

Edward stopped.

Jonathan read the line.

"That explains why the comparison office never received it."

"Yes," Edward said.

Dr. Grant looked at the register.

"But it doesn't explain why the card was withheld."

Edward continued.

The entry had been initialled.

**P.L.**

Peter Lang.

The same person who later suspended the comparison.

He turned to the following page.

There was another entry, dated the ninth day.

**Release request received from external legal representative.**

**Requested by: Charles Vale.**

**Purpose: Transfer to independent identity review.**

The request had been refused.

The reason was written in the margin.

**Originating clinical authority required.**

Edward looked up.

"Who had authority to release the card?"

Dr. Grant checked the register's front section.

"The responsible clinician."

"Peter Lang?"

"According to the initial custody entry, yes."

"Was he still employed here on the ninth day?"

"Yes."

Edward turned the page again.

The next notation was dated the tenth day.

**Original STC-A card removed from restricted custody.**

**Receiving authority: P. Lang.**

**Destination: Not recorded.**

For several seconds, nobody spoke.

Jonathan looked at the empty destination column.

"That's the missing card."

Edward nodded.

"And we finally know who last recorded holding it."

Dr. Peter Lang was no longer practising medicine.

He had retired several years earlier and lived in a quiet residential neighbourhood outside the city.

His name, however, still appeared on the hospital's former clinical governance records.

Dr. Grant arranged contact through the hospital's legal department.

Lang agreed to meet them that afternoon.

He was a small, elderly man with a straight back and an unusually steady gaze.

When Edward showed him the register entry, he read it without visible surprise.

"You found the custody register," he said.

"Yes."

"I wondered whether anyone would eventually look there."

"Why was the original card removed?"

Lang folded his hands.

"Because I was instructed to release it."

"By whom?"

He looked at the page.

"Charles Vale."

"Charles had no authority to remove it."

"Not by himself."

"Then who authorised the release?"

Lang did not answer immediately.

Edward placed a copy of the original custody procedure on the table.

"The register states that only the originating clinician could approve removal. Your initials appear beside the entry."

"That is correct."

"So you approved it."

"Yes."

"Why?"

Lang looked towards the window.

"Because I believed the comparison could not proceed while the hospital retained control of the original card."

Jonathan frowned.

"That makes no sense. The card was the very thing required for comparison."

"Not under the arrangement that had been proposed."

"What arrangement?"

Lang turned back.

"Charles Vale wanted the materials examined by a private review office. He believed the hospital had become too involved in the identity dispute."

Edward watched him.

"Was that a medical concern?"

"It was presented as one."

"And you agreed?"

"I agreed to release the card under conditions."

"What conditions?"

"That it remain sealed, that its movement be documented, and that no identity conclusion be issued without the originating records."

"Who received it?"

Lang looked at the document again.

"I don't remember the individual."

"Was it Gabriel Vale?"

"No."

"Marianne Cross?"

A slight pause.

"I remember the name."

"Did she collect it?"

"I cannot say."

Edward leaned forward.

"Dr. Lang, this card was essential to a comparison that might have established the identity of a missing child. You removed it from the hospital's custody, and the receiving office subsequently reported that it had never received the original material."

Lang's expression tightened.

"I did not know that."

"Did you ever receive confirmation that the card had reached Northbridge?"

"No."

"Did you ask?"

"Yes."

"Who did you ask?"

"Charles Vale's office."

"And what did they tell you?"

Lang looked down.

"That the comparison had been suspended because the remaining records were incomplete."

Edward held his gaze.

"Did you believe that?"

"I believed the process had been delayed."

"That's not the same thing."

"No," Lang admitted. "It isn't."

Lang produced a small personal diary from a locked case.

It was not a medical record.

It was a private work diary he had maintained during his final years at St Catherine's.

He opened it to the relevant month.

"I kept notes because the matter was unusual," he explained. "The hospital had received several external requests, and I was concerned that the identity references were being treated as interchangeable."

Edward examined the page.

There were three short entries.

**Day 8 — Vale requests transfer of original STC-A card. Refused pending clinical authority.**

**Day 9 — Further request. External legal office claims originating family authorisation. No supporting document received.**

**Day 10 — Card released under sealed custody instruction. Receipt to be returned.**

Edward looked up.

"Who gave you the sealed custody instruction?"

"Charles."

"Was it signed?"

"Yes."

"By whom?"

Lang turned the page.

He had kept a copy of the instruction.

Edward read it.

It was signed by Charles Vale.

But the authorisation section referred to an additional approval.

**External originating-office representative: A. Holt.**

Jonathan looked at Edward.

"Adrian Holt."

Edward read the line again.

"Was Holt present when the card was released?"

"No."

"Did you speak to him?"

"Not personally."

"Then why was his name used?"

Lang looked uncomfortable.

"Charles said the Aurelius Family Office had accepted responsibility for preservation."

"Did you verify that?"

"No."

"Why not?"

"At the time, I believed the matter had been authorised at a higher level."

Edward closed the diary.

"Dr. Lang, you were the responsible clinician. You knew the originating identity material was being removed. You knew the comparison had not been completed. And you relied on a legal representative's statement without confirming the receiving authority."

Lang nodded.

"I did."

"Do you regret it?"

"Every day."

The answer came without hesitation.

Edward studied him.

It was possible that Lang had been careless.

It was possible he had been pressured.

It was also possible that he had been part of the arrangement.

But there was no reason to decide which explanation was true yet.

"Do you have the original receipt?"

Lang nodded.

He opened another section of the diary.

A small paper slip had been attached to the inside cover.

It carried a handwritten reference:

**STC-A — Original card.**

**Received under sealed custody.**

The signature was not Adrian Holt's.

It was unfamiliar.

Beneath it was a date.

The tenth day.

And a name.

**Marianne Cross.**

Edward looked at Jonathan.

They had finally found the person who physically collected the missing card.

Marianne Cross did not answer her telephone.

Edward tried twice.

On the third attempt, a woman answered.

"Ms. Cross is unavailable."

"Who is speaking?"

"Her assistant."

"This is Edward Valmont. I need to speak with Marianne regarding the St Catherine's neonatal identification card."

There was a pause.

"I'll pass on the message."

"Please tell her we have the original custody register and the receipt bearing her signature."

Another pause.

"Understood."

Edward ended the call.

Jonathan looked at him.

"Do you think she'll come?"

"I think she knows we have found the receipt."

"That wasn't my question."

Edward placed the telephone down.

"No. I don't know whether she'll come."

They waited.

Forty minutes later, a message arrived.

**Ms. Cross is willing to meet tomorrow. She requests that the meeting take place in the presence of her legal representative.**

Jonathan read it.

"At least she's not refusing."

Edward nodded.

"Prepare the documents."

That evening, the Aurelius family gathered in the estate's private dining room.

Alexander had asked Helena to brief them on the latest findings.

Adrian listened without interrupting.

Lucien, unusually quiet, sat with his hands folded on the table.

Damien leaned against the window frame.

Julian had brought his notebook, although nobody had asked him to.

"Marianne Cross collected the original card?" Adrian asked.

"Yes," Helena replied. "The receipt confirms it."

"Was the card delivered to the Family Office?"

"We have no record of its receipt."

Adrian looked at Alexander.

"Then the evidence chain is incomplete."

"Correct."

"Who authorised the collection?"

"Charles Vale, with a reference to your predecessor, Adrian Holt."

Adrian frowned.

"Holt never told me."

"He may not have known the card had been collected by Marianne."

Victoria looked at the documents.

"Then what happened to it?"

Nobody answered.

Julian glanced at the notebook in front of him.

He had crossed out several items on his list over the previous weeks.

The list was no longer simply a collection of things he wanted to do with his missing sister.

He had begun to understand that finding her would require more patience than he had ever imagined.

He looked at his father.

"Can we find out where the card went?"

Alexander turned towards him.

"We're trying."

"Could Marianne have kept it?"

"Possibly."

"Could she have given it to someone else?"

"Also possible."

Julian nodded slowly.

Then he looked down at his notebook.

At the top of the page, item forty-eight remained unchanged.

**Find out what she wants before making plans.**

He had not crossed it out.

He had decided it was the most important item.

The following afternoon, Edward met Marianne Cross in a private conference room.

She arrived with a solicitor.

She looked older than she had in the photograph Isabella had described, but her features were recognisable.

The silver ring was still on her right hand.

The dark stone caught the overhead light.

Edward noticed a faint scar near her left thumb.

It was the same detail Isabella had remembered.

Marianne saw him looking.

"Is there something on my hand?"

"Isabella Vale described that ring."

Marianne's expression changed slightly.

"I assumed she would remember it."

Edward placed the receipt on the table.

"You collected the original St Catherine's identification card on the tenth day after the births."

Marianne looked at the document.

"Yes."

"Where did you take it?"

"To Northbridge Clinical Review Unit."

"Do you have proof of delivery?"

"I believe so."

"Where?"

"In my personal archive."

"Can you produce it?"

"Not today."

Edward looked at her solicitor.

"Will you arrange for the document to be provided?"

The solicitor nodded.

"Subject to review."

Edward returned his attention to Marianne.

"Why did you collect the card?"

"Because I was instructed to."

"By Charles Vale?"

"Yes."

"Did you know what it contained?"

"I knew it was an identification card from St Catherine's."

"Did you know it was required for the comparison?"

"Yes."

"Then why did Northbridge report that it had not received it?"

Marianne folded her hands.

"Because the card was not accepted."

Edward paused.

"Not accepted?"

"Dr. Lang released it under a sealed custody instruction. Northbridge refused to accept it without an originating medical authority's confirmation."

"Who refused it?"

"Dr. Samuel Hale."

Edward remembered the Westbridge records.

Hale had been the receiving physician for 28-B and M-204.

"Did you deliver it to him?"

"No. I delivered it to the Northbridge office."

"Who was present?"

"Gabriel Vale."

Edward's expression remained neutral.

"What happened?"

Marianne looked towards her solicitor.

He gave a slight nod.

She continued.

"Gabriel checked the envelope. He said the reference was correct. Then Dr. Hale reviewed the accompanying instruction and refused to accept the card."

"Why?"

"Because the instruction did not establish that the card was authorised for comparison."

"Was the envelope opened?"

"No."

"Did Gabriel take it?"

Marianne hesitated.

"He said he would arrange the next step."

"Where did he take it?"

"I don't know."

Edward studied her.

"You're telling me you handed the original card to Gabriel Vale after Northbridge refused the delivery."

"I'm telling you that the card was not accepted by Dr. Hale. Gabriel took responsibility for arranging its return."

"Did you see him leave with it?"

"Yes."

"Did you ever see the card again?"

"No."

Edward wrote down the answer.

The missing card had not simply disappeared into an archive.

It had been delivered to Northbridge, refused, and then taken away by Gabriel Vale.

The same Gabriel who had signed the sixth-day transfer instruction.

The same Gabriel who had approved Cross Administrative Services payments.

The same Gabriel who had insisted that no child be identified as belonging to a family without confirmation.

Edward looked at Marianne.

"Did you know the comparison was suspended?"

"Yes."

"Did you know Isabella had consented to it?"

"Yes."

"Did you tell her that the original material had been rejected?"

"No."

"Why?"

"Charles instructed me not to."

"Did you agree with that instruction?"

Marianne looked down.

"I didn't think it was my place to decide."

Edward's expression hardened.

"You were involved in delivering the documents, arranging the transfers and communicating with receiving institutions. You were not a passive messenger."

Marianne did not respond.

He placed another document on the table.

It was the day-seventeenth letter addressed to Victoria Aurelius.

"Did you write this?"

Marianne read it.

Her fingers tightened around the edge.

"Yes."

"Did you send it?"

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because I believed the Aurelius family should know that Isabella Vale's maternity identification material existed."

"Yet you didn't send the original band."

"I didn't have it."

"Why did you send an empty pouch?"

"It was a preservation reference."

"That is not an explanation."

Marianne looked at him.

"The band was being held separately. I wanted Victoria to know that it existed, but I did not want anyone treating it as proof that the infant associated with it was the missing Aurelius child."

Edward watched her.

"Why were you concerned about that?"

"Because it wasn't established."

"Did you believe the child was Isabella's?"

"I believed the record belonged to Isabella."

"That wasn't my question."

Marianne remained silent.

Edward leaned back.

"Did you know where the child was?"

"No."

"Did you ever see the infant?"

Marianne looked at him.

For the first time, her composure faltered.

"Yes."

Edward's pen stopped.

"When?"

"The sixth day."

"Where?"

"Meridian."

"Was it the child associated with IV-204?"

"I cannot say."

"Why not?"

"Because I saw an infant. I did not see an identification record that established the child's identity."

Edward let the silence continue.

Then he asked the question he had been waiting to ask.

"Was that the same infant you saw at the private medical residence?"

Marianne looked away.

"Possibly."

"Possibly?"

"I cannot be certain."

Edward closed his notebook.

He had not obtained a confession.

He had obtained something more useful.

A new, verifiable route for the missing identification card.

Gabriel Vale had taken possession of it after Northbridge refused the delivery.

And the card had never been recorded as returned.

That night, Edward sent Jonathan a short message.

**We need Gabriel's private correspondence from the tenth through the twenty-eighth day. Find every record involving the St Catherine's card.**

Then he opened the photograph of the original custody receipt.

The card had left St Catherine's on the tenth day.

Marianne had delivered it to Northbridge.

Northbridge had refused it.

Gabriel had taken it away.

The original comparison material was now somewhere between the hospital and the private administrative network controlled by Charles Vale.

Edward looked at the second document on his desk.

The seventeenth-day letter to Victoria.

It contained a sentence he had initially regarded as a sensible warning.

**Do not permit its use as proof of family relationship.**

Now he wondered whether the warning had served another purpose.

If the evidence could never be accepted as proof, nobody would be able to use it to challenge the identities that had already been assigned.

And if the comparison remained unfinished, nobody would have to explain why.

Edward reached for his telephone.

He called Jonathan.

"One more thing."

"What?"

"Find out whether Gabriel Vale still has his private medical correspondence archive."

"Why?"

Edward looked at the receipt bearing Gabriel's name.

"Because I don't think the original card was lost."

He paused.

"I think someone kept it."
