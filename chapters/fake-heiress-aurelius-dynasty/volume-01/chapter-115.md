# **Chapter 115 — The Proposal Made Before the Review**

The date was the first thing Edward noticed.

Day 34.

He wrote it at the top of a fresh sheet and placed three documents beneath it.

The first was the Northbridge routing record.

The second was the alternate recipient schedule reference.

The third was the original OR-28 independent review request.

All three belonged to the same period.

And all three raised the same uncomfortable possibility.

A proposed receiving arrangement had been prepared before anyone had established whether the child could be correctly identified.

Jonathan entered the study carrying a file.

“The Northbridge archive has responded.”

Edward looked up.

“What did they find?”

“An administrative preparation register associated with NCRU/AR-34/02. The schedule was prepared on day 34, but the request itself was received two days earlier.”

“From whom?”

“Vale Private Counsel.”

“Charles?”

“The sender's office was recorded. The individual requesting it was not.”

Edward examined the document.

The request was brief.

**REQUEST: PREPARATION OF ALTERNATE RECEIVING ARRANGEMENT**

**PURPOSE: CONTINUITY OF CARE FOLLOWING POSSIBLE REFUSAL OF PROPOSED RECEIVING REPRESENTATIVE**

**CHILD REFERENCE: 28-B**

**IDENTITY STATUS: UNRESOLVED**

**ORIGINATING VERIFICATION: INCOMPLETE**

**REQUESTED OUTPUT: SEALED ALTERNATE RECIPIENT SCHEDULE**

Edward read the final line twice.

“Possible refusal.”

Jonathan nodded.

“It was prepared before Susan Bell formally refused the allocation.”

“So they anticipated her refusal.”

“Or they anticipated that the first proposed recipient might refuse.”

Edward turned the page.

There was a small note beneath the request.

**ALTERNATE RECIPIENT MUST NOT BE CONTACTED UNTIL RECEIVING AUTHORITY IS APPOINTED.**

The note carried the initials of a Northbridge administrative officer.

Edward leaned back.

“Then the schedule was prepared as a contingency, not an appointment.”

“That seems to be the intended distinction.”

“Did Northbridge have authority to propose a recipient?”

“No. The request specifically described the document as a preparation exercise. Northbridge could compile information supplied by the requesting office, but it couldn't independently designate a receiving family.”

Edward tapped the paper.

“Then who supplied the proposed name?”

“The register doesn't say.”

“Was the name entered by Northbridge?”

“Apparently not. The schedule was received already sealed.”

Edward looked at him.

“So Northbridge prepared the envelope, not the contents.”

“That's what the archive believes.”

Edward returned to the first page.

The distinction was important.

Someone had created an alternate recipient proposal elsewhere, sent it to Northbridge, and requested that it be placed in a sealed schedule.

Northbridge had processed it without verifying the identity of the child or the proposed recipient.

It had also recorded that the schedule could not be used until the receiving authority was appointed.

The process had been designed to prevent premature action.

Yet the schedule had later been sent to Eleanor, and someone had attempted to use it.

“Find the person who prepared the original proposal,” Edward said.

Jonathan nodded.

“And the source document used to create it.”

Edward looked again at the child reference.

“Especially that.”

Northbridge's former administrative director, Peter Langford, had not expected to be contacted again.

When Edward and Jonathan arrived, he was reading a newspaper in a quiet office.

“I thought we'd already discussed the recipient schedules,” he said.

“We discussed the records you were responsible for preserving,” Edward replied. “We have now found an earlier proposal.”

Langford placed the newspaper aside.

“Which proposal?”

Edward showed him the request.

Langford adjusted his glasses.

“Day 32.”

“Does it mean anything to you?”

He examined the reference.

“NCRU/AR-34/02. Yes. This was an administrative preparation request.”

“Who authorised it?”

“Northbridge did not authorise the proposed recipient. We only accepted the document for preservation.”

“Who prepared the contents?”

“I don't remember.”

“Would there be a register?”

“There should be.”

Langford asked his assistant to retrieve the old correspondence ledger.

While they waited, Edward asked another question.

“Did you know that Child 28-B had unresolved identity status when the schedule was prepared?”

Langford looked at him.

“Of course. That was the reason the schedule was sealed.”

“Then why prepare an alternate arrangement at all?”

“Because temporary care arrangements sometimes fail.”

“Does that justify preparing a permanent family allocation?”

“No.”

“Then what did you understand the schedule to contain?”

Langford considered his answer.

“A proposed alternative. Nothing more.”

“Was it meant to replace Susan Bell?”

“Not automatically.”

“Who would decide whether the alternate recipient was suitable?”

“The receiving identity custodian, after the originating identity had been verified.”

Edward placed the day-34 instruction on the desk.

“Yet no receiving identity custodian was ever appointed.”

Langford read the document.

“That is correct.”

“And no originating identity was established.”

“Correct.”

“So the schedule could never have been used.”

Langford's expression tightened.

“Not under the procedure I understood.”

His assistant returned with the ledger.

Langford opened it.

The entry appeared on the second page.

**DAY 32 — SEALED PROPOSAL RECEIVED FROM VPC PERSONAL INSTRUCTIONS OFFICE.**

**PREPARATION REQUEST: ALTERNATE RECEIVING REPRESENTATIVE.**

**CHILD REFERENCE: 28-B.**

**ATTACHED MATERIAL: RECIPIENT PROFILE, CONDITIONAL CONSENT, CONTACT ROUTE.**

**IDENTITY VERIFICATION: NOT REQUESTED.**

Edward looked at the third attachment description.

“Conditional consent?”

Langford nodded.

“That's interesting.”

“What does it mean?”

“Someone had apparently obtained a preliminary willingness from the proposed alternate recipient.”

“Willingness to do what?”

“To be considered as a possible receiving representative if the initial arrangement failed.”

“Was that person told the child's identity?”

“No. The record says the person was given a general description of the proposed responsibility, not the child's originating identity.”

“Was the person told that the child was Child 28-B?”

Langford turned the page.

“No.”

Edward looked at Jonathan.

That changed the nature of the document.

The alternate recipient had not merely been named.

Someone had already approached that person and obtained conditional consent.

Before the independent review.

Before Susan Bell's refusal.

Before a receiving authority had been appointed.

“Who obtained the consent?” Edward asked.

Langford checked the entry.

“The request says it was returned through Vale Private Counsel.”

“Does it identify the intermediary?”

“No.”

“Was the consent signed?”

“The ledger records a signed document.”

“Can we see it?”

“Not without access to the sealed schedule.”

Edward nodded.

“Then we will seek the proper authority.”

He stood.

But before leaving, he asked one more question.

“Mr. Langford, did you ever object to the preparation of this schedule?”

Langford looked at the ledger.

“I objected to its description.”

“Why?”

“Because the request called it an alternate receiving arrangement. I wanted it recorded as an alternate recipient proposal.”

“Was the wording changed?”

“Yes.”

“By whom?”

“Charles Vale's office requested the change.”

Edward's expression remained neutral.

“And you agreed?”

“I refused the first time.”

“What happened?”

“They sent a corrected request.”

Langford turned the ledger around.

The corrected wording was visible.

**SEALED ALTERNATE RECIPIENT PROPOSAL — NO APPOINTMENT OR NOTIFICATION AUTHORISED.**

Langford pointed to the final line.

“That was the wording I accepted.”

Edward read it carefully.

The distinction had been deliberate.

Someone wanted an alternate recipient identified.

But Langford had insisted that the proposal could not be treated as an appointment.

It was another example of the same conflict.

One office preparing arrangements.

Another office trying to preserve the difference between a proposal and a decision.

That afternoon, Edward received permission to inspect a separate Northbridge correspondence file.

It contained the corrected request, the preparation register, and a short memorandum from Charles Vale's office.

The memorandum was dated day 31.

It contained only a few paragraphs.

**The temporary receiving arrangement for Child 28-B may not remain available indefinitely. An alternative should therefore be identified and kept ready.**

**The alternative must not be contacted or informed of the child's originating identity before the required medical and receiving verification procedures are complete.**

**The existence of an alternative shall not be treated as confirmation of the original allocation.**

At the bottom was Charles's signature.

Edward read it again.

It was genuine, according to the Northbridge signature verification note.

“Does this make sense to you?” Jonathan asked.

Edward considered the wording.

“More than some of the later instructions.”

“Because it explicitly says the alternative must not be contacted.”

“Yes.”

“And that the alternative doesn't confirm the original allocation.”

“Yes.”

Edward placed the memorandum beside the day-120 dispatch instruction.

“Then we have a contradiction.”

Jonathan looked at the papers.

“Charles ordered an alternate proposal prepared but prohibited notification. Later, someone tried to notify the alternate.”

“Correct.”

“Could Charles have changed his mind?”

“He could have. But if he did, we need the authorisation.”

Jonathan pointed to the day-120 request.

“There's no signature.”

“And no receiving authority.”

Edward gathered the documents.

“We need to know who prepared the conditional consent.”

The answer came from an unexpected source.

Patricia Moore.

When Edward contacted her, she initially denied having any involvement with the alternate schedule.

“I worked on the OR-28 status records,” she said. “I didn't prepare recipient proposals.”

Edward placed a copy of the day-32 register before her.

“Do you recognise the reference?”

Patricia read it.

Her expression changed slightly.

“I remember the file.”

“Why?”

“Because it came through the personal instructions division.”

“Did you handle it?”

“Not directly.”

“Did you prepare the conditional consent?”

“No.”

“Did you see it?”

Patricia hesitated.

“I saw a cover sheet.”

“What did it contain?”

“I don't remember the exact wording.”

“Try.”

She looked at the reference again.

“There was a note about a person who had agreed to be considered if the first arrangement could not proceed.”

“Was the person named?”

“I don't remember.”

“Was the consent genuine?”

“I didn't examine the signature.”

“Who did?”

Patricia shook her head.

“I don't know.”

Edward waited.

She finally added, “There was a separate review desk for proposed recipients. It wasn't part of the ordinary allocation team.”

“Where?”

“Vale Private Counsel. Personal instructions division.”

“Who worked there?”

“I don't know the full staff list.”

“Who supervised it?”

Patricia thought for a moment.

“Charles's office handled the requests. But there was an external reviewer who sometimes checked the proposed recipient documents.”

“Name?”

“I never met him.”

“Was it a man?”

“I think so.”

“Do you remember anything else?”

Patricia frowned.

“There was a reference used on the correspondence.”

“Which one?”

She closed her eyes briefly.

“Something like R-12.”

Edward wrote it down.

**R-12.**

“Was that an office?”

“I don't know. It may have been a review code.”

“Did it appear on the conditional consent?”

“I can't be certain.”

“Would you recognise the document?”

“Possibly.”

Edward closed his notebook.

“Then we need to find it.”

At the Aurelius Estate, Victoria was reviewing a report from the investigation when Alexander entered her sitting room.

She had been reading the same page for several minutes.

“What is it?” he asked.

“Child 28-B had an alternate recipient proposal prepared before the first receiving representative refused.”

Alexander sat opposite her.

“Was the proposal connected to us?”

“We don't know.”

“Did the investigation establish who the alternate was?”

“No.”

Victoria placed the report on the table.

“But someone had already obtained conditional consent.”

Alexander looked at the document.

“From the proposed recipient?”

“Yes. Apparently without disclosing the child's originating identity.”

He read the summary.

“Was the person told that the child was connected to our family?”

“No evidence of that.”

“Then we cannot assume anything.”

“I know.”

She looked toward the garden.

“Still, I cannot stop thinking about how many people were asked to make decisions about children whose identities they were never allowed to know.”

Alexander said nothing.

“Some were trying to protect them,” she continued. “Some were trying to protect themselves. Some may have believed they were doing what was necessary.”

“And some may have known exactly what they were doing.”

Victoria looked at him.

“Yes.”

Alexander folded the report.

“We need the originating records. Not another administrative summary.”

Victoria nodded.

“I have asked Edward to pursue the original consent document.”

“Good.”

She looked down at her hands.

“If a family was approached, even conditionally, I want to know whether they were told the truth.”

Alexander's expression softened.

“So do I.”

But neither of them said what they were both thinking.

An alternate recipient could have been anyone.

A person who had been asked to help.

A family that had been promised a child.

Or someone who had been given only enough information to sign a document without understanding its consequences.

Nothing in the current evidence connected that person to Elena.

Nothing established that the proposal had ever been carried out.

For now, the Aureliuses remained where they had been for months.

Waiting for proof.

Elena's final preparations were nearly complete.

Her travel folder was now organised into separate sections.

Passport and identification.

Programme admission.

Accommodation.

Emergency contacts.

Travel insurance.

Academic documents.

She had also printed a small copy of her project proposal, although she planned to keep the main version on her laptop.

Nicholas stood beside the dining table, checking the departure schedule.

“You have the car arranged?”

“Yes.”

“Airport arrival time?”

“Three hours before departure.”

“Documents?”

“In my bag.”

“Passport?”

Elena looked at him.

“Would you like to inspect it?”

“Yes.”

She handed it over.

Nicholas checked the cover and returned it.

“Good.”

“Anything else?”

“Your phone charger.”

“Packed.”

“Laptop charger.”

“Packed.”

“Adapter.”

“Packed.”

“Emergency cash.”

“Packed.”

Nicholas nodded.

“Excellent.”

Elena narrowed her eyes.

“Are you going to ask me whether I've packed underwear?”

He looked genuinely offended.

“I was going to leave that to your judgment.”

“Generous.”

He laughed.

A few minutes later, Julian appeared carrying a small paper bag.

“I have something.”

Elena looked inside.

There was a compact notebook with a dark blue cover.

“What's this?”

“For your trip.”

She opened it.

The first page contained a handwritten message.

**THINGS TO REMEMBER**

- You don't have to do everything perfectly.
- You are allowed to ask for help.
- Don't skip meals because you're busy.
- Call when you have time.
- Buy something nice once in a while.
- Come home whenever you want.

Elena looked up.

“Did you write this?”

Julian nodded.

“It's not a list of instructions.”

“It looks suspiciously like one.”

“It's a list of suggestions.”

“Very different.”

“Exactly.”

She closed the notebook carefully.

“Thank you.”

Julian shrugged.

“It's small. You can carry it.”

Elena gave him a hug.

He froze for half a second before hugging her back.

Nicholas watched them with a smile.

Then he glanced at the dining table.

“Should we tell Sebastian the car is ready?”

Julian pulled away.

“Yes.”

Elena shook her head.

“You two are treating this like a military operation.”

“Airport security is serious,” Julian said.

“Then I suppose I should salute.”

She took the notebook and placed it in her bag.

For a brief moment, the house felt ordinary.

There were no investigations, no missing documents, no disputed identities.

Only a family preparing to send one of its members away for a new beginning.

And Elena was looking forward to it.

Edward was still at his desk when Jonathan returned with the next discovery.

“R-12 has been identified.”

Edward looked up.

“What is it?”

“An external recipient review office. It was operated by a private administrative consultancy that worked with Vale Private Counsel.”

“Is the consultancy still active?”

“No. It closed years ago.”

“Records?”

“Some were transferred to a successor company. Others were deposited with a professional records custodian.”

“Who was the reviewer?”

Jonathan placed a single-page index on the desk.

“The name is listed here.”

Edward read it.

**R-12 — INDEPENDENT RECIPIENT REVIEW**

**REVIEW OFFICER: DR. EVELYN SHAW**

He looked up.

“Evelyn Shaw?”

“Yes.”

“But she was the independent reviewer for OR-28.”

“Correct.”

Edward studied the index.

“She told us she had refused the OR-28 appointment.”

“She did.”

“And now we find her name on the alternate recipient review.”

Jonathan nodded.

“There is more.”

He placed another document beside it.

The index listed three separate tasks.

**TASK 1 — REVIEW OF PROPOSED ALTERNATE RECIPIENT PROFILE.**

**TASK 2 — VERIFICATION OF CONDITIONAL CONSENT.**

**TASK 3 — CONFIRMATION THAT RECIPIENT INFORMATION MAY BE RELEASED AFTER APPOINTMENT OF RECEIVING AUTHORITY.**

Edward read the dates.

Task 1: day 33.

Task 2: day 34.

Task 3: day 35.

The work had begun before the OR-28 independent review was formally suspended.

He looked at Jonathan.

“Did Dr Shaw complete the tasks?”

“The index says the first two were reviewed. The third remained pending.”

“Where are the reports?”

“Not in the Northbridge archive. The index says the original reports were returned to Vale Private Counsel.”

Edward stared at the dates.

Dr Shaw had refused to certify the broader identity review because the records were incomplete.

Yet she had been asked to examine the proposed alternate recipient and the person's conditional consent.

That did not necessarily mean she had approved the proposal.

But it meant she knew more about the alternate schedule than she had previously disclosed.

Edward reached for his telephone.

“Arrange another meeting with Dr Shaw.”

Jonathan nodded.

“What do you want to ask her?”

Edward looked at the three tasks.

“Who agreed to receive a child whose identity had not been established?”

He paused.

“And whether that person was ever told that the arrangement might never become permanent.”

For the first time, the investigation had found a direct connection between the alternate recipient proposal and the independent reviewer.

But the most important document was still missing.

The conditional consent.

And somewhere in the sealed schedule, a name remained waiting to be read.
