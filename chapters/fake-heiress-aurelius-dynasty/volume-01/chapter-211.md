# **Chapter 211 — The Correction Made on the Sixth Day**

The amendment history arrived at 7:58.

Edward opened it before Sophie had finished placing her coffee on the table.

The document was short.

**Record:** SC-MAT/02-17\
**Original entry:** 2:17 a.m.\
**Amendment date:** Day 6\
**Amendment category:** Receiving identification reconciliation\
**Authorising department:** Medical Records Administration\
**Original clinical entry:** Preserved\
**Amended field:** Receiving identification reference\
**Clinical details changed:** None recorded

Edward read the last line again.

“No clinical details changed,” Sophie said.

“Which means the hospital is not claiming that the birth information was corrected.”

“Only the receiving identification.”

“Yes.”

He opened the original and amended entries side by side.

The original field contained a temporary reference.

The amended field contained a different reference.

Both were visible.

Neither was accompanied by a document establishing that the new reference belonged to the same infant.

Edward wrote the distinction into the investigation notes.

**A change in receiving identification is not proof of a change in biological identity.**

At 8:30, Dr Miriam Cole joined the review.

She had obtained permission to explain the amendment process but not to disclose the restricted recipient identity.

“Receiving identification reconciliation was an administrative procedure,” she said. “It was used when the receiving unit's reference differed from the initial maternity admission reference.”

“Was that normal?”

“It could be. An infant might be transferred between departments, assigned a temporary neonatal reference, or entered into a receiving institution's separate system.”

“Would the change normally require supporting documentation?”

“Yes.”

“What kind?”

“An authorised transfer record, an identification confirmation or another approved source document.”

Edward displayed the amendment.

“Does this entry identify which supporting document was used?”

Dr Cole examined it.

“No. It contains a reference to a supporting schedule.”

“Can we see that schedule?”

“The schedule is held separately.”

“Under which reference?”

“SC-MAT/02-17/S.”

Edward entered the reference.

The catalogue returned a result.

**Supporting schedule preserved. Access restricted.**

Edward requested the schedule's administrative description.

At 9:12, the Aurelius family's legal office sent a formal notice.

It was not an attempt to obtain the identity.

It was a request to preserve all records associated with the 2:17 a.m. maternity entry, including original versions, amendment histories, supporting schedules and transfer records.

Alexander had signed it.

Victoria had countersigned.

The notice expressly stated that the family was not asking the hospital to confirm any biological relationship.

Edward read the final paragraph.

**The request is made solely to prevent the destruction, alteration or loss of potentially relevant historical records. It does not assert that the patient recorded in SC-MAT/02-17 is the person sought by the Aurelius family.**

He approved the wording.

For once, the family's involvement was exactly what the investigation needed.

Not a claim.

A preservation request.

At 10:05, the Valmont family received the same preservation notice.

Catherine read it in silence.

Richard sat opposite her.

“Why are we being included?”

“Because St. Catherine's has records associated with several historical maternity entries,” Edward explained over the call. “The investigation has not established which entries, if any, relate to the Valmont family.”

Richard looked displeased.

“Are you suggesting that our hospital records were involved?”

“No. I am saying that the records need to be preserved before anyone draws a conclusion.”

Catherine leaned forward.

“Does this concern the maternity record from my admission?”

“That is one of the records under examination. But we do not yet know whether the amendment refers to your infant's receiving identification.”

Catherine's expression tightened.

“Then I want the original preserved.”

Richard nodded.

“Send the notice to our legal office.”

Edward did.

At 11:20, the supporting schedule's administrative description arrived.

It was a single-page index.

**SC-MAT/02-17/S — Receiving Identification Reconciliation Schedule**

The schedule had been prepared on day 6.

It contained three fields:

- Original maternity reference.
- Temporary neonatal reference.
- Proposed receiving reference.

The first two were completed.

The third had been entered in handwriting.

The handwriting had been independently preserved as part of the original document.

Edward enlarged the image.

The proposed receiving reference was:

**STC-A.**

Sophie looked at him.

“That's the Infant A reference.”

“Yes.”

“But the schedule doesn't say the two references belong to the same infant?”

“Not in the visible extract.”

He checked the signature field.

It contained the initials of a medical records officer.

There was no signature from the receiving unit.

No receiving officer's acknowledgement.

No independent identification confirmation.

Edward looked at the amendment again.

The hospital had changed the receiving identification field to STC-A.

But the supporting schedule did not show that the receiving institution had confirmed the match.

He requested the full schedule and its authorisation history.

At 12:10, Elena received the pilot's first formal review report.

The reviewers had tested the platform with incomplete applications, duplicate submissions and conflicting evidence.

The system had handled most of them correctly.

One weakness remained.

An administrator could attach a document to an application and mark it as supporting evidence without recording whether the document had been independently verified.

Elena read the finding.

She had assumed that evidence status would be obvious from the document's source.

It was not.

She opened her design notes.

A document could be:

- Received.
- Authenticated as an original or valid copy.
- Verified against an independent source.
- Accepted as relevant to a particular claim.

These were four separate questions.

She added a new field.

**Verification basis.**

The system would require a reviewer to specify what had actually been checked.

A document could be genuine without proving the claim for which it had been submitted.

She sent the revised design to the coordinator.

Then she wrote a note to herself:

*Never let a valid document become proof of something it was never designed to prove.*

At 13:45, Dr Cole returned with the full supporting schedule.

The document contained an additional page that had not appeared in the administrative extract.

It was an authorisation note.

**Purpose:** Reconcile receiving identification for continuity of neonatal records.

**Authority:** Medical Records Administration.

**Limitation:** This instruction does not constitute independent confirmation of infant identity or authorisation for permanent receiving placement.

Edward read it carefully.

The wording was clear.

The records department had permission to update an administrative field for continuity.

It did not have authority to establish the child's biological identity.

Sophie pointed to the date.

“Day 6.”

“Yes.”

“Same day as the Gabriel transfer.”

Edward nodded.

The timing was relevant.

But the documents still did not establish that the transfer and amendment concerned the same infant.

He asked Dr Cole whether the schedule had been used to update the neonatal register.

“Yes,” she replied.

“Was the receiving institution notified?”

“The transfer register records a notification.”

“Was the notification acknowledged?”

“I would need to check.”

Edward requested the transfer register.

At 14:30, the transfer register arrived.

The entry showed that the receiving reference STC-A had been entered into the hospital's administrative system.

A notification had been sent to the neonatal records desk.

The status field read:

**Received for administrative reconciliation.**

Not:

**Identity confirmed.**

Not:

**Infant accepted.**

Not:

**Transfer completed.**

Edward highlighted the wording.

“Someone later treated this as a completed transfer.”

“Who?” Sophie asked.

He searched the investigation records.

The phrase appeared in a day-28 administrative summary.

That summary described STC-A as an established receiving identity.

But the original transfer register did not say that.

The summary had expanded the meaning of the original entry.

Edward added it to the evidence comparison.

At 15:25, the hospital's preservation director called.

“We found the receiving desk's original response.”

Edward straightened.

“What did it say?”

“It was entered on day 6, later that afternoon.”

“Was the receiving reference accepted?”

Dr Cole hesitated.

“Not exactly.”

She shared the original response.

**The receiving desk acknowledges receipt of the proposed reference for administrative review.**

Below it was another line.

**No infant identity confirmation has been completed. Original identification material is required before the receiving reference can be treated as verified.**

Edward read it twice.

The receiving desk had explicitly refused to confirm the identity.

Yet the maternity register had already been amended to show STC-A.

“Was the response entered into the amendment file?”

“No.”

“Was it attached to the supporting schedule?”

“No.”

“Was it included in the later administrative summary?”

Dr Cole checked.

“No.”

Edward looked at Sophie.

The original records now showed a sequence.

First, an administrative amendment was authorised.

Then a receiving reference was entered.

The receiving desk acknowledged the proposed reference but stated that identity confirmation remained incomplete.

That response was not incorporated into the amendment file.

The records department had preserved the original response.

But the later summaries had omitted it.

At 16:10, Edward contacted the former medical records officer.

Her name was Helen Price.

She had retired seven years earlier.

She agreed to speak, provided the conversation remained limited to her administrative role.

Edward explained the documents.

Helen listened carefully.

“I remember the reconciliation procedure,” she said.

“Do you remember this particular entry?”

“I remember the reference.”

“Did you personally establish that the receiving reference belonged to the infant recorded at 2:17?”

“No.”

“Who supplied STC-A?”

“I received it through the neonatal administration channel.”

“Was there an identity confirmation attached?”

“Not to the paperwork I was given.”

“Why did you enter it?”

“Because the instruction authorised an administrative reconciliation. I understood that the receiving desk would complete its own verification.”

“Did you see the receiving desk's response?”

Helen paused.

“I don't remember seeing it.”

“Would it have changed your entry?”

“It would have changed the status. I would not have described the reference as verified.”

Edward thanked her.

He asked one more question.

“Who instructed you to use the receiving reference?”

“The request came through the medical records administration office. I didn't deal directly with the family or the receiving institution.”

“Do you remember who submitted the request?”

“No. I would need to see the original task record.”

Edward made a note.

The officer had performed an authorised administrative action.

She had not verified the identity.

The missing task record might establish who supplied the proposed reference.

At 17:05, Sophie found the original task record.

It had been stored under the day-6 amendment file.

The task had been created at 10:18.

**Requested action:** Update receiving identification field to maintain neonatal record continuity.

**Supporting material:** Provisional receiving schedule.

**Identity verification:** Pending receiving institution confirmation.

**Task owner:** Medical Records Administration.

The requester was recorded as a restricted institutional account.

Edward checked the account history.

It had been accessed by a hospital administrator.

The administrator's name was visible.

**Martin Keane.**

Edward recognised it from the migration override records.

Martin had previously used an administrative override to transfer record references between systems.

But the task record did not prove he had created the schedule or chosen STC-A.

It established only that his account had submitted the amendment request.

Edward opened the audit history.

The original task had contained a different instruction.

**Do not replace the temporary neonatal reference until receiving confirmation is obtained.**

At 10:04, that instruction had been revised.

The new wording was:

**Enter proposed receiving reference for administrative continuity. Identity verification remains pending.**

The revision was attributed to a shared administrative terminal.

Edward stared at the timestamp.

10:04.

Fourteen minutes before the task was created.

The change had happened before Helen Price received the instruction.

At 18:00, Edward received a message from Dr Cole.

The hospital had located a second document associated with the receiving desk's response.

It was a handwritten note from the neonatal records supervisor.

The note read:

**Please retain the temporary reference until the original identification item is examined. A proposed receiving reference is not sufficient for permanent reconciliation.**

The note had been filed separately.

It had not been attached to the amendment record.

Edward read the sentence again.

The original identification item had been required.

He returned to the source inventory.

Group C contained two items.

One had an incomplete condition description.

The other had a separate reference.

The hospital had not yet established which item the receiving desk had been waiting to examine.

He requested the original identification-item inventory.

At 18:42, the response arrived.

The two items had been stored in separate containers.

**C-1:** Original maternity identification slip, preserved with the delivery register.

**C-2:** Original neonatal band material, received separately under restricted custody.

C-2 had been recorded as incomplete.

Its associated patient reference was not disclosed in the accessible inventory.

But the final line contained a warning.

**No evidence establishes that C-1 and C-2 originated from the same infant.**

Edward closed the file.

The investigation had not yet proved that the 2:17 a.m. maternity entry belonged to STC-A.

It had not proved that STC-A belonged to C-2.

And it had not proved that either reference belonged to Elena.

But it had established something important.

On day 6, an administrative receiving reference had been entered before the receiving institution confirmed the infant's identity.

The receiving institution had expressly requested the original identification material.

That request had not been incorporated into the amendment file.

And the person who revised the original task had used a shared administrative terminal.

Edward looked at the time.

The next day's examination would finally put the original identification materials on the table.

For the first time, he would be able to ask whether the physical evidence supported the references that had been copied through the system.

He saved the latest documents.

Then he noticed one final field in the original inventory.

**C-2 — Previous custody reference: MC-06/11.**

He searched the investigation index.

The result appeared.

**MC-06/11 — Meridian Clinical Receiving Log.**

Edward went still.

Meridian had appeared repeatedly in the historical records.

It was where several newborn references had been transferred, renamed or temporarily held.

The original band material had not simply come from St. Catherine's.

It had passed through another institution.

And until they established how it reached Meridian, the chain of custody remained incomplete.
