# **Chapter 116 — The Consent That Was Never an Acceptance**

Dr. Evelyn Shaw remembered the alternate recipient review.

She did not remember it fondly.

When Edward arrived at her office, she was already waiting with a folder on her desk.

“I assume this concerns R-12,” she said.

Edward took the offered seat.

“It does.”

Dr. Shaw glanced at Jonathan.

“I was wondering when that file would resurface.”

“You knew it still existed?”

“I knew the review documents had been returned to Vale Private Counsel. I did not know whether they had been preserved.”

Edward placed the Northbridge index before her.

“Your name appears against three tasks.”

“I can see that.”

“Did you review the proposed alternate recipient?”

“Yes.”

“Did you verify the conditional consent?”

“I examined the document. I did not verify the identity of the person who signed it.”

“Why not?”

“Because I was not given the necessary original identification material.”

Edward waited.

Dr. Shaw opened her folder.

“The request was presented as a limited administrative review. I was asked to examine whether the proposed recipient profile contained enough information to be considered for a future appointment, whether the conditional consent had been properly documented, and whether the information could be released once a receiving authority was appointed.”

“Were you asked to determine whether the recipient was suitable?”

“No.”

“Were you asked to determine whether the recipient was related to the child?”

“No.”

“Were you asked to determine whether the recipient was the child's biological family?”

“Absolutely not.”

Edward looked at Jonathan.

The distinction was becoming familiar.

Every review had a narrow purpose.

Every document had a carefully limited meaning.

And every time the records were later summarised, those limitations seemed to disappear.

“Did you approve the proposal?” Edward asked.

“No.”

“Did you reject it?”

“I did not have enough information to do either.”

“Then what did you record?”

Dr. Shaw removed a photocopy from her folder.

At the top was the reference R-12/AR-34.

Below it:

**PROPOSED RECIPIENT PROFILE RECEIVED FOR PRELIMINARY REVIEW.**

**IDENTITY OF PROPOSED RECIPIENT NOT INDEPENDENTLY VERIFIED.**

**CONDITIONAL CONSENT PRESENT; SCOPE AND INFORMED BASIS NOT ESTABLISHED.**

**NO APPOINTMENT RECOMMENDED.**

Edward read the final line.

“Why wasn't the appointment recommended?”

“Because the proposed recipient had not been given enough information to make an informed decision.”

“What information was missing?”

“First, the originating identity of the child. Second, the legal nature of the proposed arrangement. Third, the fact that the receiving authority had not been appointed.”

“Was the recipient told that the child might not belong to the family being proposed?”

Dr. Shaw looked at him.

“That was one of the questions I raised.”

“And?”

“I was not given an answer.”

Edward placed the day-31 memorandum beside the report.

“Charles Vale's instruction said the alternate recipient must not be informed of the child's originating identity before verification.”

“That is correct.”

“Did you consider that reasonable?”

“I considered it reasonable not to disclose confidential medical information prematurely. I did not consider it reasonable to obtain consent without explaining what the person was being asked to accept.”

Edward leaned forward.

“So the person agreed to be considered, but may not have understood the actual arrangement.”

“Precisely.”

“Did you recommend that the consent be rejected?”

“No. I recommended that it remain conditional and that no appointment be made until the missing information was supplied.”

“Was that recommendation followed?”

Dr. Shaw looked down.

“I don't know.”

Edward studied her.

“Your report was returned to Vale Private Counsel on day 35.”

“Yes.”

“Did anyone ask you to revise it?”

“Yes.”

“Who?”

“Charles Vale's office.”

“What did they want changed?”

“They wanted the phrase ‘no appointment recommended’ replaced with ‘appointment pending administrative confirmation.’”

Edward's expression hardened.

“And you agreed?”

“No.”

“Did they issue a revised version anyway?”

“I cannot say. I never authorised one.”

She removed another page.

“This is the copy I retained.”

Edward examined it.

The wording was unambiguous.

**NO APPOINTMENT RECOMMENDED.**

**CONDITIONAL CONSENT NOT SUFFICIENT FOR RECEIVING AUTHORITY.**

**FURTHER INFORMATION REQUIRED BEFORE ANY NOTIFICATION OR RELEASE OF RECIPIENT IDENTITY.**

At the bottom was Dr. Shaw's signature.

Edward looked up.

“Why did you retain this copy?”

“Because I had already seen one of my earlier reports altered.”

“The OR-28 report?”

“Yes. I wanted a record of exactly what I had concluded.”

Edward placed the page in his folder.

“Did you know the alternate recipient's name?”

“No.”

“Did you know the person personally?”

“No.”

“Did you know whether the person was a family member, a guardian, or an institution?”

“The profile described a private individual acting on behalf of a proposed receiving household. It did not establish the person's relationship to the child.”

“Did the person have a spouse or other family members involved?”

“I was not given that information.”

Edward paused.

“Was the proposed recipient an individual or a household?”

Dr. Shaw considered the question.

“The paperwork used both terms. That was another issue I raised. The consent was signed by one individual, but the proposed arrangement referred to a receiving household.”

“So one person had agreed to be considered on behalf of several people.”

“Possibly. But I could not determine whether that person had authority to represent anyone else.”

Edward closed the folder.

“Did you ever receive a response to your objections?”

“Only a short acknowledgement.”

“What did it say?”

“That the matter would remain under administrative review.”

“Signed?”

“No.”

Edward stood.

“Thank you, Dr. Shaw.”

She looked at him.

“Mr. Valmont.”

He paused.

“I want to make something clear. I did not know where that child eventually went. I did not approve the recipient. And I never certified that the proposed family had any biological connection to the child.”

“I understand.”

“I hope the people investigating this understand the difference.”

Edward nodded.

“We do.”

On the drive back, Jonathan reread Dr. Shaw's report.

“Her conclusion is almost identical to her OR-28 position.”

“She consistently refused to certify what the records could not establish.”

“But this time she was examining the recipient rather than the child.”

“Yes.”

Jonathan looked out the window.

“Then we have a person who agreed to be considered, but no proof they understood the arrangement.”

“Correct.”

“And no proof the person was ever appointed.”

“Correct.”

“Do you think the proposed recipient knew the child was Child 28-B?”

“Dr. Shaw says the profile did not disclose the originating identity.”

“That doesn't mean the person didn't know the administrative reference.”

“No. But we cannot assume they did.”

Edward looked down at the copy of the report.

There was one phrase he found particularly important.

**CONDITIONAL CONSENT NOT SUFFICIENT FOR RECEIVING AUTHORITY.**

Someone had signed a document.

Someone had agreed to something.

But the agreement did not amount to accepting a child.

It did not establish identity.

It did not appoint a receiving representative.

And it did not authorise anyone to transfer a child permanently.

The investigation had spent months tracing the consequences of people treating incomplete documents as final decisions.

Now they had found another example of how easily a conditional agreement could be misrepresented.

“Find the original request sent to Dr. Shaw,” Edward said.

Jonathan nodded.

“And the correspondence asking her to revise the report.”

“Especially that.”

At Vale Private Counsel, Rachel Morton located the original request two days later.

It had been preserved in a separate administrative correspondence file.

The request was dated day 33.

Its sender was recorded as:

**PERSONAL INSTRUCTIONS DIVISION — CHARLES VALE OFFICE.**

The body contained four questions.

1. Whether the proposed recipient's profile could be retained for possible future consideration.
2. Whether the conditional consent had been documented.
3. Whether the recipient could be contacted if the initial proposed arrangement failed.
4. Whether the recipient's identity could be released to the relevant receiving authority once appointed.

Edward read the document.

“Nothing here asks her to approve the appointment.”

“No,” Rachel said.

“Was there an attached recipient profile?”

“Yes. It is listed in the schedule.”

“Can we inspect it?”

“Not without the required access authority.”

“What about the consent?”

“Also sealed.”

“Can we inspect the correspondence asking Dr. Shaw to revise her report?”

Rachel checked the index.

“There are two letters.”

She brought them out.

The first was a request to clarify the scope of her findings.

The second was more direct.

**Please reconsider the use of the phrase ‘no appointment recommended.’ The present administrative arrangement requires the proposed alternative to remain available for continuity purposes. Your review is limited to the sufficiency of the recipient profile and conditional consent, not the final appointment.**

There was no signature.

The second letter included a handwritten note.

**A negative recommendation may prevent necessary continuity arrangements. Please distinguish between rejection and incomplete verification.**

Edward studied the wording.

It was not entirely unreasonable.

A proposal could remain available even if it was not ready for approval.

But changing the wording from “no appointment recommended” to “appointment pending administrative confirmation” would imply that the process had advanced further than it had.

“Did Dr. Shaw respond?” he asked.

Rachel found the reply.

She had written:

**I do not recommend appointment at this stage. This does not prevent the proposal from being retained for future consideration.**

Edward read it.

“She made the distinction herself.”

“Yes.”

“Was the response accepted?”

“The correspondence register records it as received.”

“Any further action?”

Rachel turned to the final entry.

**REPORT RETAINED. ALTERNATE PROPOSAL TO REMAIN SEALED. NO NOTIFICATION AUTHORISED.**

Edward nodded.

At least in this part of the record, the restriction had survived.

But there was another entry.

**SUBSEQUENT ADMINISTRATIVE SUMMARY ISSUED DAY 47.**

He looked at Jonathan.

“Day 47.”

The same day OR-28 independent review had been suspended.

Rachel retrieved the summary.

It was a single-page document.

**R-12 REVIEW STATUS: COMPLETED.**

**ALTERNATE RECIPIENT: RETAINED FOR CONTINUITY.**

**CONDITIONAL CONSENT: RECORDED.**

**FURTHER INDEPENDENT REVIEW: NOT REQUIRED FOR ADMINISTRATIVE PRESERVATION.**

Edward read the final line.

“Who issued this?”

Rachel checked the record.

“Shared administrative account FA-ADMIN / REVIEW DESK.”

Edward looked at Jonathan.

The same account had been involved in the altered OR-28 status record.

“Was Dr. Shaw's name used?”

“No.”

“Was the summary sent to her?”

“No.”

“Was the underlying report attached?”

“No.”

Edward took a photograph.

The summary did not say that the recipient had been appointed.

It did not say the consent had been verified.

But it declared the review completed.

Someone had taken a limited preliminary review and converted it into an administrative status that looked final.

“Who authorised this summary?”

Rachel turned the page.

There was a handwritten instruction in the margin.

**Retain for administrative continuity. Do not issue as identity determination.**

The initials were C.V.

Edward stared at them.

Charles Vale again.

The wording suggested that Charles had understood the difference.

He had ordered the administrative status to be recorded, but explicitly prohibited it from being used as an identity determination.

Yet the status had been marked completed.

And the receiving proposal had remained available.

Edward could not tell whether Charles was attempting to keep a contingency alive or quietly create a substitute for a verification process he could not complete.

The evidence supported both possibilities.

It did not yet establish which one was true.

At the Valmont Estate, Elena was on the telephone with the programme's accommodation coordinator.

“Yes, I received the confirmation.”

She opened the document on her laptop.

“I'm just checking the arrival procedure. The programme begins on Monday, but I arrive two days earlier.”

The coordinator explained the check-in process.

Elena made a note.

“Thank you. And if I arrive outside the regular office hours?”

A short explanation followed.

“Understood. I'll use the emergency contact number if necessary.”

She ended the call and checked the document again.

Everything appeared to be in order.

Her flight was confirmed.

Her accommodation was confirmed.

Her programme registration was complete.

The only remaining task was to finish packing.

She looked at the small notebook Julian had given her.

The first page still contained his list of reminders.

Elena smiled.

Then she turned to her laptop.

Her project proposal needed one final revision before she sent it to Clara.

She opened the document and added a section about audit histories.

A grant application should not disappear into an administrative office without a record of who received it.

A decision should not be treated as approved merely because someone had acknowledged an envelope.

A preliminary review should not become a final determination simply because a status field had been updated.

She paused.

The principles seemed obvious when written plainly.

Yet institutions repeatedly struggled to follow them.

Elena saved the document.

Then she sent it to Clara.

A few minutes later, a reply appeared.

**CLARA:** This is much better. I like the part about keeping the original application history. Also, please sleep before your flight.

Elena typed back.

**ELENA:** I am perfectly capable of sleeping.

**CLARA:** You sent me a revised document at 11:48 p.m.

**ELENA:** That is unrelated.

**CLARA:** It is extremely related.

Elena laughed and closed the laptop.

For once, she decided to stop working.

Edward's next meeting was with Daniel Mercer.

The former review-desk supervisor looked uncomfortable when Edward placed the day-47 R-12 summary before him.

“I remember this document,” Daniel said.

“Did you prepare it?”

“I processed it.”

“That's not the same thing.”

“No.”

“Who wrote the wording?”

Daniel examined the page.

“The summary was generated from a template.”

“Who selected the status?”

“I don't remember.”

“Was the underlying report from Dr. Shaw attached?”

“No.”

“Why was the review marked completed?”

Daniel hesitated.

“The review task had been completed.”

“Dr. Shaw refused to recommend appointment.”

“She had finished her review.”

“But she had not verified the recipient.”

“Correct.”

“She had not verified the consent.”

“Correct.”

“She had not recommended appointment.”

“Correct.”

Edward leaned forward.

“Then what was completed?”

Daniel looked down.

“The administrative task.”

“What administrative task?”

“Retaining the alternate proposal for continuity purposes.”

Edward waited.

Daniel finally said, “The distinction was supposed to be that the proposal remained available, but it was not approved.”

“Was that distinction visible in the summary?”

“Not clearly.”

“Was it explained to the person who might eventually receive the child?”

“I don't know.”

“Was the receiving authority appointed?”

“No.”

“Then why did the summary say further independent review was not required?”

Daniel looked uncomfortable.

“That was the wording in the request.”

“Whose request?”

“I don't remember.”

Edward placed Charles's handwritten instruction beside the summary.

**DO NOT ISSUE AS IDENTITY DETERMINATION.**

“Did you understand what this meant?”

“Yes.”

“Then you understood that the review was not an identity determination.”

“Yes.”

“Did you also understand that the recipient had not been approved?”

Daniel was silent for several seconds.

“Yes.”

Edward sat back.

“Then why did you process the summary as completed?”

“Because the task was administrative.”

“An administrative task that made an unverified recipient proposal appear complete.”

Daniel's face tightened.

“I didn't intend that.”

“I didn't ask what you intended.”

Daniel looked away.

Edward continued.

“Did anyone instruct you to contact the proposed recipient?”

“No.”

“Did anyone instruct you to release the schedule?”

“No.”

“Did anyone ask you to remove Dr. Shaw's restrictions?”

Daniel shook his head.

“Not directly.”

“Then what happened to the original report?”

“It remained in the restricted correspondence file.”

“Was the summary distributed?”

“Yes.”

“To whom?”

Daniel checked the routing record.

“Personal Instructions Division, R-04 reference office, and the family allocation records office.”

Edward wrote the recipients down.

The summary had travelled farther than the original review.

It had been sent to offices that needed to know the proposal existed, but not necessarily the conditions under which it had been examined.

Another administrative shortcut.

Another gap between the original document and the version people later saw.

“Who authorised distribution?” Edward asked.

Daniel looked at the final page.

“Charles Vale's office.”

“Was the distribution authorised by Charles personally?”

“The record carries his office code.”

“That's not what I asked.”

Daniel shook his head.

“I cannot say.”

Edward closed the file.

He had obtained another piece of the chain.

But the central question remained unanswered.

Who was the alternate recipient?

And what exactly had that person agreed to?

That evening, Jonathan called with an update.

“The successor records custodian has found the original consent's dispatch envelope.”

“Does it contain the document?”

“No. The consent itself is still inside the sealed recipient schedule.”

“Then what did they find?”

“The envelope has a sender reference and a return address.”

Edward reached for his notebook.

“Read them.”

“The sender reference is R-12.”

“Dr. Shaw's review office?”

“Yes.”

“And the return address?”

Jonathan paused.

“Not a residential address. It's a private administrative contact office registered to a trust.”

Edward frowned.

“Which trust?”

“The name is partially obscured in the old index, but the surviving portion reads ‘Northbridge Continuity Trust.’”

Edward went still.

“Was that the R-04 trust?”

“No. Separate entity.”

“Who administered it?”

“Dr. Samuel Hale.”

Edward closed his eyes briefly.

Hale again.

The retired medical records authority had administered R-04, preserved the original records, and refused to certify the proposed recipient schedule.

Now his name appeared in connection with a separate trust that had received the alternate recipient's conditional consent.

“Was the trust established before or after the proposed placement?”

“Before. The registration date is day 18.”

Edward looked at the calendar.

Day 18.

The same period when the original recipient arrangements were beginning to take shape.

“Find the trust deed,” he said.

“Already requested.”

“Who were its beneficiaries?”

“That's the unusual part.”

Jonathan paused.

“The deed does not name a beneficiary.”

Edward looked up.

“What does it say?”

“‘For the preservation of continuity arrangements concerning children whose originating and receiving identities remain subject to verification.’”

Edward said nothing.

The wording sounded familiar.

Too familiar.

A trust established to preserve arrangements that could not yet be legally completed.

A second trust receiving conditional consent from an unidentified alternate recipient.

Both administered by Dr. Hale.

“Was the Northbridge Continuity Trust ever closed?” Edward asked.

“No. The record says it was transferred to a successor custodian after the clinic closed.”

“Where?”

“Meridian Records and Preservation Services.”

Edward slowly lowered his pen.

The same institution holding Private Instruction 07.

The same institution preserving the original Annex C records.

The same institution that held the sealed alternate recipient schedule.

“Jonathan,” he said, “request the complete trust file.”

“I will.”

“And don't assume the trust was created for Child 28-B. We need the beneficiary schedule and original funding records.”

“Understood.”

The call ended.

Edward sat alone for a while.

The alternate recipient had not been identified.

The conditional consent had not been verified.

The receiving authority had never been appointed.

But the proposed arrangement had been supported by a separate trust, created before the independent review and administered by the same man who had repeatedly refused to certify the incomplete records.

It was becoming increasingly difficult to believe that the alternate proposal had been an ordinary administrative contingency.

And somewhere inside Meridian's archive, the original consent remained sealed.

Beside it, another document waited.

The trust deed.

The one that might finally explain why an unidentified person had agreed to be considered for a child whose identity nobody could establish.
