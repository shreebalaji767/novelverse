# **Chapter 126 — The Operator Who Was Not at the Desk**

Edward read the audit entry again.

**Day 42 — 14:16**

**Account:** PERSONAL-INSTRUCTIONS\\
**Operator:** ELEANOR VALE\\
**Action:** Amend delivery instruction\\
**Change:** Principal acknowledgement not required for administrative continuity\\
**Status:** Submitted

There was no separate approval reference.

No handwritten authorisation.

No attached explanation.

Only Eleanor's name.

Jonathan stood beside the desk.

“The system confirms the change was entered through her account.”

“Does it confirm she entered it?”

“No.”

“Then we have an account attribution, not an identified operator.”

Jonathan nodded.

“Martin Keane may be able to retrieve the original terminal logs.”

“Find him.”

Edward placed the audit printout beside Eleanor's day-44 objection.

The contradiction was difficult to ignore.

On day 42, someone had used Eleanor's account to remove the principal acknowledgement requirement.

On day 44, Eleanor had formally objected to treating Gabriel's conditional response as personal consent.

Both records could be genuine.

Both could have been created by different people.

Until the original audit was recovered, Edward would not assume otherwise.

Martin Keane arrived the following afternoon.

He examined the printout without touching it.

“Where did you get this?”

“Personal Instructions system archive.”

Martin looked at the operator field.

“That's not enough to establish who performed the action.”

“Can you retrieve the original log?”

“I can request the preserved migration record.”

“Do that.”

Martin examined the date.

“Day 42 was during the system transition.”

Edward looked up.

“What transition?”

“The Personal Instructions Division was moving from the old correspondence platform to the consolidated administrative system. Several shared accounts were being migrated.”

“Were user credentials retained?”

“Some were.”

“Like Patricia Moore's?”

Martin hesitated.

“Yes.”

“Were Eleanor's?”

“I don't remember.”

“Find out.”

Martin nodded.

He understood the importance of the question.

During the earlier investigation, he had admitted using Patricia's credentials to process an administrative status change. He had also explained that shared accounts and temporary overrides had been used during the migration.

Edward had no intention of assuming that the same thing had happened here.

But it was a possibility.

“Who had technical access to the Personal Instructions system?” Edward asked.

“Me, two migration administrators, and the department's authorised users.”

“Could an administrator submit an amendment under another person's account?”

“Yes, if the migration credentials had been retained.”

“Would the system record the administrator's identity?”

“Not necessarily. It depended on the type of operation.”

Edward leaned forward.

“Then the operator field might only identify the account used.”

“Correct.”

Eleanor received Edward's call that evening.

He did not begin with the accusation.

He sent her a copy of the day-42 audit entry.

She examined it in silence.

“Did you enter this amendment?” he asked.

“No.”

“Are you certain?”

“Yes.”

“Did you authorise anyone to enter it under your account?”

“No.”

“Were you aware the principal acknowledgement requirement had been removed?”

“Not until I received the day-44 notice.”

“Why did you issue that notice?”

“Because I discovered the amended instruction when I reviewed the returned correspondence.”

Edward paused.

“You discovered it after the package had already been returned?”

“Yes.”

“Did you know Gabriel had received the amendment?”

“I knew he had received an instruction. I did not know what he had been told.”

“Did he follow it?”

“I cannot say.”

Eleanor looked at the audit entry again.

“This is precisely why I insisted on the original correspondence being preserved.”

“Because the system record may be misleading?”

“Because the system record tells you which account was used. It does not necessarily tell you who made the decision.”

Edward understood.

“Do you remember anyone asking for your credentials during the migration?”

“Several people requested access to my old account to retrieve archived correspondence.”

“Who?”

“Martin Keane's migration team. I approved temporary access to the archive, not authority to alter instructions.”

“Was that distinction documented?”

“It should have been.”

Edward wrote down the answer.

“Did you ever receive a report that your credentials had been used to change the PR-06 instruction?”

“No.”

“Would you have approved that change?”

“No.”

Her answer was immediate.

“Why?”

“Because the principal's acknowledgement was the only evidence that the person whose decision mattered had actually received the information. Removing that requirement would have made the entire procedure meaningless.”

Edward looked at the day-44 notice.

“Then someone removed the requirement and used your account to do it.”

“That appears to be what happened.”

Martin returned two days later with the migration records.

He had recovered the original access log from a preserved system backup.

The day-42 operation was recorded in greater detail than the summary Edward had seen.

**14:11 — Personal Instructions account accessed.**

**14:13 — Legacy correspondence index opened.**

**14:16 — PR-06 delivery instruction amended.**

**14:17 — Amendment submitted.**

**14:19 — Original delivery instruction archived.**

The account was Eleanor's.

But the terminal identifier was not.

**Terminal: PI-MIG-02.**

Edward looked at Martin.

“Where was this terminal?”

“The temporary migration office.”

“Who used it?”

“Migration administrators and authorised technical staff.”

“Can you identify the user?”

Martin opened another page.

“The terminal access register lists two people.”

He placed it on the table.

**Martin Keane.**

**Helen Ward.**

Edward recognised the second name.

Helen Ward had been a former employee of Vale Private Counsel's records administration team. She had previously helped identify the separation between originating records and receiving identity schedules.

“Did Helen have authority to amend PR-06 instructions?”

“No. She had access to retrieve and index archived correspondence.”

“Could she submit changes?”

“Not through her ordinary account.”

“What about the migration terminal?”

Martin checked the permissions.

“Anyone with an active migration session could access the shared account if the credentials were retained.”

Edward looked at him.

“Who was responsible for the terminal?”

“I was.”

“Were you present at 14:16?”

Martin checked the duty roster.

“No. I was at the Northbridge office.”

“Was Helen present?”

“The access register says she entered the room at 13:52 and left at 14:31.”

Edward studied the page.

“Did she have a reason to open PR-06?”

“Possibly. She was indexing correspondence connected to the Personal Instructions Division.”

“Did she have authority to change its delivery requirements?”

“No.”

The evidence was narrowing.

But it was not yet conclusive.

Helen Ward agreed to meet them at the former Vale Private Counsel office.

She looked older than Edward remembered, but her manner remained composed.

When he showed her the audit entry, she read it carefully.

“I didn't amend this instruction.”

“You accessed the migration terminal on day 42.”

“Yes.”

“You were there when the amendment was entered.”

“I don't know that I was.”

“The terminal register says you were.”

“That means I was in the room. It doesn't mean I was at the terminal.”

Edward placed the second record beside the first.

“Did you open the PR-06 correspondence index?”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“I was instructed to identify the original delivery records and separate them from the general administrative summaries.”

“Who instructed you?”

“Eleanor Vale.”

Edward looked up.

“Eleanor?”

“She wanted the original instructions preserved separately. The migration team needed a list of files that should not be merged into the general record.”

“Did you find the PR-06 package?”

“Yes.”

“Was the delivery instruction amended when you found it?”

“No. The original instruction was still in the archive.”

“Then when did you see the amended version?”

“After I returned from the Northbridge office. It had already been entered.”

Edward paused.

“Why did you go to Northbridge?”

“To collect a sealed index from the clinical records department.”

“Who accompanied you?”

“Nobody.”

“Did you return to the migration office?”

“Yes.”

“Did you see Martin?”

“No.”

Edward looked at Jonathan.

The timing mattered.

Helen had entered the migration room before the amendment and left after it.

But she denied using the terminal.

“Did anyone else have access?”

Helen thought for a moment.

“There was another person.”

“Who?”

“A temporary records assistant. I don't remember the name.”

“Can you describe them?”

“An external administrator. They had been assigned to help reconcile the old correspondence index.”

“Were they authorised to use Eleanor's account?”

“No.”

“Did you report their presence?”

“I don't remember doing so.”

Edward closed the folder.

“Can you find their name?”

Helen nodded.

“I may be able to locate the temporary staffing register.”

At the Aurelius Estate, Victoria was reading the latest update from Edward.

She stopped at the section concerning Eleanor.

“Do you think she changed the instruction?” Alexander asked.

“I don't know.”

“Edward doesn't think the audit is sufficient.”

“Nor should he.”

Victoria returned to the page.

Eleanor had spent months preserving the separation between the originating records and the receiving arrangements.

She had repeatedly objected to incomplete verification.

Yet her account had been used to remove a safeguard.

Victoria found herself thinking about how many people had been given access to records in the name of administrative convenience.

“How many decisions were made in our name without our knowledge?” she asked.

Alexander did not answer immediately.

“More than we know.”

Victoria looked at the report again.

“Then we must be careful not to make another decision without evidence.”

Alexander nodded.

“We will wait for the original records.”

In Singapore, Elena's project group had reached its first internal review.

The prototype was functional, but the reviewers had identified a weakness.

The application tracking screen showed the current status.

It did not show the history of status changes.

Elena read the feedback.

One reviewer had written:

*If an application changes from pending to approved, the applicant should be able to see when the change occurred and which authorised office made it.*

Elena considered the suggestion.

“That should have been included from the beginning,” she said.

Her teammate, Daniel, shook his head.

“It's an additional feature.”

“It's a necessary one.”

“Why?”

“Because a status without a history can be misleading.”

She opened the audit module.

“We should preserve the original entry and record every amendment separately. If someone changes a decision, the previous version shouldn't disappear.”

The group discussed the proposal.

They agreed to add a basic version history.

Elena wrote down the requirement.

She was beginning to understand that the hardest part of designing an administrative system was not making it efficient.

It was making it accountable.

Helen sent the temporary staffing register the following morning.

The name appeared on the second page.

**Peter Ashford — Contract Records Administrator.**

His assignment began on day 31 and ended on day 49.

He had been authorised to index and retrieve archived correspondence.

The access register showed that he had entered PI-MIG-02 on day 42.

His recorded departure time was 14:28.

That placed him inside the migration office during the amendment.

Edward arranged a meeting.

Peter arrived with a folder of his own.

He seemed uncomfortable when Edward showed him the audit entry.

“I remember that system,” he said.

“Did you use Eleanor Vale's account?”

“I don't think so.”

“Did you access PR-06?”

“I may have opened the file.”

“Did you amend the delivery instruction?”

Peter looked at the document for a long moment.

Then he shook his head.

“I didn't make a decision about the child.”

“That wasn't my question.”

Peter folded his hands.

“I was asked to update a document.”

“By whom?”

“Someone from the Personal Instructions Division.”

“Who?”

“I don't remember.”

“Was it Eleanor Vale?”

“No.”

“Was it Charles Vale?”

Peter hesitated.

“I didn't speak to Charles directly.”

“Who gave you the instruction?”

“A man from the legal office.”

“Name?”

“I was told his name was Mr Greer.”

Edward recognised it.

Thomas Greer, Charles Vale's solicitor.

“What exactly were you asked to do?”

Peter looked at the document again.

“I was told to update the delivery instruction to reflect the revised administrative procedure.”

“What revised procedure?”

“That the representative's response could be recorded for continuity, and that the original acknowledgement form would be retained with the file.”

“Were you told to remove the principal acknowledgement requirement?”

“I was given replacement wording.”

“Did you understand what it meant?”

“I thought it was a formatting change.”

Edward's expression hardened.

“You replaced a requirement for the principal's personal acknowledgement with a statement that it was not required.”

Peter looked down.

“I didn't understand that it changed the authority.”

“Did anyone explain it?”

“No.”

“Did you ask?”

Peter shook his head.

“I was a temporary administrator. I was told the wording had been approved.”

Edward leaned back.

“Did you submit the amendment under Eleanor's account?”

“I used the account that was open on the terminal.”

“Whose account?”

“Eleanor Vale's.”

“Did you know that?”

Peter nodded.

“Was she present?”

“No.”

“Did you have her permission?”

“No.”

Edward looked at Jonathan.

The account attribution had been explained.

But one question remained.

“Who supplied the replacement wording?”

Peter opened his folder.

“I kept the instruction sheet because I wasn't sure whether I was supposed to retain it.”

He placed a photocopy on the table.

At the top was a reference.

**PI-42 / ADMINISTRATIVE CONTINUITY**

Below it was the replacement wording.

At the bottom appeared a handwritten instruction:

*Use the representative's response for administrative continuity. Do not describe this as permanent acceptance.*

The signature was not Charles Vale's.

It belonged to Thomas Greer.

Edward examined it carefully.

Greer had authorised the wording.

But the instruction still did not explain why the day-47 summary later recorded consent.

Peter pointed to another line.

“There was something else.”

“What?”

“I was told to keep the original acknowledgement form in the package. It wasn't supposed to be destroyed.”

Edward looked up.

“Who told you that?”

“Mr Greer.”

“Did he say why?”

Peter shook his head.

“He said the principal's personal acknowledgement might still be required later.”

Edward went still.

The requirement had been removed from the immediate delivery process.

But the original form had been preserved.

Someone had not wanted the principal's answer destroyed.

Someone had wanted the administrative record to proceed without waiting for it.

Edward looked again at the handwritten instruction.

**Do not describe this as permanent acceptance.**

The wording was not innocent.

It was carefully limited.

And yet, five days later, the administrative summary would say:

**Consent recorded.**

Edward placed the instruction sheet in a clear evidence sleeve.

“Mr Greer needs to explain why he changed the delivery requirement.”

Jonathan nodded.

“And why the day-47 summary went further than his instruction.”

Edward looked at the blank principal acknowledgement field.

The person identified as P-04 had still not been heard.

The record had been altered.

The requirement had been removed.

The representative's conditional response had been used for administrative continuity.

But the principal's own answer remained absent.

And now Edward had found evidence that the original acknowledgement form had deliberately been preserved.

**Someone had wanted the arrangement to continue without consent—and had also wanted the possibility of consent to remain open.**
