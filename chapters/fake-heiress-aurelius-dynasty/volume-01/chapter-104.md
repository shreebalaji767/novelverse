# **Chapter 104 — The Register That Recorded Every Recipient**

The R-04 distribution register was not held with the trust's financial records.

That was the first difficulty.

It had been classified as correspondence custody material, which meant its storage location was determined by the office that received the documents, not the office that originally sent them.

For eighteen years, the distinction had seemed unimportant.

Now it was the difference between knowing that a warning had been sent and knowing who had actually received it.

Edward stood beside Jonathan in the archive's restricted records room.

The archive manager had placed three inventory sheets on the table.

“R-04 maintained separate registers for incoming correspondence, outgoing correspondence, and restricted recipient acknowledgements,” she explained. “The first two were transferred to our custody. The third was transferred separately.”

“Where?” Edward asked.

“An external preservation facility.”

“Which facility?”

She checked the inventory.

“Northbridge Records Continuity Office.”

Jonathan frowned.

“Northbridge again?”

“Not the clinical review unit,” the manager clarified. “A separate administrative records facility.”

Edward examined the transfer reference.

**NRC-04 / R-04 / RESTRICTED DISTRIBUTION**

“Who authorised the transfer?”

“The R-04 trust administrator.”

“Dr Samuel Hale?”

“No. The transfer was authorised by the correspondence custodian.”

“Name?”

The manager turned the sheet around.

**CUSTODIAN: M. CROSS**

Edward looked at Jonathan.

“Marianne Cross.”

Marianne Cross was not pleased to see them again.

She was seated in a small office above the administrative records company she had established after leaving Vale Private Counsel. The office contained filing cabinets, two computers, and a large collection of old storage boxes.

She listened without interrupting as Edward explained what they had found.

When he finished, she asked one question.

“Have you opened the register?”

“No.”

“Good.”

“Why?”

“Because the restricted distribution register contains recipient acknowledgements, not merely delivery information.”

Edward watched her.

“Are you saying it should not be examined?”

“I'm saying you should understand what you're looking at before you treat a signature as proof of authority.”

Jonathan exchanged a glance with Edward.

It was an answer they had heard before.

“Did you maintain the R-04 register?” Edward asked.

“I maintained its correspondence records. I did not appoint receiving authorities.”

“Did you receive the OR-28 withdrawal instruction?”

“I received several documents relating to OR-28.”

“Did you receive this one?”

Edward showed her the preserved copy.

Marianne examined it carefully.

“Yes.”

“When?”

“Day forty-seven or forty-eight. I would need the original register to be certain.”

“Who received it?”

“I did.”

“Were you acting as R-04 custodian?”

“Yes.”

“Did you forward it?”

Marianne paused.

“Not immediately.”

“Why not?”

“Because the instruction did not contain a valid recipient designation.”

Edward placed the document on the table.

“Explain.”

“The R-04 correspondence system had a restricted recipient channel. Documents concerning proposed receiving identities were supposed to be forwarded to the appointed receiving identity custodian.”

“But no custodian had been appointed.”

“Correct.”

“So what happened to the instruction?”

“I retained it.”

“Did you tell Charles Vale?”

“Yes.”

“What did he say?”

“He told me to record it as received and pending onward distribution.”

“Did you?”

“Yes.”

“Did you record that the recipient was unidentified?”

Marianne looked at him.

“I recorded that no receiving identity custodian had been appointed.”

“Did Charles object?”

“He said the appointment would be completed shortly.”

Edward leaned forward.

“Was it?”

“No.”

Marianne retrieved an old storage box from a locked cabinet.

Its label read:

**R-04 — RESTRICTED CORRESPONDENCE / ORIGINAL REGISTERS**

She placed it on the desk.

Inside were three bound registers and a collection of sealed envelopes.

The first register contained ordinary incoming correspondence.

The second recorded outgoing communications.

The third was smaller, with a dark protective cover.

**RESTRICTED RECIPIENT ACKNOWLEDGEMENTS**

Marianne opened it carefully.

The entries were arranged by reference number, not by recipient name.

Edward found OR-28.

The first entry was dated day forty-seven.

**Document received: Independent Review Suspension.**

**Originating office: Vale Private Counsel.**

**Receiving channel: R-04 Restricted.**

**Recipient: Receiving Identity Custodian — Not Appointed.**

**Action: Retained pending appointment.**

Edward read the final line.

**No onward delivery authorised.**

“That is clear,” Jonathan said.

“Yes,” Marianne replied.

Edward continued reading.

The next entry was dated day forty-nine.

**Follow-up instruction received.**

**Originating office: Vale Private Counsel.**

**Instruction: Continue administrative processing under Schedule 4.**

**Recipient: R-04 Custodian.**

**Action: Acknowledged for preservation only.**

There was a handwritten note beside it.

**No receiving authority accepted. No family notification permitted.**

Edward looked at Marianne.

“Did you write this?”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“Because the instruction was addressed to R-04, but R-04 was not the receiving family.”

“Did you tell Charles?”

“I sent him a copy.”

“Did he respond?”

“He said the distinction was understood.”

Edward looked back at the register.

The next entry was dated day fifty-two.

It concerned Schedule 4.

**Document received: Current Care Arrangements.**

**Originating office: Vale Private Counsel.**

**Proposed receiving authority: Aurelius Family Office.**

**Status: Preservation copy only.**

**Receiving identity confirmation: Outstanding.**

Edward stopped.

“Why was the Aurelius Family Office listed?”

Marianne looked at the entry.

“Because it was one of the proposed recipient offices.”

“Proposed by whom?”

“Charles Vale's office.”

“Did the Aurelius Family Office accept?”

“No. It accepted a preservation extract. It did not accept a receiving identity.”

“Did R-04 know that?”

“Yes.”

“Then why was it still listed?”

“Because the schedule had been prepared before the receiving authority was appointed. The proposed recipient field was not supposed to be treated as an accepted appointment.”

Edward studied the register.

The distinction appeared repeatedly.

Proposed.

Pending.

Preservation only.

Not accepted.

Not verified.

The records were full of warnings.

Yet later summaries had stripped away the qualifications.

The most important entry appeared several pages later.

Day fifty-four.

**OR-28 — Independent Review Suspension.**

**Recipient acknowledgement: Received by R-04 Custodian.**

**Review status: Not completed.**

**Proposed allocation status: Not approved.**

**Further action: Await appointment of independent reviewer and receiving identity custodian.**

Marianne pointed to the entry.

“That was the final status I recorded.”

Edward looked at her.

“Then what changed?”

“Someone requested a revised status summary.”

“When?”

“Day fifty-six.”

“Who?”

“Vale Private Counsel.”

“Charles?”

“The request came through his office.”

“Did you approve it?”

“No.”

“Why?”

“Because the requested wording did not accurately reflect the register.”

Edward turned to the next page.

A later entry had been made in a different ink.

**OR-28 — Administrative Review Closed.**

**Reason: Receiving arrangements recorded under Schedule 4.**

**Further independent review: Not required.**

There was no signature.

No recipient acknowledgement.

Only a small reference:

**VPC-ADMIN-04.**

Jonathan looked at Edward.

“The same shared account used to alter the notification summaries.”

Edward nodded.

“Who entered this?”

Marianne examined the handwriting.

“I cannot identify the person.”

“Did you authorise the change?”

“No.”

“Did you see it when it was entered?”

“No. I found it during a later reconciliation.”

“Did you challenge it?”

“I placed a correction beside it.”

She turned the page.

The correction was unmistakable.

**This entry does not reflect the original R-04 acknowledgement. The independent review was not completed. No receiving identity custodian was appointed.**

Her initials appeared beneath it.

**M.C.**

Edward looked at her.

“Why didn't you report the alteration?”

“I did.”

“To whom?”

“Charles Vale.”

“And?”

“He told me the entry was an administrative closure, not a declaration of identity.”

“Did you believe him?”

“No.”

“Then why did you leave it in the register?”

“Because I could not erase an entry made by another authorised account. I could only append a correction.”

Marianne closed the register.

“I was a correspondence custodian. I was not the person who could determine which family received a child. I could preserve the record, refuse to certify an invalid acknowledgement, and document the discrepancy. I could not force the office to appoint an independent reviewer.”

Edward looked at the register again.

The problem was no longer merely that R-04 had received incomplete documents.

Someone had used its administrative account to make an unfinished process appear complete.

And Marianne had recorded the objection.

“There's another thing,” Marianne said.

Edward looked up.

“The restricted recipient register had a second purpose.”

“What purpose?”

“It recorded who was permitted to receive certain categories of correspondence.”

“Not just who actually received them?”

“Correct. A person could collect an envelope without being authorised to receive the information inside it.”

Jonathan leaned forward.

“Like Adrian Holt collecting the preservation package.”

“Exactly. He could receive it for preservation. That did not make him the receiving identity custodian.”

Edward thought of the false appointment created with Holt's reproduced signature.

“Was there a list of authorised recipients?”

“Yes.”

“Can we see it?”

Marianne opened the second register.

The relevant section contained a list of names and roles.

Charles Vale.

Marianne Cross.

Eleanor Vale.

Adrian Holt.

Margaret Voss.

Each had specific limits.

Charles Vale could submit administrative instructions and request preservation transfers.

Marianne Cross could receive, register, and forward restricted correspondence.

Eleanor Vale could collect preservation material and issue separation instructions.

Adrian Holt could accept sealed custody transfers.

Margaret Voss could monitor originating records and report discrepancies.

None of them was authorised to appoint the receiving identity custodian.

None was authorised to approve a permanent family allocation.

None was authorised to declare the biological identity of an infant.

Edward read the list again.

“Who could appoint the independent reviewer?”

Marianne turned to the next page.

The answer was written in a narrow column.

**Appointment Authority: Joint Approval Required.**

**Originating Records Authority.**

**Independent Administrative Oversight.**

“Who held those positions?” Jonathan asked.

Marianne checked the appointment register.

“The originating records authority was Dr Samuel Hale.”

“And independent administrative oversight?”

She turned another page.

There were two names.

**Dr Evelyn Shaw — Proposed.**

**Helena Brooks — Proposed alternate.**

Both had been withdrawn.

There was no replacement.

Edward closed the register.

“So no one could complete the appointment.”

“Not under the procedure as written,” Marianne replied.

“Did anyone try?”

“Yes.”

“Who?”

Marianne hesitated.

“Eleanor Vale.”

Marianne retrieved a thin envelope from the bottom of the box.

It was not sealed.

The outside carried a handwritten reference.

**OR-28 / APPOINTMENT AUTHORITY QUERY**

Inside was a letter dated day fifty-eight.

It was addressed to the R-04 custodian.

Eleanor had asked for clarification about the vacant independent reviewer position.

The letter was brief but direct.

She wanted to know whether the appointment authority had been transferred to another office, whether a replacement reviewer had been nominated, and whether Schedule 4 could be used while the review remained suspended.

The reply had been received on day sixty.

It contained only one paragraph.

**The existing administrative procedure remains in effect. Schedule 4 may continue to be retained for preservation and temporary care administration. No permanent receiving identity shall be entered until the independent review and receiving authority requirements have been satisfied.**

There was no signature.

Only the office reference:

**VPC-ADMIN-04.**

Edward looked at the date.

Day sixty.

The same account that had later changed the OR-28 status.

“Did Eleanor receive this response?” he asked.

“Yes.”

“Did she accept it?”

“She acknowledged receipt.”

“Did she agree with it?”

“I don't know.”

Edward read the letter again.

It did not authorise a permanent allocation.

It specifically prohibited one until the review and receiving authority requirements were satisfied.

Yet Schedule 4 had continued to circulate.

And on day one hundred and eighteen, Eleanor had discovered that a copy had been used to notify a family.

“Did you ever learn who controlled VPC-ADMIN-04?” Edward asked.

Marianne shook her head.

“It was a shared administrative account. Several clerks had access.”

“Charles?”

“His office controlled the account permissions.”

“Did he use it personally?”

“I never saw him log in.”

“Could someone have used it without his knowledge?”

“Yes.”

“Could someone have changed the records under his authority?”

“Yes.”

Edward placed the letter back into the envelope.

The account was a route.

Not a person.

And until they found the individual who had entered the altered status, they could not establish who had made the decision.

At the Valmont Estate, Elena was having lunch with Isabelle when Nicholas entered the dining room carrying a small box.

Elena looked at it.

“What is that?”

“Something useful.”

She narrowed her eyes.

“Why do I feel suspicious?”

“Because you know me.”

Nicholas placed the box on the table.

Inside were several travel organisers, a compact power bank, and a small luggage scale.

Elena picked up the power bank.

“I already have one.”

“This one has multiple charging ports.”

“I also have a charging adapter.”

“This one has a better battery capacity.”

Elena looked at him.

“Did you compare specifications?”

“Of course.”

“Did you compare prices?”

Nicholas hesitated.

“That's not the important part.”

“It is to me.”

Isabelle laughed quietly.

Nicholas looked offended.

“I am trying to be helpful.”

“You are trying to turn a practical packing list into a shopping expedition.”

“Those things are not mutually exclusive.”

Elena shook her head, smiling.

“I'll keep the organisers. But I'm returning the rest.”

Nicholas sighed.

“Fine.”

Isabelle watched them.

There was a familiarity between the two that had once seemed impossible to her.

But she was beginning to understand that it had not appeared overnight.

It came from years of shared routines, small arguments, and ordinary moments.

She looked down at her own notebook.

“Could I ask you something?”

Elena turned toward her.

“Of course.”

“Do you think I should apply for the foundation's junior research placement?”

“Why wouldn't you?”

“Because it's my mother's foundation.”

Elena considered that.

“Then make sure you apply through the same process as everyone else.”

“That's what I was thinking.”

“Ask for a written role description, selection criteria, and a supervisor who isn't your mother.”

Isabelle nodded.

“I hadn't thought about the supervisor.”

“It might make things easier for you and for everyone else.”

Isabelle smiled.

“Thank you.”

Nicholas looked between them.

“Are we having a serious discussion without me?”

“Yes,” Elena said.

“Unfair.”

“You were busy defending your expensive power bank.”

He sighed.

For a moment, the dining room felt almost ordinary.

And perhaps that was what made the moment important.

Edward returned to the investigation that evening.

The R-04 register had answered one question.

The independent review was formally suspended, but the suspension had never authorised permanent family allocation.

It had also established that someone using VPC-ADMIN-04 had altered the review status after Marianne recorded the correct position.

But the account had been shared.

The individual remained unknown.

Jonathan entered with another document.

“We found the access log.”

Edward looked up.

“From where?”

“The Northbridge Records Continuity Office. Their old terminal records were included in the transfer archive.”

“Does it identify the user?”

“Not by name. But it identifies the terminal.”

Jonathan placed the sheet on the desk.

The access log showed that VPC-ADMIN-04 had been used on day fifty-six to alter the OR-28 status.

The same terminal was used on day sixty to issue the reply to Eleanor.

And again on day sixty-one to generate a preservation transfer summary.

Edward studied the location code.

**NRC-04 / TERMINAL 2 / FAMILY ALLOCATION DESK**

“Who had access to Terminal Two?”

“That's what we're checking.”

“Was it inside Northbridge?”

“Yes.”

“Which department?”

“Family Allocation and External Correspondence.”

Edward looked at the dates.

Day fifty-six.

Day sixty.

Day sixty-one.

Three separate uses.

All concerning the same incomplete process.

“Find the staff access roster,” he said.

Jonathan handed him another page.

“We already have it.”

Edward read the names.

There were six authorised users.

Five were administrative clerks.

The sixth was a supervisor.

**Patricia Moore.**

Edward stopped.

Patricia had already admitted that Charles instructed her to combine Richard Valmont's temporary-care approval with a revised family allocation schedule.

She had also admitted preparing the wording that described a family as informed of a proposed arrangement.

But she had never mentioned controlling the VPC-ADMIN-04 account.

“Did Patricia Moore have exclusive access?” Edward asked.

“No. But she was the only supervisor authorised to approve changes to restricted family allocation entries.”

Edward looked at the access log again.

The entry for day fifty-six carried a supervisor approval code.

**PM-06.**

Jonathan spoke carefully.

“Patricia's initials.”

Edward did not immediately respond.

The code might identify her.

It might identify a desk or an approval sequence.

It was not yet proof that she personally altered the record.

But it was the first direct link between a named person and the account that had repeatedly converted unresolved matters into administrative conclusions.

Edward placed the log beside the OR-28 correspondence.

“Find Patricia.”

Jonathan nodded.

“And get the original terminal access records, not another summary.”

Edward looked once more at the day-sixty entry.

Eleanor had asked whether Schedule 4 could continue while the independent review remained suspended.

The answer had been explicit.

Temporary care and preservation only.

No permanent receiving identity.

Someone had acknowledged that restriction.

Someone had later altered the status anyway.

And Patricia Moore had been in a position to approve the change.

The next interview would not be about what Charles Vale had instructed her to write.

It would be about what she herself had approved.

*The original access log may finally establish who changed the records—and whether Patricia Moore acted alone.*
