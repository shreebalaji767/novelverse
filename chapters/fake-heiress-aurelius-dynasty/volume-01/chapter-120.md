# **Chapter 120 — The Delivery Made Through V-17**

The delivery record was not in the Cross Administrative Services archive.

Edward had expected that possibility.

What he had not expected was the location where the missing record eventually appeared.

V-17.

Again.

He placed the day-40 cover sheet beside the property custody register recovered several chapters earlier.

The same private residential property had held Annex C, Schedule 4, and the sealed Personal Instruction 07 archive.

Now it appeared in the history of a conditional response sent to Cross Administrative Services.

Jonathan examined the two records.

“V-17 was not listed as an authorised correspondence centre.”

“No.”

“Then why would a response pass through it?”

“Because someone wanted the sender's identity protected.”

“Or because someone wanted to control the delivery.”

Edward closed the folder.

“We need the original property register.”

The former caretaker of V-17, Arthur Bell, was no longer living at the property.

The estate had been transferred to a preservation company after Charles Vale's death, and Arthur now lived with his daughter in a quiet residential district.

He recognised Edward immediately.

“You've been asking about that house for a long time.”

“Unfortunately, it keeps appearing in the records.”

Arthur gave a tired smile.

“Some houses are difficult to leave behind.”

Edward showed him the day-40 cover sheet.

“Do you recognise this delivery reference?”

Arthur adjusted his glasses.

“V-17. Personal delivery.”

“Did you receive correspondence under that description?”

“Sometimes.”

“Who delivered it?”

“Different people. Mr Vale's office, his solicitor, occasionally people who came without an appointment.”

“Do you remember anyone bringing a conditional response around day 40?”

Arthur looked at the date.

“Eighteen years ago?”

“Yes.”

“That is a long time.”

Edward waited.

Arthur studied the paper again.

“Was there a woman carrying a brown leather case?”

Edward's attention sharpened.

“There may have been.”

Arthur nodded slowly.

“I remember her.”

“Did she deliver this document?”

“I cannot say that. I remember the case, not the paperwork.”

“Tell me what happened.”

Arthur leaned back.

“She arrived late in the afternoon. She didn't use the main entrance. She asked for Mr Vale's private office and said she had brought something that must not be entered into the general household register.”

“Did she give you a name?”

“No.”

“Did you see her face?”

“Yes, but I wouldn't be able to identify her reliably now.”

“Did she meet Charles Vale?”

Arthur thought for a moment.

“I believe she did.”

“Did she leave anything?”

“A sealed envelope.”

“Was it addressed to Cross Administrative Services?”

“I don't remember.”

“Did you record it?”

“Not in the ordinary delivery book. Mr Vale had a separate instruction for private correspondence.”

Edward placed another document before him.

The old V-17 custody register.

Arthur examined it.

Then he pointed to a small entry.

**Private delivery — Day 40 — External correspondence — transferred under restricted instruction.**

There was no sender.

No recipient.

Only a reference:

**CA-17.**

Edward looked at Jonathan.

The delivery had occurred.

But the record still did not identify the person who had carried it.

Arthur brought out an old notebook from a locked cabinet.

He explained that he had retained it because it contained his own reminders, not official property records.

The page for day 40 had only two lines.

*Woman with brown case arrived.*

*Mr Vale instructed that the package should go directly to restricted correspondence, not the general office.*

Below that was another note.

*Do not ask for sender's name.*

Edward read it twice.

“Did Mr Vale explain why?”

“No.”

“Did you question the instruction?”

“Not at the time.”

“Was the woman the same person who later brought correspondence to V-17?”

Arthur nodded.

“I saw her again. Several times.”

“Did she always carry the same case?”

“Yes.”

“Did she ever identify herself?”

“Not to me.”

Edward turned the page.

There was a second entry, dated day 61.

*Private box transferred to Meridian under joint-preservation instruction.*

The same date Annex C and Schedule 4 had been moved into the separate Unit Six storage arrangement.

Edward looked up.

“Did the woman visit on day 61?”

Arthur checked his notebook.

“No. That transfer was arranged by Mr Vale's solicitor.”

“Was the brown-case woman involved?”

“Not that day.”

Arthur closed the notebook.

“I should tell you something.”

Edward waited.

“Mr Vale once said the woman was not to be treated as a family representative.”

Edward looked at him carefully.

“Did he say who she was?”

“No.”

“What did he mean?”

Arthur shook his head.

“He said she could carry documents, but she could not speak for the people named in them.”

That distinction was important.

The woman might have been an intermediary.

A messenger.

A custodian.

Or something else entirely.

But she was not authorised to accept a child on behalf of a family.

At the office, Jonathan found a related entry in the Cross Administrative Services ledger.

The day-40 delivery had been received through a private courier arrangement.

The courier field contained a code:

**V17-EXT/06.**

The receiving clerk had recorded the package but not the sender.

Edward requested the original courier agreement.

It had been filed under a separate commercial account belonging to Vale Private Counsel.

The agreement identified three permitted delivery methods:

1. Registered external courier.
2. Authorised legal representative.
3. Personal delivery by an approved administrative intermediary.

The third category did not require the sender's name to appear in the ordinary delivery register.

However, it did require a separate identity acknowledgement.

Edward searched the archive.

The acknowledgement was missing.

“Another missing document,” Jonathan said.

“Not necessarily missing.”

Edward pointed to the index.

“The agreement says it was maintained separately.”

They requested the restricted intermediary register.

The register was held by Meridian Records and Preservation Services.

It arrived the next morning.

Unlike the other records, this one contained actual names.

Most entries were ordinary couriers, legal clerks, and administrative representatives.

The day-40 entry was different.

**Intermediary reference: V17-EXT/06**

**Identity verification: completed in person**

**Authority: document transport only**

**Name: withheld from ordinary register**

The restricted identity field was covered by a preservation strip.

Edward examined the approval note.

The strip could be removed only with the permission of the original custodian or a court order.

“Who was the original custodian?” Jonathan asked.

The register named:

**Eleanor Vale.**

Edward leaned back.

“Of course.”

Eleanor agreed to meet them that evening.

She was no longer surprised when Edward brought another document connected to her.

But when she saw the intermediary reference, her expression changed.

“You found that?”

“Yes.”

She looked at the code.

“V17-EXT/06.”

“Do you know who it identifies?”

“I know why the record was restricted.”

“Why?”

“Because the intermediary had no authority to act as a family representative.”

“Was it the woman with the brown leather case?”

Eleanor looked up.

“Who told you about her?”

“Arthur Bell.”

She returned her attention to the document.

“I cannot confirm that without the restricted identity record.”

“Did you approve the restriction?”

“Yes.”

“Did you know the person?”

“I met her.”

“Was she employed by Charles Vale?”

“Not directly.”

“Was she employed by Cross Administrative Services?”

“No.”

“Then what was her role?”

Eleanor folded her hands.

“She was authorised to carry documents between institutions when ordinary correspondence could not be used.”

“Why?”

“Because some of the documents contained private medical information and unverified family references.”

“Was she permitted to explain their contents?”

“No.”

“Could she make decisions?”

“No.”

“Could she accept a placement?”

“Absolutely not.”

Edward studied her.

“Then why was she allowed to deliver a response concerning a proposed receiving arrangement?”

Eleanor paused.

“Because the response was not an acceptance.”

“Who wrote it?”

“I cannot tell you from the document alone.”

“Do you know who signed it?”

Eleanor looked down.

“I know who the intermediary was. I do not know whether the person who wrote the response was the same person who delivered it.”

“Did you know the sender's identity?”

“I was told it would remain sealed until the originating records were verified.”

“By whom?”

“Charles.”

Edward's expression hardened.

“Was that a lawful restriction?”

“It was a preservation instruction. It was not a court order.”

“Did you agree with it?”

“I agreed that an unverified identity should not be disclosed casually. I did not agree that a person should be prevented from receiving information about their own responsibilities.”

Eleanor looked at him.

“That distinction matters.”

Edward nodded.

“Did you ever see the original response?”

“No. I saw the intake record and the envelope reference.”

“Did you know Susan Bell might have written it?”

“No.”

“Did you know she was being considered as a receiving representative?”

“I knew her name appeared in the temporary residential review records. I did not know she was the person behind the conditional response.”

Edward looked at the document again.

The investigation was becoming more complicated, but the boundaries were clearer.

The intermediary had no decision-making authority.

The sender had not accepted a child.

And Eleanor had preserved the restriction because she believed identity information should not be circulated without verification.

“Why was the identity acknowledgement never attached to the courier record?” Edward asked.

Eleanor's expression became guarded.

“Because I did not receive it.”

“Did you request it?”

“Yes.”

“And?”

“I was told the intermediary's identity had been verified in person and that the separate record would remain with the originating custodian.”

“Who told you?”

“Charles.”

Edward wrote that down.

“Did you challenge him?”

“I asked for a copy.”

“And?”

“He said it was unnecessary because the intermediary was only transporting documents.”

Eleanor paused.

“I did not accept that explanation.”

Edward returned to V-17's records the following morning.

The courier agreement had a second attachment.

It was a standard identity verification certificate.

The form required the intermediary to confirm:

- Their legal identity.
- Their employing or authorising organisation.
- The document they were authorised to transport.
- The receiving office.
- The limits of their authority.

The certificate also contained a separate field:

**Does the intermediary have authority to represent any named family or receiving party?**

The answer had been marked:

**NO.**

The signature field was empty in the copy.

But a handwritten reference appeared beneath it.

**Identity verified — original retained by E.V.**

Edward called Jonathan.

“Find Eleanor's original certificate.”

“She's already told us she didn't receive it.”

“Not the certificate she received. The one she retained.”

A short silence followed.

“You think she has it?”

“I think she may have a separate copy that she was never asked to disclose because the original was supposed to remain with the originating custodian.”

They contacted Eleanor again.

This time, she did not hesitate.

“I have the copy,” she said.

“Why didn't you mention it earlier?”

“Because you asked about the response and the intermediary. I did not realise you were looking for the identity certificate.”

“Can we see it?”

“Yes.”

The copy was in a sealed personal correspondence file.

Eleanor had retained it after the day-40 delivery because she believed the authority restrictions were important.

The document contained the intermediary's legal name.

Edward read it.

Then read it again.

The name belonged to a woman who had worked as an independent records courier for several private medical and legal institutions.

It did not match Marianne Cross.

It did not match Margaret Voss.

It did not match Eleanor Vale.

And it did not match any of the other known administrators.

Jonathan looked at Edward.

“Do we know her?”

“Not yet.”

The certificate showed that she had been authorised to transport one sealed correspondence package from V-17 to Cross Administrative Services.

The package reference was CA-17.

The sender field was blank.

The recipient field was Cross Administrative Services.

Her authority ended upon delivery.

No authority to disclose identity.

No authority to receive a response.

No authority to represent the proposed recipient.

Edward studied the signature.

There was a second handwritten note beneath it.

**Intermediary must not be asked to identify the originating family or proposed recipient.**

The note bore Charles Vale's initials.

Edward placed the certificate into the file.

“Find her current address.”

Jonathan nodded.

“Do you think she'll remember the delivery?”

“She may remember the envelope.”

“And if she doesn't?”

Edward looked at the certificate.

“Then we'll ask who hired her.”

At the Valmont Estate, the departure morning arrived quietly.

Elena stood in the entrance hall with her suitcase beside her.

Catherine adjusted the strap of Elena's carry-on bag.

“Your passport?”

“In the front compartment.”

“Programme documents?”

“Inside the blue folder.”

“Emergency contacts?”

“Saved on my phone and printed.”

Catherine nodded.

She had asked the same questions the previous evening.

Elena did not mind.

She understood that her mother was trying to make the departure easier by concentrating on practical details.

Richard stood near the entrance, holding the car keys.

Sebastian had already checked the travel arrangements.

Nicholas was carrying the suitcase.

Edward was still away.

Isabelle came down the stairs with a small paper bag.

“I brought something.”

Elena looked inside.

A notebook.

Its cover was simple and dark blue.

“It's for your project,” Isabelle said. “You keep writing ideas on loose papers.”

Elena smiled.

“That's true.”

“I thought you might need somewhere to keep them.”

“Thank you.”

Isabelle hesitated.

“Send me your university schedule when you receive it.”

“Why?”

“So I know when not to call you.”

Elena laughed.

“That's surprisingly considerate.”

“I'm learning.”

The family gathered near the door.

There were no speeches.

No declarations.

Just a series of small conversations, reminders, and promises to stay in touch.

Before entering the car, Elena turned toward the house.

For years, she had believed this was the only place she belonged.

Then she had lost it.

Now she was leaving again, but the feeling was different.

She was not being sent away.

She was choosing to go.

Nicholas opened the car door.

“Ready?”

Elena looked at the house one final time.

“Ready.”

The car pulled away.

At the airport, Elena sent a short message to Clara.

**Leaving now. I'll message when I land.**

Clara replied almost immediately.

**Don't forget to eat. And don't spend the entire flight working.**

Elena smiled.

She put her phone away and joined the boarding queue.

For once, she was not thinking about the old records.

She was thinking about classes, unfamiliar streets, a new routine, and the project she wanted to build.

Her life was moving forward.

Whether the mystery behind her birth was solved tomorrow or years later, she had decided that it would not be the only thing defining her future.

Edward received the courier's current address shortly before midnight.

The woman was alive.

She had retired from administrative work and was living in a coastal town several hours away.

Her name was **Nora Whitcomb**.

Her old employment records confirmed that she had worked as an independent document courier for Vale Private Counsel and several medical institutions.

More importantly, the records showed another delivery.

Day 61.

A sealed box from V-17 to Meridian Records and Preservation Services.

The same day Annex C, Part One, Part Two, and Schedule 4 entered restricted preservation.

Edward looked at the two delivery entries.

Day 40.

Day 61.

The same intermediary.

The same private authority.

Two separate transfers, thirty-one days apart.

He called Jonathan.

“Arrange a meeting with Nora Whitcomb.”

“Tomorrow?”

“As soon as she agrees.”

“Anything else?”

Edward looked at the day-61 entry.

The delivery instruction contained a final notation:

**The receiving office must acknowledge physical custody without accepting the contents or the authority attached to them.**

He read it twice.

The language was familiar.

It was the same distinction that had appeared throughout the investigation.

Receipt was not acceptance.

Custody was not authority.

And delivering a document did not make the courier responsible for what it contained.

Edward closed the file.

“Ask her whether she remembers carrying a second package.”

Because the woman with the brown leather case might finally be able to explain how the documents travelled.

But she might also know something far more important.

**Who had been trying to keep the sender, the recipient, and the child separate from one another.**
