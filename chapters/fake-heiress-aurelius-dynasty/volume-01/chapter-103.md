# **Chapter 103 — The Reviewer Who Was Never Appointed**

Edward read the OR-28 entry again.

It was only one line in a long administrative schedule.

**OR-28 — Separate Recipient Review. Not part of maternal comparison.**

Below it appeared Eleanor Vale's initials, followed by a designation that had remained unresolved for eighteen years.

**Independent reviewer — pending appointment.**

There was no name.

No acceptance.

No refusal.

No final report.

Only an appointment that had apparently been expected but never completed.

Jonathan placed a second document beside it.

“There's another reference.”

Edward looked down.

It was a payment ledger from Vale Private Counsel.

The entry was dated day thirty-four.

**OR-28 — Independent Review Preparation.**

The payment had been authorised through the same administrative account used for several Annex C expenses.

“Who received it?” Edward asked.

“That's the problem. The beneficiary field is blank.”

“Amount?”

Jonathan showed him.

Edward examined the figure.

It was not a large payment by the standards of the organisations involved. It was enough to cover an external professional review, but not enough to suggest a major institutional contract.

“Was it paid?”

“Yes. Released on day thirty-five.”

“Then someone was paid to prepare a review that had no appointed reviewer.”

“That's what it looks like.”

Edward leaned back.

“Find the authorisation.”

Jonathan opened another file.

The authorisation carried Charles Vale's office reference.

But the signature field contained no handwritten signature.

Instead, there was a typed approval code.

**CV-ADMIN / GENERAL PRESERVATION AUTHORITY**

Edward frowned.

“General preservation authority again.”

“Yes. The same authority used for the earlier transfers.”

“Did it permit him to appoint an independent reviewer?”

“No. It permitted administrative preservation and payment of approved preservation expenses.”

“Not an appointment.”

“Not according to the authority schedule.”

Edward looked at the ledger.

“Someone was preparing a review without having the authority to appoint the person who would conduct it.”

Jonathan nodded.

“And the review was kept separate from the maternal comparison.”

Edward returned to the OR-28 entry.

“Then we need to know what the reviewer was supposed to examine.”

The archive manager located the original OR-28 correspondence index that afternoon.

Unlike the other documents, it had not been incorporated into Annex C.

It was stored in a separate administrative collection under the reference **OR-28/REVIEW**.

The collection contained four items:

1. A review request dated day thirty-four.
2. A list of proposed documents for examination.
3. A draft appointment notice.
4. A withdrawal instruction dated day forty-seven.

Edward read the request first.

The wording was precise.

**Purpose of review: To determine whether proposed receiving identities may be independently associated with the originating medical records without relying on provisional placement documents, administrative acknowledgements, or temporary identification references.**

He read it twice.

That was not a simple records check.

The reviewer was supposed to determine whether the proposed recipients could actually be connected to the originating children.

Not whether someone had signed a receipt.

Not whether a child had been placed in a residence.

Not whether a family had received a notification.

Whether the records independently established the connection.

Jonathan examined the proposed document list.

“Look at this.”

The list included:

- St Catherine's original maternity and neonatal records.
- Isabella Vale's IV-204 maternity file.
- Victoria Aurelius's original maternity record.
- Northbridge receiving registers.
- Meridian transfer records.
- The original Child 28-B residential review.
- Annex C Part Two.
- Schedule 4, Current Care Arrangements.

Edward stopped at the final item.

“Schedule 4 was included.”

“Yes.”

“Even though Dr Shaw later refused to certify it.”

“Which means the independent review was expected to examine the proposed allocation, not simply the medical records.”

Edward continued reading.

The next paragraph contained an important restriction.

**The reviewer must not treat the proposed family allocation as an established receiving identity. Any discrepancy between originating and receiving records must remain unresolved until independently reconciled.**

There was no ambiguity.

The person who prepared this review understood the problem.

They knew the documents were incomplete.

They knew the proposed allocations could not be accepted as proof.

And they had written that limitation into the review request.

“Who drafted it?” Jonathan asked.

Edward turned the page.

The authorisation field was blank.

The document had been prepared under the Vale Private Counsel administrative office.

But the drafter's name had been removed from the surviving copy.

The draft appointment notice was more revealing.

It named a proposed independent reviewer.

**Dr Evelyn Shaw.**

Edward looked at Jonathan.

“Dr Shaw was nominated.”

“Yes. But her later interview suggested she declined after examining incomplete records.”

“Was she formally appointed before she declined?”

Jonathan checked the register.

“No appointment was completed.”

“Then why is there a draft?”

“Someone prepared it. It was never issued.”

Edward placed the draft beside the payment ledger.

The payment had been released on day thirty-five.

The draft appointment was prepared on day thirty-six.

Dr Shaw's refusal was recorded on day forty-two.

And the entire appointment process was withdrawn on day forty-seven.

“Get her here,” Edward said.

Dr Evelyn Shaw arrived the following morning.

She recognised the OR-28 reference immediately.

“I wondered when someone would find that.”

Edward watched her carefully.

“You knew about the separate review?”

“I was approached about it.”

“By whom?”

“An administrative representative from Vale Private Counsel. I believe the communication came through Charles Vale's office.”

“Did you meet Charles?”

“Once. Briefly.”

“What did he want?”

“He wanted an independent review of the records.”

“That sounds reasonable.”

“It would have been, if the purpose had remained independent.”

Edward placed the review request before her.

“Is this the document you received?”

Dr Shaw read it.

“Yes. I recognise the wording.”

“Did you agree to conduct the review?”

“I agreed to examine the proposed scope. I did not accept the appointment.”

“Why?”

She looked at the document list.

“Because I was not given the complete material.”

“Which documents were missing?”

“The original receiving identity records. Annex C Part Two was not available to me. I was given references to it, summaries of its contents, and extracts from the proposed allocation schedule.”

“Were you told Part Two existed?”

“Yes.”

“Were you told where it was?”

“I was told it was under restricted custody.”

“Who told you?”

“The administrative representative.”

“Charles Vale?”

“I cannot say whether he personally gave that instruction. The communication came from his office.”

Edward nodded.

“What else was missing?”

“The original neonatal identification material for at least one of the children. The Northbridge register was incomplete. The proposed receiving schedule contained administrative references that could not be independently matched to the originating records.”

“Could you have completed the review with those documents missing?”

“No.”

“Could you have issued a preliminary opinion?”

“I could have described the discrepancies. I could not have confirmed a receiving identity.”

“Did anyone ask you to do that?”

Dr Shaw paused.

“Yes.”

“Who?”

“Charles Vale.”

Edward leaned forward.

“What did he ask?”

“He asked whether I could confirm that the proposed arrangements were sufficiently supported for administrative purposes.”

“Meaning?”

“He wanted a statement that the arrangements could continue without being treated as final identity determinations.”

Edward considered that.

“That sounds similar to the temporary-care language used in Schedule 4.”

“It was similar. But I was concerned that the distinction would not survive once the statement entered the administrative record.”

“Why?”

“Because a provisional arrangement can be used properly for a limited purpose. It can also become the foundation for a permanent decision if people begin treating it as settled.”

She placed the document down.

“I refused to provide that statement.”

“Did you explain why?”

“I wrote that an independent review could not certify an identity using documents that were themselves dependent on the proposed identity.”

Edward looked at her.

“That is the circular problem.”

“Exactly.”

“Did Charles accept your refusal?”

“He said he understood.”

“Did he?”

“I don't know.”

She looked at the document again.

“But I received a second communication.”

Edward's attention sharpened.

“When?”

“Several days later.”

“From whom?”

“The same office.”

“What did it say?”

“That the independent review was no longer required because the receiving arrangements had already been recorded under a separate administrative procedure.”

Edward exchanged a glance with Jonathan.

“Schedule 4?”

Dr Shaw nodded.

“I believe so.”

“Did you see the final version?”

“No. I only saw the draft and the proposed wording.”

“Did you sign anything?”

“No.”

“Did you receive payment?”

“No.”

Edward placed the ledger before her.

“This payment was released for OR-28 preparation.”

Dr Shaw examined it.

“I never received it.”

“Do you recognise the account?”

“No.”

“Then the payment was not made to you.”

“Apparently not.”

“Do you know who received it?”

“No.”

Dr Shaw looked uncomfortable.

“I assumed the review had simply been cancelled.”

Edward closed the ledger.

“Someone continued preparing for a review after you refused to participate.”

Dr Shaw did not answer.

Back at the archive, Jonathan found the withdrawal instruction.

It was dated day forty-seven.

The wording was brief.

**OR-28 independent review suspended. Existing administrative allocation procedure to continue under Schedule 4. No further external reviewer required.**

The instruction carried Charles Vale's office reference.

But there was an attachment.

A handwritten note had been placed behind the withdrawal instruction.

**The reviewer has not certified the proposed receiving identities. This suspension must not be recorded as acceptance of the allocation.**

No signature.

No date.

Only a small reference at the bottom.

**E.V.**

Edward recognised the initials.

“Eleanor.”

Jonathan examined the note.

“Was she responsible for OR-28?”

“Not formally. She was responsible for preserving the review records.”

“Then she knew the review had been suspended.”

“Yes.”

“Did she challenge the decision?”

“Apparently.”

Edward read the note again.

It was not a general preservation instruction.

It was a warning.

Someone had suspended the only independent review capable of examining the proposed receiving identities, then instructed the administration to continue using Schedule 4.

Eleanor had objected to treating that suspension as approval.

It explained why she later insisted that Schedule 4 remain separate.

But it did not explain why she had not exposed the matter earlier.

Edward wrote the question down.

**What did Eleanor know about the withdrawal, and when did she learn it?**

At the Aurelius Estate, Victoria was reviewing the latest update from Helena Brooks.

The report was short.

It confirmed that the OR-28 review had never been completed, that Dr Evelyn Shaw had declined the appointment, and that no independent reviewer had been formally appointed afterward.

Alexander read the final page.

“So the proposed family allocation continued without independent review.”

“Yes,” Helena replied.

“Was the allocation accepted by the Aurelius Family Office?”

“No. We accepted preservation materials and limited administrative correspondence. We did not accept a receiving identity.”

Alexander placed the report down.

“Then why does our name continue appearing in these records?”

“Because the Aurelius Family Office was listed as an originating family representative for one of the maternity records.”

Victoria looked at the document.

“One of the records.”

Helena nodded.

“Not a confirmed receiving identity.”

Victoria's expression changed.

For a moment, she looked toward the window.

“Eighteen years,” she said quietly.

Alexander turned toward her.

“We still don't know where our daughter was taken.”

“No.”

“And we still don't know whether the child listed in these records was her.”

Victoria closed the report.

“Then we must not pretend we know.”

She paused.

“But someone knew enough to keep the records separate. Someone knew the allocations were unverified. And someone allowed the process to continue anyway.”

Alexander nodded.

“Find out who.”

Elena, meanwhile, had begun organising her travel documents into separate folders.

Passport.

Programme acceptance.

Accommodation confirmation.

Medical and emergency contact information.

Travel insurance.

She had also prepared a small notebook for the programme.

On its first page, she had written:

**Things I want to learn.**

Below it were three points:

- How large institutions manage information.
- How digital records can prevent administrative confusion.
- How to develop a practical project with limited resources.

She looked at the list.

Then she added a fourth.

\- How to work with people who disagree with me.

Her phone rang.

Clara's name appeared on the screen.

Elena answered.

“Hello!”

“Are you packed?”

“Almost.”

“That's not an answer.”

“Most of my things are packed.”

“Which means you haven't packed everything.”

Elena laughed.

“I have a system.”

“You always have a system.”

“It's a good system.”

Clara asked about the programme, the accommodation, and the first week's schedule.

Elena answered patiently.

When Clara asked whether she was nervous, Elena thought for a moment.

“A little.”

“Good.”

“Good?”

“If you weren't nervous, I'd think you were planning to run the entire institution by the second day.”

Elena laughed again.

“I'll try to limit myself to the first week.”

After the call, she returned to her notebook.

For the first time in months, her future seemed to contain something that belonged entirely to her.

She wanted to protect that feeling.

Not because her past did not matter.

But because she did not want her entire life to become a search for answers other people had failed to provide.

That evening, Edward received a message from Dr Sen.

The examiner had recovered another portion of the handwritten note attached to the OR-28 withdrawal instruction.

The newly visible words were brief.

**If the independent review is suspended, the proposed recipient schedule must remain unapproved.**

Below them was a second sentence.

**No notification may be issued on the basis of administrative continuity alone.**

Edward read the message twice.

Then he opened the day-118 notification records.

The same principle appeared there.

No family notification could be treated as complete without verified receiving authority.

No proposed allocation could be treated as final without independent review.

And yet a notification had been recorded as completed.

A receiving representative had been named without accepting the appointment.

A signature had been reproduced.

A family had supposedly been informed.

The documents were beginning to reveal a pattern.

The administrative process had not simply failed.

At several separate points, people had deliberately written warnings about what must not happen.

And at several later points, the records had been altered to suggest that those restrictions no longer mattered.

Edward called Jonathan.

“There's one more thing we need to establish.”

“What?”

“Who received the OR-28 withdrawal instruction after Eleanor wrote her objection?”

Jonathan checked the distribution record.

The original instruction had been sent to three offices.

Vale Private Counsel.

Unit Six.

And one external recipient.

Edward waited as Jonathan opened the final page.

The external recipient's name was partly obscured by an old preservation mark.

But the designation beneath it remained readable.

**Receiving Authority Liaison — R-04.**

Edward stared at the entry.

R-04.

The same trust that had never received an appointed beneficiary.

The same correspondence facility that had accepted the proposed recipient schedule before Annex C had even been created.

The same office whose final acknowledgement had been changed from a refusal into a notification of completed family allocation.

Jonathan spoke quietly.

“Someone at R-04 received the warning that the independent review had been suspended.”

Edward nodded.

“And someone there continued processing the recipient schedule.”

He closed the file.

“Find the original R-04 distribution register.”

Because if the warning had reached the office before the false notification was issued, then the next question was no longer whether the people involved understood the restrictions.

It was whether they had chosen to ignore them.
