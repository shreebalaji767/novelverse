# **Chapter 114 — The Name Written on the Alternate Schedule**

Eleanor Vale had never claimed to be an important person in the arrangement.

That was what Edward found most difficult to reconcile with the documents.

She had preserved instructions, corrected records, refused to merge identities, and repeatedly warned that temporary arrangements must not become permanent.

Yet the alternate recipient schedule had been sent directly to her personal correspondence division.

Not to the medical records authority.

Not to R-04.

Not to the receiving identity custodian.

To Eleanor.

And nobody had explained why.

Edward arrived at Vale Private Counsel shortly after nine the following morning.

Jonathan was already waiting outside the archive office.

“You received the access response?” Edward asked.

Jonathan handed him a printed notice.

“Restricted correspondence collection. They will allow inspection of the routing register, but not the original schedule.”

“Why?”

“Because the schedule is still classified as an unresolved recipient record.”

“Who has authority to release it?”

“The originating representative and the designated receiving authority together.”

Edward gave him a dry look.

“Of course.”

Jonathan almost smiled.

“The receiving authority remains unappointed.”

“Which means we can inspect the history of the document, but not its contents.”

“That's what they told me.”

Edward looked at the notice again.

There was a reference at the bottom.

**AV-18/ALT-RECIPIENT.**

The same code that had appeared on the sealed envelope.

“Let's see the routing register.”

The archive manager, Rachel Morton, greeted them in a small records room.

She had been cooperative throughout the investigation, but Edward noticed that she had brought a second staff member this time.

“Because the document is restricted,” she explained. “Anything we inspect will be witnessed and recorded.”

“That's reasonable.”

Rachel opened the register.

The first entry was dated day 120.

**DOCUMENT: ALTERNATE RECIPIENT SCHEDULE**

**REFERENCE: AV-18/ALT-RECIPIENT**

**ORIGINATING OFFICE: VALE PRIVATE COUNSEL — PERSONAL CORRESPONDENCE**

**RECEIVING OFFICER: ELEANOR VALE**

**PURPOSE: RESTRICTED REVIEW**

Edward read the entry carefully.

“Was the schedule physically delivered to Eleanor?”

“The register indicates that it was.”

“Was she required to sign for it?”

“Yes.”

Rachel turned the page.

A receipt had been attached.

Eleanor's signature appeared at the bottom.

Beside it, she had written:

**RECEIVED FOR PRESERVATION ONLY. NO AUTHORITY TO ACCEPT OR CONFIRM ALTERNATE RECIPIENT.**

Edward pointed to the sentence.

“She received the schedule but refused to act on it.”

“Correct.”

“Was it returned?”

“No.”

“Why not?”

“Because the accompanying instruction required it to remain sealed until the receiving authority was independently appointed.”

Edward looked up.

“Then why was it sent to her?”

Rachel checked the routing history.

“The dispatch request came from Charles Vale's office.”

“Signed?”

“No.”

“Authorised by whom?”

“The routing register identifies the requesting office, but not the individual who prepared the request.”

Jonathan leaned forward.

“Was there a reason given for choosing Eleanor?”

Rachel turned to the next page.

“There is a short explanation.”

She read aloud.

“‘Recipient schedule to remain outside the general identity file. Personal custody required to prevent premature circulation.’”

Edward considered the wording.

“Was that consistent with the earlier instruction?”

“Yes. Private Instruction 07 was also held separately.”

“So the schedule and the instruction were both kept outside the general identity file.”

“Correct.”

Edward looked at the two references.

“Were they ever stored together?”

Rachel checked.

“No. The alternate schedule remained in Eleanor Vale's personal correspondence custody. Private Instruction 07 was transferred to V-17 and later to Meridian.”

“Who arranged the separation?”

“The records don't say.”

Edward stood still for several seconds.

Two documents.

Two separate custody routes.

One concerned an instruction that could not be opened without joint authority.

The other identified an alternate recipient but could not be examined until that authority existed.

The system appeared designed to prevent anyone from making a final decision alone.

Yet the investigation had already uncovered several attempts to make administrative decisions without the required verification.

“Can we inspect the schedule's physical condition?”

Rachel shook her head.

“Not without the required access authority.”

“Can we establish whether it has been opened?”

“Yes.”

She brought out a preservation record.

The original envelope had been sealed when Eleanor received it.

It had remained sealed during transfer to the current archive.

There were no recorded opening attempts.

Edward read the last inspection entry.

**SEAL INTACT. CONTENTS NOT EXAMINED.**

He looked at Jonathan.

“Eleanor never opened it.”

“Apparently not.”

“Then we need to ask her why she accepted custody.”

Eleanor was not surprised when Edward arrived.

She was, however, displeased by the question.

“You believe I know who the alternate recipient was?”

“I believe you were entrusted with a document that identified one.”

“I was entrusted with an unopened document.”

“You signed for it.”

“I signed for its preservation.”

Edward placed a copy of the routing receipt on her desk.

“You wrote that you had no authority to accept or confirm the alternate recipient.”

“Because I didn't.”

“Did you know why Charles sent it to you?”

Eleanor removed her glasses.

“I knew why he said it was being sent.”

“What did he say?”

“That the schedule contained a proposed alternate recipient and that it must not be circulated before the receiving authority was independently appointed.”

“Did he tell you who the alternate was?”

“No.”

“Did you ask?”

“Yes.”

“What did he say?”

“That the name was not the important part.”

Edward looked at her.

“What did he mean?”

“He said the purpose was to ensure that the child would not remain without a receiving arrangement if the first proposed representative refused.”

“Which first representative?”

Eleanor did not answer immediately.

“Susan Bell.”

Edward nodded.

“So the alternate schedule was created because Susan refused the proposed allocation.”

“Not necessarily created at that moment. I was told it had been prepared earlier.”

“How much earlier?”

“I don't know.”

“Did you consider the arrangement appropriate?”

“No.”

Her answer came without hesitation.

“Why?”

“Because an alternate recipient cannot simply be selected to replace someone who refuses responsibility. The originating identity must first be established. The receiving representative must then be independently verified. Only after that can a permanent arrangement be considered.”

“Yet the schedule existed.”

“Yes.”

“And Charles wanted it preserved.”

“He said it was a contingency.”

Edward studied her.

“Did you believe that?”

Eleanor looked down at the routing receipt.

“At the time, I believed he was trying to prevent the children from being left in an indefinite temporary arrangement.”

“That doesn't answer whether you believed the procedure was correct.”

“No,” she said. “It doesn't.”

She placed her glasses on the desk.

“Charles and I disagreed about the meaning of administrative responsibility. He believed that a child needed a stable arrangement even when the identity records were incomplete. I believed that an arrangement made without verified identity could create a second injustice.”

Edward remained silent.

“He was not entirely wrong about the practical problem,” she continued. “Children cannot remain indefinitely in administrative uncertainty. Hospitals need to know who is responsible for their care. Medical decisions cannot wait for lawyers to finish arguing over documents.”

“But?”

“But those practical necessities do not grant anyone the right to invent a permanent family identity.”

Edward nodded.

“Did you tell him that?”

“Repeatedly.”

“Did he listen?”

“He listened. Whether he agreed is another matter.”

Edward placed a second document on the desk.

It was the day-120 dispatch instruction.

Eleanor read the line about the alternate recipient.

Her expression changed.

“Where did you obtain this?”

“Marianne Cross.”

Eleanor read the remainder.

**ALTERNATE RECIPIENT TO BE CONTACTED IF INITIAL REPRESENTATIVE DECLINES.**

Then she looked at the reference.

**AV-18.**

“This was not supposed to be used as a notification instruction.”

“Why not?”

“Because the alternate schedule was not an appointment.”

“Was it intended to become one?”

“It could only become relevant after the receiving authority was appointed and the originating records were reconciled.”

Edward pointed to the day-120 request.

“Someone tried to contact the alternate anyway.”

Eleanor read the refusal.

“Marianne refused?”

“Yes.”

“Good.”

The word was quiet but firm.

“Did you know about this attempted notification?”

“No.”

“Did you receive a copy?”

“No.”

“Then why did the schedule remain in your custody?”

“Because I was instructed not to release it without the required authority.”

Edward leaned forward.

“Who gave you that instruction?”

“Charles.”

“Was it written?”

“Yes.”

“Where is the original?”

Eleanor looked at him.

“Not here.”

“Where?”

“In the personal correspondence collection.”

Edward waited.

Eleanor finally opened a locked drawer.

She removed a small folder and placed it on the desk.

Inside was a single page.

**AV-18 — CUSTODY INSTRUCTION**

The document stated that the alternate recipient schedule was to remain sealed until:

1. The originating identity had been independently confirmed.
2. The receiving authority had been formally appointed.
3. The proposed recipient had acknowledged the arrangement.
4. The originating representative and receiving authority had jointly approved any release of the schedule.

At the bottom were two signatures.

Charles Vale.

Eleanor Vale.

Edward read the date.

Day 34.

The instruction had been signed almost three months before the attempted notification.

“Why did Charles sign this?” he asked.

Eleanor looked at the page.

“Because he knew the schedule was not ready.”

“Then why did he later attempt to notify the alternate recipient?”

“I cannot tell you.”

“Did he ever ask you to release it?”

“Once.”

“When?”

“Day 120.”

Edward's eyes sharpened.

“What did he ask?”

“He wanted me to send the schedule to the administrative office.”

“Did you?”

“No.”

“Why?”

“Because the receiving authority had not been appointed.”

Edward pointed to the instruction.

“Was that the reason you gave him?”

“Yes.”

“What did he say?”

Eleanor took a moment before answering.

“He said the process had become circular.”

Edward looked at her.

“Explain.”

“He argued that the receiving authority could not be appointed without identifying a proposed recipient. But the proposed recipient could not be contacted without an appointed authority. He believed the procedure had reached an impasse.”

“And your response?”

“That an impasse does not create authority.”

For a moment, Edward said nothing.

Then he asked, “Did he tell you what he intended to do next?”

“No.”

“Did you believe he would bypass the restriction?”

Eleanor looked at the two signatures.

“I did not think he would use the alternate schedule without the required approvals.”

Edward noticed the careful wording.

“You trusted him.”

“I trusted that he understood the consequences.”

“That isn't quite the same thing.”

“No,” Eleanor said. “It isn't.”

At the Valmont Estate, Isabelle was sitting at the dining table with Catherine.

Between them lay a collection of foundation documents, budget summaries, and a draft proposal for a small educational grant programme.

Isabelle had been reviewing the foundation's existing application process.

She had discovered that some applications were rejected because organisations failed to submit documents in the correct format, while others remained pending because nobody had recorded who was responsible for reviewing them.

The problem had bothered her enough to spend several evenings studying the process.

Catherine turned a page.

“You've separated eligibility review from final approval.”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“Because the same person shouldn't have to determine whether an application is complete and decide whether the organisation deserves funding.”

Catherine smiled faintly.

“That is a sensible distinction.”

“I thought so.”

“Have you considered how smaller organisations will manage the additional steps?”

Isabelle pointed to the second section.

“I've included a simplified submission process. They would receive a checklist before applying. The foundation could also assign someone to help them understand the requirements.”

“That would require additional staff.”

“I know. I haven't included the cost yet.”

Catherine looked at her.

“Then you should.”

Isabelle nodded.

She had expected her mother to praise the idea.

Instead, Catherine had immediately identified the missing part.

It was more useful.

“I'm still learning,” Isabelle said.

“And you are allowed to make incomplete proposals while you learn.”

Isabelle looked down at the pages.

“Sometimes I feel as though everyone expects me to know what I'm doing because I'm here.”

Catherine put her pen down.

“You don't have to become competent overnight.”

“I don't want to disappoint you.”

Catherine's expression softened.

“Isabelle, you were not brought here because you already knew how to run a foundation.”

She reached across the table.

“You were brought here because you are my daughter. Everything else can be learned.”

Isabelle swallowed.

For a moment, she said nothing.

Then she nodded and returned to the proposal.

She did not want every conversation to become about what had happened eighteen years ago.

She wanted to build something that belonged to her present.

And she was beginning to understand that she could do that without pretending the past had never existed.

Elena, meanwhile, had finally closed her suitcases.

She had checked her documents one last time and placed her passport in the small bag she would carry herself.

Her departure was approaching.

The family had agreed not to turn it into an elaborate event, although Nicholas had already suggested a farewell dinner, Sebastian had asked for her complete travel schedule, and Julian had sent her a message containing eleven reminders.

The last reminder read:

**DO NOT FORGET TO CALL US. THIS IS NOT OPTIONAL.**

Elena had replied:

**I WILL CALL IF I SURVIVE THE AIRPORT.**

Julian had immediately sent a wounded-face emoji.

She was checking the programme's arrival instructions when Isabelle appeared at her bedroom door.

“Are you busy?”

“Not particularly. Come in.”

Isabelle stepped inside, holding a folder.

“I wanted to ask something.”

“Okay.”

“Could you look at my foundation proposal?”

Elena glanced at the folder.

“Sure.”

Isabelle placed it on the desk.

“It's still a draft.”

“Mine is too.”

“Yours is about a digital grant platform, isn't it?”

“Yes.”

“I was wondering whether you could look at the application process in mine. I think I've made it unnecessarily complicated.”

Elena opened the folder.

She read the first few pages.

Then she looked up.

“You've separated eligibility from approval.”

Isabelle nodded.

“But I think the preliminary review still asks for too many documents.”

Elena turned to the next page.

“Maybe you can divide them into essential documents and documents required only after shortlisting.”

Isabelle leaned closer.

“That would reduce the initial burden.”

“And it would stop small organisations from spending money collecting documents before they know whether they qualify.”

Isabelle considered the suggestion.

“That's actually useful.”

Elena smiled.

“Good.”

They continued reviewing the proposal.

There were disagreements.

Isabelle wanted stricter documentation requirements.

Elena thought the initial process should be simpler.

Neither changed her mind immediately.

But they listened.

After a while, Isabelle closed the folder.

“Thank you.”

“You're welcome.”

She hesitated.

“Are you excited about Singapore?”

“Yes.”

“Are you nervous?”

“A little.”

Isabelle looked at the closed suitcase.

“I think I would be.”

Elena smiled.

“I'm sure you'll have your own opportunities.”

“I hope so.”

The conversation settled into a comfortable silence.

Then Isabelle said, “I don't want you to think I'm only asking for help because you're leaving.”

Elena looked at her.

“I don't.”

“I know things have been strange between us.”

“They have.”

“And I know I haven't always been fair.”

Elena did not interrupt.

Isabelle continued.

“I don't expect everything to become normal just because we're trying to be civil.”

“Neither do I.”

Isabelle nodded.

“Good.”

Elena returned the folder.

“Your proposal is promising. It just needs a clearer process.”

“Yours too.”

Elena laughed.

“Mine has an entire budget attached.”

“Which is probably why yours is more realistic.”

“Not necessarily.”

Isabelle stood.

“I'll revise it.”

“Send me the next version.”

“I will.”

After she left, Elena looked at the folder's empty place on the desk.

Their relationship was still complicated.

But perhaps it did not need to become simple immediately.

That evening, Edward received a call from Rachel Morton.

“The alternate schedule has a second custody reference,” she said.

Edward sat upright.

“What reference?”

“AV-18/ALT-RECIPIENT. The schedule was not the only document transferred to Eleanor Vale.”

“What else?”

“A sealed cover sheet.”

“Does it identify the recipient?”

“No. But it identifies the office that prepared the proposed alternate arrangement.”

Edward waited.

Rachel continued.

“Vale Private Counsel's personal correspondence division did not prepare the schedule. It received it from an external administrative office.”

“Which one?”

“Northbridge Clinical Review Unit.”

Edward's expression hardened.

“Northbridge prepared the alternate recipient schedule?”

“The routing record says it originated there.”

“Who authorised it?”

“The authorisation field is restricted.”

“Can you provide the reference?”

Rachel read it out.

**NCRU/AR-34/02.**

Edward wrote it down.

“Is there a name attached?”

“Not on the routing sheet.”

“Any other reference?”

“Yes. A note indicates that the schedule was prepared using an earlier recipient proposal.”

“Which proposal?”

Rachel paused.

“That's what makes this unusual.”

“What?”

“The earlier proposal was filed under a different child reference.”

Edward looked at his notebook.

“Which one?”

“Child 28-B.”

The room seemed to grow quieter.

Child 28-B.

The infant who had been given a proposed name, transferred through multiple temporary arrangements, and later used in a series of disputed family allocation documents.

Edward spoke carefully.

“Does the schedule state that the alternate recipient was intended for Child 28-B?”

“No. It only says the proposal was derived from the Child 28-B administrative file.”

“Could that mean the alternate recipient was meant to receive Child 28-B?”

“It could. But the schedule itself remains sealed.”

Edward closed his notebook.

“Do not release anything. Preserve the routing record and the cover sheet. We'll request the original authority through the proper process.”

“Understood.”

The call ended.

Jonathan, who had been reviewing the investigation timeline, looked up.

“Child 28-B again?”

“Yes.”

“Then the alternate recipient may have been connected to the same proposed allocation Susan Bell refused.”

“Possibly.”

Jonathan tapped the table.

“But if the alternate schedule was prepared at Northbridge, who gave the clinic permission to propose another recipient?”

Edward looked at the reference.

**NCRU/AR-34/02.**

The answer was likely in the original authorisation record.

But there was another problem.

The schedule had been prepared at Northbridge, transferred to Eleanor, and kept sealed under an instruction signed by Charles and Eleanor.

Someone had later attempted to use it to contact an alternate recipient.

The attempt had failed.

And the identity of that alternate remained hidden.

Edward opened the investigation timeline and added a new entry.

**DAY 34 — NORTHBRIDGE ALTERNATE RECIPIENT SCHEDULE PREPARED FROM CHILD 28-B FILE.**

He stared at the words.

Then he added another line.

**ORIGINAL PROPOSAL AND AUTHORITY NOT YET IDENTIFIED.**

The investigation had uncovered another layer of the arrangement.

But it had not established whether the proposed alternate recipient was a biological family member, a temporary guardian, or simply another administrative destination.

Until the schedule was lawfully opened, the name would remain unknown.

And now Edward had a new question.

Why had Northbridge prepared an alternate family arrangement for Child 28-B before the independent identity review had even been completed?
