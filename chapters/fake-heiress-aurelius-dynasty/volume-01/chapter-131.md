# **Chapter 131 — The Person Chosen to Speak for Someone Who Had Not Answered**

Edward kept the request open on his desk.

**REQUEST FOR SUBSTITUTE RECEIVING PARTICIPANT — P-04.**

The document had been prepared on day 46, the same day the supposed joint custody review was recorded.

Its final instruction was more troubling than the proposed substitution itself.

*Do not contact the proposed substitute until the principal's position has been established.*

But P-04's position had never been established.

The original acknowledgement remained unsigned.

Gabriel Vale had never spoken directly to the protected principal.

And the second contact request had been denied.

“There's a problem with the sequence,” Jonathan said.

Edward looked up.

“The substitute was proposed before the principal had been contacted again. The request says not to contact the substitute until the principal's position is established, but the principal couldn't establish a position without receiving the information.”

“Exactly.”

“Whoever prepared this knew the process was incomplete.”

Edward tapped the page.

“Or knew enough to recognise that the substitute could not legitimately act yet.”

Jonathan examined the partially obscured signature.

“Can we identify the person who submitted it?”

“The request was routed through R-04. We need the original register, not another administrative summary.”

“Marianne Cross?”

“Start with her.”

Marianne Cross arrived at the meeting carrying a folder of old correspondence.

She looked older than she had during their previous meeting, but her manner remained composed.

Edward placed the substitute request in front of her.

“Do you remember this?”

She read it carefully.

“Yes.”

“Did you prepare it?”

“No. I received it.”

“From whom?”

“Vale Private Counsel.”

“Charles Vale?”

“I cannot say. The delivery instruction carried the office reference, not an individual sender.”

“Who was supposed to be substituted?”

Marianne looked at the heading.

“The receiving identity authority.”

“Was one appointed?”

“No.”

“Then why was a substitute requested?”

“Because the administrative office wanted someone to attend the proposed review if the receiving authority could not be appointed in time.”

“Was that permitted?”

Marianne closed the folder.

“Not under the original instruction.”

“Was the request rejected?”

“Not immediately.”

Edward waited.

Marianne opened her folder and removed a narrow register sheet.

“This is the acknowledgement entry. It was received on day 46 and placed in the restricted correspondence section.”

The entry read:

**Request:** Substitute receiving participant\
**Principal:** P-04\
**Authority:** Not established\
**Status:** Held pending originating confirmation\
**Action:** No contact authorised

There was no recipient name.

No approval.

No signature.

Edward looked at her.

“Who decided to hold it?”

“The instruction accompanying the request said that no contact was to be made until the principal's position had been established.”

“Who wrote that instruction?”

“I don't know.”

“Did you ask?”

“I did.”

“And?”

“I was told it came from the Personal Instructions Division.”

“Did you accept that explanation?”

“I accepted the document into custody. That is not the same as accepting the instruction as valid.”

Edward almost smiled.

Marianne had become very careful about the distinction.

“Did anyone later ask you to release the substitute's name?”

“Yes.”

“When?”

“Day 58.”

Edward sat straighter.

“Who?”

“Charles Vale.”

“What did he ask for?”

“He wanted the recipient schedule and the identity of the proposed substitute.”

“Did you provide them?”

“No.”

“Why not?”

“Because the principal's position was still unverified, and the substitute had not been contacted or appointed.”

Marianne turned the register towards him.

“I recorded the request as refused.”

Edward examined the entry.

There was a reference beneath it.

**R-04/ALT-58 — PRESERVATION COPY RETAINED.**

“Do you still have the copy?”

“I do.”

She removed another sheet.

It was a delivery instruction with a handwritten note on the reverse.

*The substitute may be used to complete the review if the receiving authority remains unavailable.*

The handwriting was not identified.

Edward read it twice.

“Was this attached to the original request?”

“No. It arrived separately on day 58.”

“Did it include an authority reference?”

“No.”

“Did you follow it?”

“No.”

“Did you send it back?”

Marianne shook her head.

“I preserved it. I did not want a later record to suggest that I had accepted an instruction without knowing who had issued it.”

Edward placed the two documents side by side.

The original request had been conditional.

The later instruction attempted to remove the condition.

Neither contained a valid appointment.

“Did you ever learn the substitute's identity?”

Marianne hesitated.

“I saw a name in a separate correspondence extract.”

“What name?”

“I cannot be certain it was the same person. The extract referred to a person identified as S. Bell.”

Edward's expression changed.

“Susan Bell?”

“I don't know. The surname was Bell. There was no full identity confirmation.”

Susan Bell did not seem surprised when Edward contacted her.

She had already become accustomed to receiving questions about documents she had once handled.

But when Edward showed her the substitute request, her expression changed.

“I never agreed to represent anyone,” she said.

“Did anyone approach you about attending a joint custody review?”

“Not as a receiving identity authority.”

“Did anyone approach you as a substitute?”

She examined the document.

“I remember being asked whether I would attend a meeting concerning a proposed family allocation.”

“Who asked?”

“A man from Vale Private Counsel. I believe he was a solicitor, but I don't remember his name.”

“What did he tell you?”

“That the meeting would concern the administrative continuity of a child already in temporary care.”

“Did he identify the child?”

“No.”

“Did he identify the proposed receiving family?”

“No.”

“Did he tell you that you would be acting on behalf of P-04?”

Susan looked at him sharply.

“Certainly not.”

Edward waited.

She continued.

“He said I would be asked to review a proposed arrangement and confirm whether the paperwork had been received.”

“Did you agree?”

“I said I would consider attending if I received the documents beforehand.”

“Was that recorded as conditional agreement?”

“I don't know what they recorded.”

“Did you receive the documents?”

“No.”

“Did you attend?”

“No.”

Edward showed her the day-46 schedule.

Susan read the two participant fields.

Her eyes stopped at the bottom.

“That's not what I agreed to.”

“Did you recognise the name S. Bell in the correspondence extract?”

“I recognise my own name, yes. But I did not authorise anyone to appoint me as a representative.”

“Did you sign anything?”

“No.”

“Did you receive an appointment letter?”

“No.”

“Did anyone explain that the proposed substitute might be asked to speak for a protected principal?”

Susan pushed the document away.

“No. I would never have agreed to that without knowing who the person was and what authority I was being asked to exercise.”

Edward noted her answer.

“Did you know who P-04 was?”

“No.”

“Have you ever been told?”

“No.”

Susan paused.

“Mr Valmont, I refused to accept a child whose identity had not been established. Why would I agree to speak on behalf of someone whose identity I wasn't even allowed to know?”

Edward had no answer.

Because that was precisely the problem.

Someone had tried to convert her willingness to examine a proposal into something much larger.

At the Aurelius Estate, Victoria listened to Edward's report without interrupting.

When he finished, she looked towards Alexander.

“They wanted Susan Bell to attend in place of the receiving authority.”

“Possibly,” Edward said. “But we don't yet know whether the request was ever intended to become a formal appointment.”

Alexander frowned.

“Then why use the word substitute?”

“That is what we are investigating.”

Victoria looked at the original schedule.

“Susan refused a child because the identity was not established. She was right to do so.”

“She never agreed to act for P-04,” Edward said.

Victoria's expression softened.

“Then she should not have been placed in that position.”

Alexander leaned back.

“Whoever prepared the request knew the principal had not been contacted. Whoever processed the later instruction knew the receiving authority was still vacant.”

He looked at Edward.

“Do we know whether the proposed substitute was ever entered into the official register?”

“No. R-04 never recorded an appointment.”

“Then where did the name come from?”

“Possibly the Family Allocation Review office. We have requested its original nomination records.”

Victoria returned her attention to the document.

“There's something else.”

Edward waited.

“The day-46 notice I received described a proposed joint review. It did not mention a substitute.”

“That is correct.”

“Then why was the substitute request prepared on the same day?”

Edward had been asking himself the same question.

Perhaps the proposed review and the substitute request had been prepared as parallel administrative measures.

Perhaps someone had expected the receiving authority to remain vacant.

Or perhaps the request had been designed to make a meeting appear possible when the required participant did not exist.

He did not want to choose an explanation before seeing the original records.

“We need the person who created the request,” he said.

In Singapore, Elena's project presentation was scheduled for the following afternoon.

Her team had spent the morning testing the final version.

The prototype now included separate permissions for applicants, reviewers, administrators and committee members.

Each user could see only the information required for their role.

The system also preserved the history of every important change.

Daniel, one of her teammates, watched as she demonstrated the review dashboard.

“So an administrator can correct a document status?”

“Yes.”

“But they can't approve the application?”

“Not unless they also have the required committee authority.”

“And if someone changes the status without the right permission?”

“The system flags it.”

Daniel pointed to the audit panel.

“What if the person uses another administrator's account?”

“Then the system records the account, but we still need a way to establish who actually used it.”

“Can we do that?”

“Not perfectly. But we can reduce shared-account access, require individual authentication, and preserve device and approval records.”

Her supervisor, Professor Lin, joined them.

“That's a useful distinction, Elena. A record can tell us which account performed an action. It cannot always prove which person was sitting at the terminal.”

Elena nodded.

“That's why I added the separate verification field.”

Professor Lin examined the screen.

“Good. Now show me how the system handles a decision that has not been approved.”

Elena selected a sample application.

The status changed from *Under Review* to *Pending Authorisation*.

A warning appeared.

**Administrative processing may continue. Final approval remains unavailable until the authorised decision-maker confirms the action.**

Professor Lin smiled.

“That's the part I wanted to see.”

Elena looked at the screen.

She had spent weeks trying to make the prototype practical rather than complicated.

It was satisfying to see the pieces working together.

After the meeting, Clara called her.

“How did it go?”

“Good. The supervisor liked the authorisation section.”

“Of course he did. You spent half your life arguing about who is allowed to click which button.”

Elena laughed.

“Someone has to.”

“Are you going to develop it further?”

“Maybe. I want to see how the final evaluation goes first.”

“That's sensible.”

Elena looked out of the window at the Singapore campus.

For once, she was not thinking about the Valmont name, the family investigation, or the questions surrounding her birth.

She was thinking about what she might build next.

That evening, Isabelle received a message from Elena.

**Presentation completed. It went well.**

Isabelle replied almost immediately.

**Congratulations. Catherine said the foundation committee may invite you to demonstrate the platform when you return.**

Elena stared at the message.

**They can see it, but I don't want it treated as a family project.**

**I told her that. She agreed.**

A second message arrived.

**And I told her you would probably refuse any attempt to put your name on the committee without asking you first.**

Elena smiled.

**You know me too well.**

**I'm learning.**

Elena read that sentence again.

It was simple, but it meant something.

Their relationship was still awkward in places.

There were conversations neither of them knew how to begin, and memories they did not share.

But they were learning to speak without turning every disagreement into a contest.

Edward's next lead came from the Family Allocation Review office.

The archive had preserved the original nomination register, although several pages were missing.

The relevant entry was on day 45.

**Proposed substitute participant: S. Bell**

**Purpose:** Administrative review attendance.

**Scope:** Receipt of proposed allocation materials; no authority to accept placement or confirm identity.

**Approval status:** Pending.

Beneath it was a second entry.

**Day 46 — Nomination withdrawn.**

No reason was recorded.

The withdrawal had been processed under the shared account FA-ADMIN / REVIEW DESK.

Edward examined the time.

The nomination had been withdrawn at 08:47.

Victoria's notice had been delivered at 09:16.

The joint custody review status was changed at 09:32.

The order was unmistakable.

The request had been made.

The nomination had been withdrawn.

The meeting was then recorded as completed for administrative purposes.

And the person whose participation had supposedly made the review possible had already been removed.

Jonathan opened the audit details for the withdrawal.

The account was FA-ADMIN / REVIEW DESK.

The operator field was blank.

But the approval reference was not.

It read:

**PI-07 / PRINCIPAL REPRESENTATION — PENDING.**

Edward stared at it.

“That's impossible.”

Jonathan looked at him.

“Why?”

“Because PI-07 required an appointed receiving authority and an originating representative to be present together. It did not create a principal representative.”

He pointed to the entry.

“Someone has used the private instruction as the approval reference for withdrawing a proposed substitute.”

Jonathan opened the linked document.

It was a short instruction, dated day 46.

*The proposed substitute is not to be contacted. The principal's representation must remain separate from receiving authority appointment.*

There was no signature.

Only a distribution mark.

**P-04 — PERSONAL REPRESENTATION FILE.**

Edward read the wording again.

The substitute had been withdrawn because the principal's representation was supposed to remain separate from the receiving authority.

But P-04 had never been contacted.

Gabriel had never met the principal.

And the original personal acknowledgement was still blank.

Edward closed the document.

For the first time, he wondered whether the substitute had not merely been prevented from attending a review.

Perhaps someone had been trying to keep a different person from speaking at all.

He looked at the final line of the instruction.

**Personal representation to remain under the control of the originating office until further notice.**

The originating office had never been named.

And the person who controlled it had never been identified.
