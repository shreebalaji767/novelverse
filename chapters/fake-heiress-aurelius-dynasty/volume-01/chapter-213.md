# **Chapter 213 — The Storage Group No One Could Name**

The request for RPRA/14/02 was rejected at 8:06.

Edward read the response without speaking.

**The referenced instruction is held under restricted personal-records authority.**

**The Medical Records Preservation Office is not authorised to disclose its contents.**

**A request must be submitted to the originating authority.**

Sophie put her notebook down.

“Can Eleanor obtain it?”

“She can submit the request. She cannot guarantee its release.”

“Does the restriction identify a protected person?”

“No. It identifies a category of records.”

Edward opened the R-17 storage history again.

The code had appeared in several places.

A restricted external personal-custody representative.

A storage group at Meridian.

A reference in the correspondence records.

The same characters had been used in different administrative contexts.

That did not make them the same authority.

He added a new note.

**R-17 must be treated as a code requiring contextual verification. No common identity or function may be inferred from the shared label.**

Then he sent the request.

At 9:15, Rachel Dunn called.

“I've located the original Meridian storage configuration,” she said.

“Was R-17 created specifically for the band?”

“No. The group existed before the band was transferred.”

“Who else was assigned to it?”

“I cannot see the underlying identities. I can see the number of items.”

“How many?”

“Seven.”

Edward looked at Sophie.

“Seven items?”

“Yes. The band was one of them.”

“Were the other six medical records?”

“Not all of them. The archive classified the group as restricted personal material.”

“Can you identify the other categories?”

“Three sealed correspondence packets, two identity-related documents and one original clinical item.”

Edward wrote the list down.

The storage group had not been created for a single infant record.

It had been created to hold different types of restricted material.

“Was there a designated custodian?”

“Yes. The system lists a custodian office.”

“Which one?”

Rachel read the entry.

“Restricted Records Custodianship Unit.”

Edward had not encountered that exact title before.

He searched the investigation index.

Nothing.

“Was the unit part of Meridian?”

“No. It was an external administrative unit authorised to supervise restricted storage.”

“Did it have physical access?”

“The system says it could authorise access. Meridian staff still controlled the archive.”

Edward thanked her.

The next question was obvious.

Who had created the unit?

At 10:05, Eleanor Vale joined the call.

Edward explained the seven-item storage group.

She was quiet for several seconds.

“The Restricted Records Custodianship Unit was established under a separate preservation framework,” she said.

“Was it part of RPRA?”

“No. It operated under the authority of the preservation framework, but it was not the same office.”

“Could it decide identity?”

“No.”

“Could it authorise permanent custody of an infant?”

“No.”

“Could it determine who received personal correspondence?”

“No. It could supervise storage and approve access under the relevant instructions.”

“Then what was its purpose?”

“To prevent restricted material from being handled through ordinary administrative channels before the appropriate authority had been established.”

Edward considered that.

“Was R-17 its storage group?”

“Possibly. I need the original designation schedule.”

“Can you obtain it?”

“I can request it.”

Eleanor paused.

“But I should warn you: the framework may have been created precisely because several offices were handling material they were not authorised to interpret.”

Edward looked at the list of seven items.

That would explain why a neonatal band, identity documents and correspondence packets had been stored together.

It would not establish why they had been grouped.

At 11:30, the Aurelius family received the latest update.

Adrian asked the question Edward had expected.

“Does R-17 connect to the personal representative who appeared in the earlier records?”

“No conclusion can be drawn yet.”

“But the same code appears.”

“In different contexts. One is a storage designation. Another is a restricted representative identifier. We need the original designation schedule before we can establish whether they share an administrative origin.”

Alexander nodded.

“Do not let a repeated code become a shortcut.”

Victoria looked at the seven-item list.

“Are any of the other items connected to the family communication?”

“We don't know.”

“Can we ask for their descriptions?”

“Only through the relevant custodial authority.”

Julian looked at the screen.

“Is the band still safe?”

“Yes,” Edward said. “It is preserved, and its seal is intact.”

Julian nodded.

That answer seemed to matter more to him than the rest.

At 12:20, the designation schedule arrived.

Eleanor had obtained a limited release.

It contained the original purpose of the storage group.

**R-17 — Restricted Material Preservation Group**

**Created:** Day 14

**Purpose:** Temporary preservation of original personal and identity-related materials pending determination of the competent examining authority.

**Physical custodian:** Meridian Clinical Records Archive.

**Access supervisor:** Restricted Records Custodianship Unit.

**Identity authority:** Not assigned.

Edward read the last line.

“Not assigned,” Sophie repeated.

“Yes.”

The schedule continued.

**No item within this group may be treated as evidence of a common person, family relationship or receiving appointment solely because it shares the storage designation.**

Edward leaned back.

The seven items had been grouped for preservation, not because they were believed to belong to the same person.

That removed one possible shortcut.

It also explained why the storage group had never appeared as an ordinary patient file.

At 13:05, Sophie found the individual item index.

The seven items were listed by category.

**R-17/01:** Original neonatal identification material.

**R-17/02:** Sealed correspondence packet.

**R-17/03:** Personal identity record.

**R-17/04:** Sealed correspondence packet.

**R-17/05:** Original clinical material.

**R-17/06:** Personal identity comparison document.

**R-17/07:** Sealed correspondence packet.

Edward looked at the list.

The neonatal band was R-17/01.

The three correspondence packets were separate.

The two identity records were separate.

The original clinical material had its own reference.

“Could the correspondence packets be related to F-02?” Sophie asked.

“Possibly. But we don't know.”

He checked the dates.

R-17/02 had been received on day 14.

R-17/04 on day 19.

R-17/07 on day 27.

Edward paused.

Day 27.

The same day the family communication had been initiated.

He opened the entry.

The packet was described as:

**Original family communication material, sealed pending personal delivery authority.**

Edward looked at Sophie.

“This is the first direct documentary link between R-17 and F-02.”

“Does it establish that the family communication concerned the band?”

“No.”

“Does it establish that the recipient was the infant in the maternity record?”

“No.”

“But the packet was placed in the same preservation group.”

“Because both required restricted handling. That is all we can say.”

He requested the packet's administrative history.

At 14:10, the custodian approved a limited review.

The packet had been received by the Restricted Records Custodianship Unit on day 27.

It had arrived sealed.

The accompanying instruction stated:

**Preserve without opening. Do not forward to the proposed receiving institution until the originating authority confirms the recipient's identity and the personal delivery channel.**

Edward read the sentence twice.

The packet had not been opened by the custodian.

It had not been sent to Northbridge.

It had remained sealed.

Sophie looked at him.

“So the family communication itself might still be intact.”

“Yes.”

“Could it contain the missing identity evidence?”

“Possibly. But we are not authorised to open it.”

Edward checked the sender reference.

It was the same restricted family authority identifier recorded in F-02/SUB-01.

The family had submitted the communication.

The custodian had preserved it.

The delivery institution had never received it.

The packet was still sealed.

At 15:00, Elena completed the second-stage pilot demonstration.

The programme coordinator had tested the system using a deliberately confusing application.

The evidence was genuine.

The applicant's identity was verified.

But the supporting document belonged to a different organisation.

The platform correctly showed that the document had been authenticated but not accepted as evidence for the applicant's claim.

The coordinator looked impressed.

“That's an important distinction.”

Elena nodded.

“It is easy to verify that a document exists. It is harder to establish what it proves.”

The coordinator closed the test application.

“We'd like to use the system for a limited programme next month.”

Elena blinked.

“Actually use it?”

“Under supervision. We would retain our existing approval process. Your platform would record the evidence and review trail.”

Elena tried not to look too pleased.

“I'll need the programme's written authority structure before I configure the permissions.”

“Of course.”

“And I want the reviewers to test the system before real applications are submitted.”

“Agreed.”

The coordinator smiled.

“You are making this difficult.”

“Only because I don't want to make it easy to approve something incorrectly.”

The coordinator laughed.

“Fair enough.”

After the meeting, Elena sat alone for a moment.

The project was becoming real.

Not because someone had offered her a grand title or a large sum of money.

Because people were willing to use something she had built.

At 16:05, Edward received the packet's preservation certificate.

It contained a final note from the originating authority.

**The family communication is to remain sealed until the recipient's identity and delivery authority can be independently established.**

Below it was a second instruction.

**If the identity cannot be established through the current source records, the originating institution must provide additional original evidence.**

Edward stared at the wording.

Additional original evidence.

He returned to the R-17 item index.

R-17/06 was described as a personal identity comparison document.

Its deposit date was day 19.

Eight days before the family communication.

He requested its administrative description.

At 17:00, the description arrived.

**R-17/06 — Personal Identity Comparison Document**

**Originating source:** St. Catherine's Maternity and Records Hospital.

**Purpose:** Record the basis for a proposed identity association between two historical records.

**Outcome:** Comparison incomplete.

**Reason:** Original source materials unavailable to the reviewing office.

Edward read it carefully.

Two historical records.

A proposed identity association.

An incomplete comparison.

And the original source materials had been unavailable.

He checked the document's creation date.

Day 19.

The hospital's maternity and neonatal records had been separated.

The band had not yet been transferred to Meridian.

The family communication had not yet been submitted.

Someone had attempted to compare two identities before the original materials were available.

The comparison had failed.

Then, on day 27, the family communication had been initiated.

Edward opened the restricted document request.

The custodian replied that the original comparison document could be examined by the authorised investigator, but the identity fields would remain sealed.

Edward accepted.

At 18:15, the document appeared.

It was a formal review note.

The two records were identified only as:

**Source Record A**

**Source Record B**

The examiner had been asked whether the records could be treated as belonging to the same individual.

The answer was:

**Not established.**

The reason was more specific.

Source Record A was a maternity record.

Source Record B was a personal identity record.

The two records had been supplied as copies.

Neither had been accompanied by its original source material.

The examiner had also noted that the dates and administrative references were not sufficient to establish a common identity.

At the bottom was a recommendation.

**Obtain original maternity and neonatal identification materials from the originating hospital before any further identity conclusion is attempted.**

Edward looked at the date again.

Day 19.

The recommendation had been made eight days before F-02.

And the family communication had been initiated after the comparison failed.

Sophie leaned forward.

“Do you think the family communication was intended to resolve the failed comparison?”

“We can't say that yet.”

“But the timing—”

“Is relevant. It is not proof of purpose.”

He opened the original F-02 submission.

The purpose had been classified as a personal family matter requiring the recipient's own acknowledgement.

It had not been classified as a medical identity inquiry.

The two processes might be related.

Or they might have been placed together because they involved the same restricted authority.

Edward would not assume.

At 19:05, a new message arrived from the Restricted Records Custodianship Unit.

The message concerned R-17/07, the family communication packet.

The custodian had found a second sealed attachment.

It had been deposited with the packet but listed separately.

**Attachment:** F-02/SOURCE/01.

**Description:** Original source reference for family communication.

**Restriction:** Must not be opened without approval from the originating family authority.

Edward stared at the reference.

The family communication had a separate source attachment.

The attachment had remained sealed with the packet.

And the custodian had never been authorised to open it.

He read the final line.

**The attachment is believed to contain the original authority supporting the family's right to initiate the communication.**

Edward closed the document.

The next step was no longer simply to examine the historical medical records.

They had to establish who had authorised the family communication in the first place—and whether that authority had any connection to the two historical records that had failed independent comparison.

For the first time, the investigation had reached the sealed document that sat behind the family's original request.

And it was not the communication itself.

It was the authority that had allowed the family to send it.
