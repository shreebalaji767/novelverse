# **Chapter 128 — The Copy Kept Behind the Locked Door**

Arthur Bell remembered the document.

He did not remember why it had been sent.

That distinction became important when Edward showed him the day-47 summary.

Arthur sat in the former caretaker's office at V-17, a small room that had once served as the property's administrative centre. The building had been transferred to a private holding company years earlier, but its old custody records remained preserved.

“I received this,” Arthur said, looking at the document.

“Did you open it?”

“No. It was sealed.”

“Who gave it to you?”

“A courier.”

“Name?”

“I don't remember.”

“Was it someone from Vale Private Counsel?”

“I believe so.”

Edward placed the day-48 instruction beside the receipt.

“Did you receive this at the same time?”

Arthur examined the handwriting.

“No. This was delivered separately.”

“Who delivered it?”

“Mr Vale himself.”

“Charles?”

Arthur nodded.

“What did he say?”

“He told me the two documents were not to be combined.”

“Did he explain why?”

“He said the summary was for administrative reference. The principal acknowledgement was a separate matter.”

Edward studied him.

“Did he tell you that the proposed recipient had consented?”

“No.”

“Did he tell you the child had been permanently allocated?”

“No.”

“What did he tell you?”

Arthur considered the question.

“He said the paperwork was incomplete, but the administrative arrangements needed to be preserved.”

That answer was consistent with several earlier statements.

But Edward wanted to understand what had happened to the copy.

“Where did you keep it?”

“In the west-corridor records room.”

“Was that the same room where the Private Instruction 07 box was stored?”

“Yes.”

“Were the two documents kept together?”

“No. The summary was kept in the general custody cabinet. The sealed acknowledgement package was stored separately.”

“Who could access the cabinet?”

“Mr Vale, myself, and the external records officer.”

“Who was the external records officer?”

“Eleanor Vale.”

Edward looked at Jonathan.

The separation had been maintained at V-17 as well.

The administrative summary and the original principal acknowledgement had not been allowed to become one document.

The west-corridor records room had changed little.

The cabinet was still there, although its contents had been transferred to Meridian years earlier.

Arthur opened the old access register.

The day-48 entry showed Charles Vale's signature.

The day-51 entry showed Eleanor Vale.

The day-56 entry was different.

**Access requested — Family Allocation Review.**

**Purpose — retrieval of restricted continuity summary.**

**Requesting officer — Daniel Mercer.**

The access had been denied.

A handwritten note explained:

*Original principal acknowledgement remains outstanding. No access permitted without originating records authority and receiving identity authority.*

Edward frowned.

“Why would Daniel request the summary?”

Arthur looked at the entry.

“I don't know.”

“Was he alone?”

“I never saw him here.”

“Then who denied the request?”

“Mr Vale.”

Edward turned to the next page.

A second request had been made on day 61.

This time, the request was approved.

The summary was removed from the cabinet.

The receiving signature belonged to Charles Vale.

There was no record of where it went afterwards.

“Did Charles return it?”

Arthur checked the register.

“No.”

“Was it transferred with the other materials to Meridian?”

“I don't believe so.”

Edward looked up.

“Why?”

“Because the transfer inventory lists the sealed acknowledgement package and the private instruction box, but not the day-47 summary.”

“Then where is the summary now?”

Arthur shook his head.

“I don't know.”

Jonathan found the answer in the property transfer records.

The summary had been removed from V-17 on day 61, the same day that the other protected materials were transferred to Meridian.

But it had not been included in the Meridian inventory.

Instead, it appeared in a separate delivery receipt.

**Destination: VPC — Private Records Division.**

**Item: PR-06 / ADMINISTRATIVE CONTINUITY SUMMARY.**

**Condition: Unsealed copy.**

**Received by: Thomas Greer.**

Edward read the receipt.

“Greer received it.”

“Yes.”

“Did he keep it?”

“The private records division closed several years later. Its remaining files were transferred to the successor legal archive.”

“Then we need that archive.”

Jonathan nodded.

But Edward was already looking at the date.

Day 61.

The same day that Eleanor transferred the original acknowledgement package to Meridian.

The summary went in the opposite direction.

The two documents had been deliberately separated.

Thomas Greer recognised the receipt.

“Yes,” he said. “I received this copy.”

“Why?”

“Charles asked me to preserve the administrative record separately from the principal acknowledgement.”

“Did you know the acknowledgement had not been received?”

“Yes.”

“Then why preserve a summary saying consent was recorded?”

“Because the summary was an administrative record, not a consent document.”

Edward placed the day-47 summary on the table.

“Most people reading this would interpret it as consent.”

“Only if they ignored the restrictions.”

“Those restrictions were not printed on the summary.”

Greer examined the document.

“That is correct.”

“Why?”

“The distribution system had a limited number of status fields. The qualification was supposed to remain in the supporting correspondence.”

“Was that a reasonable way to record such an important distinction?”

Greer looked at Edward.

“No.”

The admission came without hesitation.

Edward continued.

“Did you ever use this summary to claim the proposed recipient had accepted the child?”

“No.”

“Did Charles?”

“I cannot speak for Charles.”

“Did you ever see a signed acknowledgement from P-04?”

“No.”

“Did you know who P-04 was?”

Greer paused.

“I knew the protected reference.”

“Was the person a member of the Aurelius family?”

“I cannot confirm that.”

“Was the person connected to the Valmont family?”

“I cannot confirm that either.”

Edward leaned forward.

“Mr Greer, I am not asking you to reveal a protected identity without authority. I am asking whether the identity was ever independently verified.”

“It was not.”

“Then why did the records continue to describe a proposed recipient?”

“Because the proposed recipient had been identified for consideration. That did not mean the person's identity or authority had been fully established.”

Edward sat back.

The distinction was becoming familiar.

Identified for consideration.

Not independently verified.

Not appointed.

Not accepted.

The same qualifications appeared in nearly every surviving record.

Yet the administrative summaries repeatedly compressed them into completed actions.

Greer opened his archive copy.

“There is something else.”

He removed a document from the folder.

It was a short letter dated day 52.

The author was Eleanor Vale.

The subject read:

**PR-06 — SEPARATE PRESERVATION REQUIREMENT**

The letter stated:

*The administrative continuity summary must not be used as a substitute for the principal's personal acknowledgement.*

A second paragraph was more specific.

*The summary may be retained for the purpose of preserving temporary arrangements. It must not be distributed as evidence that the proposed recipient has agreed to appointment, placement, or permanent responsibility.*

Edward examined the final paragraph.

*If the principal acknowledgement is not obtained, the proposed arrangement must remain unresolved.*

“Did you receive this?”

“Yes.”

“Did you respond?”

“I acknowledged receipt.”

“Did you send it to Charles?”

“I sent a copy.”

“Did he object?”

“No.”

“Did he follow the instruction?”

Greer looked at the day-56 records.

“I cannot establish that.”

Edward studied the letter.

Eleanor had continued to object even after the administrative summary had been issued.

And Greer had preserved her objection.

But the question remained: why had Charles insisted on maintaining the summary in a separate private archive?

At the Valmont Estate, Isabelle was reviewing the results of the foundation's pilot programme.

The committee had approved a small number of grants.

Several applications had been rejected.

Isabelle had prepared a report identifying the most common reasons for rejection, including missing documents, unclear eligibility, and incomplete financial information.

Catherine read the report.

“You've separated the applications that were rejected because they were ineligible from those that were incomplete.”

“Yes. I thought it was important.”

“Why?”

“Because an incomplete application might be corrected. An ineligible one cannot be approved under the current rules.”

Catherine nodded.

“That's a useful distinction.”

Isabelle hesitated.

“I also included a section about applicants who were never told why their applications were delayed.”

Catherine looked up.

“Good.”

“I don't think the foundation should call an application complete just because someone has stopped reviewing it.”

Catherine smiled faintly.

“Someone has been listening to Elena.”

Isabelle looked down at the report.

“She's good at noticing things like that.”

There was no bitterness in her voice.

Only a quiet acknowledgement.

Catherine returned to the pages.

“You should present this to the committee.”

“Do you think they'll accept it?”

“They may disagree with some recommendations.”

“That isn't the same as rejecting the report.”

“Exactly.”

Isabelle smiled.

For the first time, she was beginning to enjoy the work without measuring every achievement against Elena's.

In Singapore, Elena received an email from her programme supervisor.

The prototype had been selected for a second internal review.

The supervisor wanted the team to demonstrate how its version history would work when an application was amended by an administrator.

Elena was pleased.

She had expected the feature to be considered unnecessary.

Instead, it had become one of the most important parts of the project.

She opened the prototype and created a demonstration record.

**Application submitted.**

**Document missing.**

**Correction requested.**

**Document uploaded.**

**Eligibility reviewed.**

**Decision pending.**

Each change had a timestamp and an authorised user.

She showed the sequence to Daniel.

“That's much clearer,” he said.

“It's still only a prototype.”

“It's a good one.”

Elena shrugged.

“Let's see what the supervisor says.”

She saved the demonstration.

Then she returned to her lecture notes.

Her life was beginning to develop a rhythm.

Classes, project meetings, study sessions, calls with home, and occasional outings with Clara.

She missed her family sometimes.

But she was no longer measuring every day by how far away she was from them.

She was building something.

And she liked that.

Edward returned to the archive question the next morning.

Greer had agreed to provide the remaining PR-06 correspondence from the successor legal archive.

The material arrived in two sealed folders.

The first contained the day-47 summary and its distribution history.

The second contained correspondence between Charles and Eleanor.

Edward opened the second folder.

The first letter was dated day 53.

Charles had written:

*The administrative continuity summary is not to be treated as a final allocation record. The proposed recipient remains subject to verification. The principal acknowledgement must be obtained before any permanent appointment.*

Edward read the sentence twice.

It was almost identical to Eleanor's warning.

The second letter was dated day 55.

Eleanor had written:

*The summary has already been distributed without its supporting qualifications. I request that all copies be withdrawn or clearly marked as incomplete.*

Charles's response was brief.

*Withdrawal would create further administrative confusion. The original acknowledgement remains preserved. The summary is not a final allocation record.*

Edward placed the letters beside each other.

Charles knew the summary could be misunderstood.

Eleanor had asked for it to be withdrawn or corrected.

Charles had refused.

Instead, he relied on the existence of the original acknowledgement package as protection against misuse.

But there was another document.

A day-56 instruction from Charles to the Private Records Division.

**PR-06 SUMMARY — RESTRICTED RETENTION**

*No additional copies are to be produced. Existing copies may be retained for continuity purposes only. The principal acknowledgement package must remain separate.*

Edward frowned.

“Why restrict copies if the summary was only administrative?”

Jonathan examined the instruction.

“Maybe he wanted to prevent it circulating.”

“Or he wanted to control where it circulated.”

Edward looked at the distribution list.

Only five offices had received the summary.

One of them was V-17.

Another was R-04.

A third was the Family Allocation Records Office.

He checked the receipt from the Family Allocation Records Office.

It had rejected the summary as a basis for permanent allocation.

The R-04 register had preserved the conditional response.

The V-17 copy had been removed and sent to Greer.

None of those offices had accepted it as proof of personal consent.

The ordinary administrative summary, however, still carried the phrase.

**Consent recorded.**

Edward turned to the final document in the folder.

It was a handwritten note from Eleanor, dated day 61.

*The original acknowledgement package is now preserved separately. The summary remains uncorrected. No office has accepted it as evidence of permanent appointment.*

At the bottom was a second sentence.

*The person whose answer is required has still not been contacted directly.*

Edward stopped reading.

He looked at Jonathan.

“That's the missing step.”

Jonathan nodded slowly.

“Gabriel was supposed to contact P-04.”

“But there is no evidence he delivered the acknowledgement form.”

“And the package was returned.”

“Then someone must explain what happened between day 40 and day 43.”

Edward looked again at the sealed package inventory.

The principal's answer had never been received.

The proposed recipient's identity remained protected.

And the administrative process had continued without either.

He closed the folder.

“Get Gabriel Vale's complete day-40 to day-43 movement record.”

Jonathan wrote it down.

Edward added one more instruction.

“And find out whether he ever returned to P-04 after the package was taken back.”

That evening, an unfamiliar envelope arrived at Edward's office.

It had no sender's name.

Only a reference written across the front:

**PR-06 / P-04**

Inside was a single photocopied page.

It was a personal acknowledgement form.

The signature field was still blank.

But someone had written a sentence across the bottom in dark ink.

*I was never given the information necessary to answer.*

There was no date.

No signature.

No explanation.

Edward read the sentence again.

It could have been written by the principal.

It could have been written by someone who had seen the original form.

It could even have been copied from an earlier note.

The paper itself was not original.

The handwriting had not yet been examined.

But the message raised a possibility Edward could not ignore.

Perhaps P-04 had never refused.

Perhaps the person had never been asked properly.

He placed the photocopy inside an evidence sleeve.

Then he noticed a faint reference in the lower margin.

**P-04 / SECOND CONTACT — NOT AUTHORISED.**

Edward stared at it.

The original delivery had failed.

A second contact had been considered.

And someone had specifically marked it as unauthorised.

He reached for his phone.

“Jonathan, we need the complete P-04 contact history.”

A pause.

“Why?”

Edward looked at the unsigned acknowledgement.

“Because we may have spent months investigating why someone agreed to receive a child.”

He lowered his voice.

“When the real question is whether that person was ever given the chance to answer at all.”
