# **Chapter 168 — The Approval That Covered Only Half the Work**

Edward placed the day-31 activation record beside the day-32 objection.

The two documents appeared to contradict each other.

They did not.

The first stated that the external recipient was ready and the route could be activated.

The second stated that the principal had not received an explanation and that operational use should remain restricted.

Both had been recorded.

Both had been retained.

And the route had remained active.

Edward drew a line beneath the activation basis.

**EXTERNAL RECIPIENT READY.**

Then he drew another beneath the objection.

**PRINCIPAL COMMUNICATION REQUIREMENT: OUTSTANDING.**

"Jonathan."

"Yes?"

"Find the original activation request."

"I have the administrative extract."

"I don't want the extract. I want the request that authorised the activation."

Jonathan glanced at the documents.

"That may be in the originating authority's restricted archive."

"Then request it."

"What if it was never formally issued?"

Edward looked up.

"That is what I want to know."

The original activation request arrived just after ten.

It was not a single document.

It was a packet containing four separate approvals.

Jonathan arranged them across the conference table.

"These were the documents used to establish the R.V. route."

Edward examined the first.

**EXTERNAL RECIPIENT ELIGIBILITY REVIEW**

The external recipient had been assessed for restricted document handling.

The review confirmed that the recipient could:

- Receive sealed correspondence.
- Preserve documents.
- Submit procedural questions.
- Return documents to the originating authority.
- Maintain restricted access to the external facility.

The second document was a security assessment.

It confirmed that the external facility could receive restricted material without exposing the recipient's identity to ordinary administrative users.

The third was a technical configuration approval.

It authorised the creation of the R.V. route.

The fourth was a preservation instruction.

It required all documents received through the route to remain traceable and prohibited the facility from making decisions on behalf of the protected principal.

Edward examined the signatures.

"All four approvals are present."

"Yes," Jonathan said.

"Was the route authorised?"

"Technically, yes."

"Was the external recipient approved?"

"Yes."

"Was the principal informed?"

"No."

"Was there an approval for the initial explanation?"

Jonathan turned over the fourth document.

There was a small field at the bottom.

**INITIAL COMMUNICATION RESPONSIBILITY**

The field was marked:

**SEPARATE IMPLEMENTATION REQUIRED.**

Edward looked at the packet again.

The route had not been activated without any approval.

It had been activated under a set of approvals that deliberately excluded the missing personal communication requirement.

"Who signed the technical configuration approval?"

Jonathan pointed to the third document.

The signature belonged to the originating records authority's systems director.

"Did that person have authority to approve the external recipient configuration?"

"Yes."

"Did they have authority to confirm the principal had been informed?"

"No."

Edward placed the document down.

"Then we need to speak with them."

The systems director was named Peter Langford.

He joined the meeting at eleven.

Edward placed the four approvals in front of him.

"Mr Langford, did you authorise the creation of the R.V. route?"

"I authorised its technical configuration."

"Did you authorise its activation?"

"I approved the technical readiness."

"That wasn't my question."

Langford examined the activation record.

"I did not approve the principal communication requirement as complete."

"Did you know it remained outstanding?"

"Yes."

"Then why was the route marked active?"

Langford's expression became guarded.

"The technical system required an overall status."

"And the status could be active while the principal communication field remained incomplete?"

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because the route had two separate functions. External document preservation could begin before personal communication was completed."

Edward glanced at the approval packet.

"Was that distinction explained to the administrative team?"

"It was documented."

"Was it explained to the person who changed the overall status?"

"I don't know who changed the status."

"Was the status change authorised?"

"Under the technical configuration approval, the route could be made available for restricted preservation."

"Could it be used for personal communication?"

"Not until the initial explanation was completed."

"Was that distinction visible on the status screen?"

Langford hesitated.

"Not in the main summary."

"Where was it visible?"

"In the detailed configuration record."

"Was the detailed record mandatory reading before changing the status?"

"I cannot confirm that."

Edward leaned forward.

"So an operator could see the word active without seeing that the principal had not been informed."

"The system displayed the outstanding requirement in the secondary field."

"That does not answer the question."

Langford looked uncomfortable.

"No. The main status could be misleading if read without the detailed field."

Edward made a note.

"Was this design reviewed?"

"Yes."

"By whom?"

"The technical governance team."

"Was there a concern about the status wording?"

"There was a suggestion to use 'preservation ready' instead of 'active'."

Edward looked up.

"Who made it?"

"I believe Eleanor Vale."

"Was the suggestion accepted?"

"No."

"Why not?"

"The team wanted one status for the route and separate flags for its functions."

"Was that decision documented?"

"Yes."

"Who approved it?"

Langford checked the packet.

"The governance chair."

"Name?"

"Dr Samuel Hale."

Edward looked at him.

"Dr Hale approved the use of one overall status even though personal communication remained incomplete?"

"He approved the technical design. I cannot say he approved every later operational use."

Edward nodded slowly.

That distinction mattered.

Dr Samuel Hale had authorised the system architecture.

It did not follow that he had authorised every action performed through it.

"Mr Langford, did you tell Dr Hale that the main status could be misunderstood?"

"I believe the concern was discussed."

"Was it resolved?"

"Not to my knowledge."

Edward closed the packet.

"Thank you. I may need to speak with you again."

Dr Samuel Hale was not available that afternoon.

His office confirmed that he would respond through the records authority after reviewing the request.

Edward did not press for an immediate interview.

Instead, he returned to the design review.

The relevant document was dated day 28.

It contained a technical discussion about the proposed R.V. route.

The participants were listed as:

- Dr Samuel Hale.
- Peter Langford.
- Eleanor Vale.
- Thomas Greer.
- Helen Ward.

The meeting had considered whether the route should be divided into separate statuses.

Eleanor had proposed three:

**PRESERVATION READY**

**PERSONAL COMMUNICATION READY**

**PRINCIPAL ACKNOWLEDGEMENT RECEIVED**

The proposal had been rejected.

The recorded reason was that separate statuses would make the route difficult to administer.

Edward read the sentence again.

It was difficult to administer.

Not impossible.

Not unsafe.

Not legally prohibited.

Difficult.

He continued.

The approved alternative used one overall status and separate sub-fields.

A warning stated that the overall status must never be used as evidence that the principal had received or understood information.

The warning appeared in the technical specification.

But it did not appear on the main status screen.

Edward turned to the final page.

There was a handwritten comment beside the approved design.

*If the main status remains singular, the outstanding personal requirement must be displayed wherever the route is referenced.*

The handwriting was Eleanor's.

Beside it was a response:

*To be considered in later interface revision.*

No later revision appeared in the archive.

Edward placed the design review beside the day-31 activation record.

The system had been built with a known limitation.

A warning had been proposed.

A safeguard had been promised.

And the interface had remained unchanged.

At the Aurelius residence, Victoria received a call from Dr Samuel Hale's office.

The caller was not Edward.

It was the secretary responsible for arranging an independent records review.

"Mrs Aurelius, Dr Hale has asked me to confirm that he is willing to provide a written account of the original R.V. configuration."

"Thank you."

"He has also asked whether the family wishes to submit any additional questions."

Victoria glanced at Alexander.

"Yes. We would like to know whether he believed the protected principal had been personally informed before the route was activated."

"I will include that."

"And whether he authorised the use of the overall active status to support later administrative decisions."

There was a pause.

"I will record both questions."

Victoria thanked the secretary and ended the call.

Alexander had been listening.

"Do you think Hale knew the system would be used this way?"

"I don't know."

"He approved the design."

"That does not prove he knew how the administrative team would use it."

"But he knew the personal communication field remained incomplete."

"Yes."

Alexander looked at the day-28 design review.

"Then he should have insisted that the route remain restricted."

Victoria did not disagree.

But she was careful.

"Perhaps he believed the separate field would be sufficient."

"And was it?"

"No."

They sat in silence.

The family had learned that responsibility could not be assigned merely because someone had been present when a system was created.

A technical approval was not an appointment.

A preservation instruction was not consent.

And a general authorisation did not automatically cover every later decision made under it.

Victoria closed the file.

"Whatever happened, we need the original records."

Alexander nodded.

"Not another summary."

At 3:30, Edward received a message from Thomas.

**Subject: Eleanor Vale — Day 28 Design Review Follow-up**

The message contained an additional email.

Eleanor had written to Thomas the evening after the design review.

*I remain concerned that a singular route status will be used as a substitute for the completion of separate communication requirements.*

Edward read the next sentence.

*If the interface cannot display the distinction, the administrative team must be required to consult the detailed record before recording delivery, acknowledgement, or personal response.*

Then:

*Please confirm whether the governance chair has accepted this limitation as a mandatory operating condition.*

There was no response in the archive.

Edward checked the date.

The email had been sent on day 28.

The R.V. route had been registered on day 31.

Three days separated the warning and the activation.

He called Thomas.

"Did you respond?"

"I don't remember."

"Can you check your personal archive?"

"I already have. I found no reply."

"Did Dr Hale respond?"

"Not to my knowledge."

"Did the technical team respond?"

"There's a short acknowledgement from Peter Langford."

"What did it say?"

"That the interface limitation would be reviewed after initial deployment."

Edward closed his eyes briefly.

Another promise of later correction.

"Was it reviewed?"

"No."

Thomas was quiet.

"Edward, I need to tell you something."

"What?"

"Eleanor was not the only person concerned about the status design."

"Who else?"

"Helen Ward."

Edward opened his notes.

"Helen Ward was listed on the day-28 review?"

"Yes. She was responsible for independent records oversight."

"What did she say?"

"She asked whether an active route could be interpreted as evidence that the protected principal had received the information."

"And the answer?"

"Dr Hale said it could not."

"Was that answer recorded?"

"Yes."

"Then why did the system still display active?"

"Because the technical team treated the status as a configuration state, not a personal communication state."

Edward was silent.

That was the explanation.

It was also the source of the problem.

The technical team had intended one meaning.

The administrative team had later used the same word in another context.

And no one had ensured that the distinction survived the transition.

"Thomas, do you know whether Helen Ward is still available?"

"She is."

"Arrange a meeting."

Helen Ward arrived the following morning carrying a small notebook.

She was older than Edward had expected, with a direct manner and little patience for unnecessary introductions.

"I understand you're looking at the R.V. route," she said.

"Yes."

"Then you should know I objected to the singular status from the beginning."

Edward placed the day-28 design review on the table.

"I've read your question."

"Good."

"You asked whether an active route could be interpreted as evidence that the principal had received information."

"Yes."

"Dr Hale said it could not."

"That was his answer."

"Did you agree?"

"I agreed that the technical definition was clear. I did not agree that the administrative presentation was safe."

Edward examined her.

"Explain."

Helen opened her notebook.

"A technical status is only useful if the people using it understand what it represents. The route could be technically active while the principal remained uninformed. That was not inherently wrong. The problem was that the summary screen displayed only the word active."

"Did you propose a different label?"

"I proposed 'preservation route configured'."

"Was it accepted?"

"No."

"Why not?"

"Administrative convenience."

Edward waited.

Helen continued.

"They argued that the users could open the detailed record if they needed more information."

"Did they?"

"Some did. Others relied on the summary."

"Was that recorded?"

"Yes. We had several cases where the summary status was copied into later reports without the detailed fields."

Edward looked at her.

"How many?"

"At least four."

"Do you have the references?"

"I brought them."

She handed him a page.

Four records were listed.

Day 31.

Day 47.

Day 88.

Day 152.

Edward recognised three immediately.

The route activation.

The OR-28 review.

The delivery-method change.

And the later appointment migration.

"Why is day 152 included?"

"Because the same design problem appeared in a different administrative category."

"How?"

"The migration summary treated a document custodian as a continuity authority because the overall status field indicated active custody."

Edward looked at the page.

"So the same kind of simplification was used twice."

"Not necessarily by the same person."

"Understood."

Helen continued.

"The system was built to preserve distinctions in its detailed records. But the administrative interface encouraged people to use one broad status. Over time, the broad status became more important than the underlying evidence."

Edward made a note.

"Did you report that?"

"Yes."

"To whom?"

"Dr Hale, Peter Langford, and the governance chair."

"What happened?"

"The issue was placed on the next review agenda."

"Was it resolved?"

"No."

"Why?"

Helen closed her notebook.

"Because the review was postponed."

Edward looked up.

"How many times?"

"Three."

The postponement records were more revealing than Edward expected.

The first review had been scheduled for day 39.

It was postponed because the technical team was completing the R.V. access configuration.

The second was scheduled for day 56.

It was postponed because the Family Allocation Review team requested that the same meeting include the status of several pending recipient arrangements.

The third was scheduled for day 74.

It was postponed because the original PR-06 procedure had not yet been distributed.

The review had never been rescheduled.

Edward read the three explanations.

Each one sounded reasonable in isolation.

Together, they showed how the unresolved design problem had been carried forward.

The system had been activated.

The external route had been used.

The original procedure had been prepared.

The recipient arrangements had remained incomplete.

And the review intended to examine the consequences of the system's design had repeatedly been delayed by the very processes that depended on it.

"Was the issue ever formally closed?" Edward asked.

Helen shook her head.

"No."

"Was it marked resolved in any system?"

"Not in mine."

Edward showed her the day-152 migration summary.

Helen read it carefully.

"That is a different matter."

"But related?"

"Related in method. Not necessarily in authorship."

She pointed to the status field.

"This says current custody is active. It does not say that the custodian has been appointed as a personal representative."

"Yet the migration treated it as continuity authority."

"Yes."

"Was that permitted by the original design?"

"No."

Edward leaned back.

"Then someone used a broad status to support a conclusion that the detailed record did not permit."

"That is what the records suggest."

Helen looked at the documents again.

"Do you know what concerns me most?"

"What?"

"The day-31 route was not supposed to be used for personal communication until the principal had been informed. That limitation was clear."

She tapped the page.

"But later, when the office needed to establish whether the principal had been notified, it relied on the fact that the route was active."

Edward looked at her.

"Do you think someone deliberately exploited the status?"

"I think the system made it easy to do so. Whether someone deliberately designed that weakness for a particular purpose is a different question."

Edward nodded.

He would not allow the investigation to turn a flawed system into proof of a conspiracy.

But he would not ignore the pattern either.

At 6:05, Jonathan returned with the latest records.

"I found the first review postponement request."

"Who submitted it?"

"The technical team."

"Named person?"

"Peter Langford."

Edward examined the second.

"Family Allocation Review?"

"Yes. The request came through the shared account."

"And the third?"

"Vale Private Counsel."

Edward looked up.

"VPC?"

"The request was submitted by Charles Vale's office."

"Was Charles the named author?"

"No. The document was signed by the external correspondence desk."

Edward placed the three requests side by side.

Technical completion.

Family Allocation Review requirements.

External correspondence work.

Different reasons.

Different offices.

The same unresolved review.

"Did any of them request that the review be cancelled?"

"No."

"Did anyone request that it be postponed indefinitely?"

"No."

"Then why was it never rescheduled?"

Jonathan checked the calendar.

"Because no one accepted responsibility for arranging the next date after the third postponement."

Edward stared at him.

"That is the official explanation?"

"Yes."

Edward let out a slow breath.

The problem had been identified.

The review had been postponed three times.

No one had cancelled it.

No one had rescheduled it.

And the system had continued operating.

He looked at the four references Helen had supplied.

Day 31.

Day 47.

Day 88.

Day 152.

The same weakness had appeared in multiple processes.

A broad status was treated as though it carried the meaning of several separate approvals.

A missing personal response became an administrative continuation.

A temporary custodian became a continuity authority.

An external facility receipt became a delivery record.

Each time, the original documents contained qualifications.

Each time, the summary was simpler.

Edward turned to Jonathan.

"Find out who was responsible for rescheduling the governance review."

"That may be difficult."

"Why?"

"The meeting administration record was transferred to a successor office."

"Which office?"

Jonathan looked at the screen.

**FAMILY ALLOCATION REVIEW — MIGRATION AND CONTINUITY DESK.**

Edward looked at the entry.

The same desk that had later changed R-17's status.

The same administrative route that had converted temporary custody into personal representation continuity.

He opened the record.

The review had been marked:

**NO FURTHER ACTION REQUIRED — OPERATIONAL ARRANGEMENT IN PLACE.**

Edward read the note beneath it.

*The active R.V. route provides sufficient continuity pending completion of the remaining personal communication requirements.*

He sat motionless.

That sentence had been entered months after the route was created.

It treated the existence of the route as a reason not to complete the very requirement that had been outstanding from the beginning.

And it had been entered under the shared administrative account.

Edward looked at the final field.

**INDIVIDUAL OPERATOR: NOT RECORDED.**

For a moment, he heard only the quiet hum of the office.

Then he noticed something else.

The status had been changed on day 76.

Two days after the third postponement.

And the entry included a reference to an attachment.

**ATTACHMENT: GOVERNANCE REVIEW — UNRESOLVED OBJECTION.**

The attachment was not in the migration folder.

It had not been included in the ordinary records transfer.

It had been classified as restricted correspondence.

Edward opened the reference.

The system returned a message.

**ACCESS REQUIRES ORIGINAL GOVERNANCE CHAIR AUTHORISATION.**

He stared at the screen.

The governance review had been closed administratively.

The objection had not been transferred with the operational record.

And the document that might explain why the review had never resumed was still sealed under the authority of the person who had approved the original design.

**Dr Samuel Hale.**
