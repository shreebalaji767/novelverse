# **Chapter 214 — The Authority Behind the Sealed Request**

The approval request was sent at 9:12 a.m.

Edward read it once more before allowing Eleanor to submit it.

It was deliberately narrow.

They were not asking to open the entire family communication packet. They were not asking for the protected recipient's identity, the originating family's name, or any information that might reveal a private relationship.

They wanted permission to inspect one attachment.

**F-02/SOURCE/01.**

The request asked for three things:

1. Confirmation that the attachment contained an original authority instrument.
2. Verification of the person or institution that had authorised the family communication.
3. Confirmation of the precise scope of that authority.

Any personal identity information was to remain sealed unless its disclosure was separately authorised.

Eleanor placed the request on the table.

“Even if they approve this, we may learn nothing about the recipient.”

“That is the point,” Edward replied.

She looked at him.

“You're becoming more careful.”

“I've become tired of discovering that somebody used a limited permission as if it were unlimited.”

“That is not the same thing.”

“No,” he admitted. “It isn't.”

The custodian had already confirmed that the attachment remained sealed in the Restricted Records Custodianship Unit's archive.

It had never been opened.

There was no indication that anyone had examined its contents since deposit.

That fact mattered.

If the attachment contained the original authority for F-02, then its preservation might establish what the originating family representative had actually been permitted to do.

It would not establish who the recipient was.

It would not establish whether the recipient was a child.

And it would certainly not establish that the recipient was connected to either the Aurelius or Valmont family.

Edward had learned not to let a useful document answer questions it had never been designed to answer.

At 10:36, Eleanor received an acknowledgement.

The originating authority had been contacted through the original restricted correspondence route.

No direct identity disclosure was included in the acknowledgement.

The response was expected within two working days.

Edward read the final sentence twice.

**The originating authority has been asked to confirm whether the attached source instrument may be inspected by an independent preservation examiner.**

“An independent examiner,” he said.

Eleanor nodded.

“Not us.”

“Good.”

“Why does that please you?”

“Because if we inspect it ourselves, someone can later argue that we interpreted it to fit our investigation.”

Eleanor closed the file.

“And if an independent examiner confirms its contents?”

“Then we will know what the document says. Not what we want it to say.”

Across the city, Elena was having a considerably less complicated morning.

Or so she thought.

Her laptop displayed the latest version of the grant platform's reviewer dashboard.

Three applications had passed the simulated review stage. One had been returned for missing documentation. Another had been marked as received but not authenticated.

The distinction had caused a disagreement during yesterday's demonstration.

One reviewer had argued that a document should be considered verified once it appeared in the system.

Elena had disagreed.

“A document appearing in a system only proves that the system received something,” she explained now to the programme coordinator, Ms. Tan. “It doesn't prove the document is genuine.”

Ms. Tan leaned back in her chair.

“And what happens if the reviewer is satisfied with the document?”

“They can record their assessment. But the system shouldn't turn that into an institutional decision unless the reviewer has the authority to make one.”

“Even if the reviewer is senior?”

“Especially then.”

Ms. Tan smiled faintly.

“You've become rather strict about authority.”

Elena looked at her screen.

“I've become strict about not pretending that one person can do five different jobs just because nobody wants to find the right person.”

The coordinator laughed.

“That may be the most practical thing you've said all week.”

Elena returned to the dashboard.

The next stage was not glamorous.

She needed to test reviewer permissions, confirm the evidence-transfer process, and document what happened when an authorised reviewer disagreed with another authorised reviewer.

There would be no automated final decisions.

There would be no hidden override that allowed a senior user to quietly change a completed assessment.

Every action would leave a record.

She had initially thought those requirements were excessive.

Now she was beginning to understand why they mattered.

At the Valmont residence, Isabelle was reviewing the foundation's existing grant procedures.

She had received Elena's preliminary technical note the previous evening.

It was short, precise, and irritatingly difficult to dismiss.

Isabelle had expected a presentation full of polished language about innovation.

Instead, Elena had listed the foundation's current weaknesses.

- Inconsistent evidence-receipt records.
- Reviewer access not always separated from decision authority.
- Unclear responsibility for confirming supporting documents.
- Limited visibility into post-award delivery.
- No consistent record of who accepted a correction and when.

Isabelle read the final paragraph again.

**The platform should support the foundation's existing governance structure, not silently replace it.**

She put the document down.

Catherine entered without knocking.

“Have you finished reviewing it?”

“Almost.”

“And?”

“It identifies problems we already know about.”

Catherine sat opposite her.

“Is that a criticism?”

“No. It's an observation.”

Isabelle turned the document around.

“The system could be useful. But we would need to decide whether the foundation is ready to change its procedures.”

“Can you arrange that discussion?”

“I can arrange a technical meeting. I cannot promise that the trustees will agree to anything.”

“Nor should you.”

Isabelle looked at her mother.

Catherine continued.

“Do not turn Elena's work into a family obligation. If the foundation wants to use it, it must approach her properly.”

“I know.”

“And she must be free to refuse.”

Isabelle nodded.

There was a pause.

“Have you spoken to her?” Catherine asked.

“Only about the meeting.”

“Was she comfortable?”

“She said she would attend, provided the discussion remained about the platform.”

Catherine smiled slightly.

“That sounds like Elena.”

Isabelle returned to the document.

She was beginning to recognise something uncomfortable.

Elena had not built the platform to prove that the Valmonts had underestimated her.

She had built it because she had identified a problem and wanted to solve it.

That made the work harder to claim.

And, strangely, easier to respect.

At 1:18 p.m., Edward received a call from the archive custodian.

“The originating authority has responded.”

Edward sat upright.

“What did they approve?”

“Inspection of the attachment by an independent preservation examiner.”

“Any conditions?”

“Yes. The examiner may confirm the existence, date, issuing authority, and scope of the instrument. The identity of the protected recipient must remain sealed.”

“And the identity of the originating family?”

“Also sealed.”

“May the examiner disclose whether the authority was valid?”

“The examiner may confirm whether the instrument supports the submission of F-02. They may not determine the recipient's identity or any family relationship.”

Edward glanced at Eleanor.

“That is sufficient.”

The custodian continued.

“There is one additional condition. The examiner must not compare the attachment with any other restricted identity material without separate authorisation.”

Edward frowned.

“Why?”

“The originating authority has expressly limited the inspection to the authority instrument itself.”

He understood.

The restriction prevented the examination from becoming an informal identity investigation.

“Who will conduct it?”

“Dr. Miriam Cole, independent preservation examiner. She has no recorded involvement in the original communication or receiving-route administration.”

“Has she accepted?”

“She has.”

Edward thanked the custodian and ended the call.

Eleanor was already making notes.

“An independent examination,” she said. “Limited scope. No recipient disclosure.”

“And no comparison with other records.”

“Then we wait for her report.”

Edward looked at the sealed attachment reference on his screen.

“For the first time, we may be able to establish who had the right to initiate the communication.”

Eleanor corrected him gently.

“Who had the authority to submit the request. We still don't know whether that authority was exercised correctly.”

Edward gave a small nod.

“You're right.”

Dr. Cole arrived at the archive the following morning.

She was in her late fifties, carried a slim leather folder, and spoke with the measured patience of someone accustomed to documents being treated as more important than the people who had created them.

She reviewed the preservation conditions before accepting the sealed attachment.

The custodian watched as she checked the seal number against the original deposit record.

“Intact,” she said.

The attachment was placed on a separate examination table.

No other R-17 material was brought into the room.

Dr. Cole examined the exterior first.

The envelope carried a handwritten source description and a date.

There was also a small notation beneath the original deposit number.

**Authority instrument — retain with initiating request.**

She looked at the custodian.

“Was this notation present at the time of deposit?”

“Yes. It appears in the original intake photograph.”

“Who wrote it?”

“Not established.”

Dr. Cole recorded the answer.

Then she opened the attachment under the approved procedure.

Inside was a folded document, two pages long.

The first page carried a formal declaration.

The second contained the signature and witness section.

Dr. Cole did not immediately read the names.

She checked the document's physical condition, paper characteristics, staple marks, ink consistency, and the relationship between the two pages.

Then she examined the declaration.

Her expression changed slightly.

“Interesting.”

The custodian looked up.

“What is it?”

“This is not an appointment instrument.”

“What is it?”

“A limited authorisation to initiate contact.”

She continued reading.

The document stated that a recognised family authority could submit a request for direct personal communication with a protected individual.

The authority was restricted to:

- Preparing and submitting the initial communication request.
- Providing the originating family's declared reason for contact.
- Authorising preservation of the request and its supporting materials.
- Receiving confirmation that the request had entered the approved process.

The document did not permit the family authority to receive the protected person's response.

It did not permit the family authority to decide whether contact should occur.

It did not permit the family authority to appoint a receiving representative.

It did not permit the family authority to determine the protected person's identity or relationship to the family.

Dr. Cole read the final paragraph.

Then she turned to the second page.

There were two signatures.

The first belonged to the authorised family representative.

The second belonged to a witness.

The witness's role was described in unusually precise language.

**The witness confirms that the authority declaration was executed in the presence of the undersigned and that the signatory was acting within the stated family mandate. The witness does not certify the identity of the protected recipient or the truth of any claimed biological relationship.**

Dr. Cole stopped.

She read it again.

“That's unusually careful.”

The custodian said nothing.

“Whoever prepared this document understood the distinction between authority to communicate and authority to determine identity.”

She checked the date.

It had been executed two days before the family communication request was submitted.

The date was consistent with the F-02 chronology.

She examined the signatures.

The document contained an original signature rather than a reproduced image.

The witness's signature was also original.

But the witness's name was not the only identifying information on the page.

Beneath it was an institutional designation.

**Independent Family Authority Witness — St Catherine's Records Liaison.**

Dr. Cole looked at the custodian.

“This designation needs careful treatment.”

“Why?”

“It identifies the witness's function at the time of execution. It does not establish that the person had authority over the hospital's maternity or neonatal records.”

She returned to the document.

“The witness was confirming the execution of a family mandate. Nothing more.”

She completed the examination.

Edward and Eleanor received the report at 3:42 p.m.

Edward read it without speaking.

Eleanor watched his expression.

“What?”

He passed her the first page.

“The family communication was not submitted without authority.”

She scanned the findings.

The attachment was an original instrument.

Its date preceded F-02/SUB-01.

The family representative had been authorised to initiate direct personal communication.

The authority did not extend to receiving a response, accepting a placement, appointing a receiving representative, or making decisions on behalf of the protected recipient.

The witness had confirmed execution of the mandate, not biological identity.

Eleanor lowered the report.

“So the original family had a legitimate right to ask for contact.”

“Yes.”

“But nobody had the right to treat that request as permission to decide where the recipient belonged.”

“Correct.”

She returned to the second page.

“The witness was connected to St Catherine's.”

“Through a records liaison designation.”

“Do we know who?”

“Not yet. The examiner was not authorised to disclose the witness's identity beyond the permitted report.”

Eleanor considered the distinction.

“The witness may have known the family authority. They may have known the request was genuine. But that doesn't mean they knew who the protected recipient was.”

“Exactly.”

Edward opened the original F-02 submission.

A sentence in the request caught his attention.

**The originating family requests that the recipient be approached through a direct and independently verified channel. No approach is authorised through family office personnel, household staff, or informal personal familiarity.**

He placed the new report beside it.

The restriction had not been invented by an administrator.

It was consistent with the original family's own authority declaration.

Someone had deliberately limited the route of communication.

Someone had wanted the protected recipient to be reached personally, without allowing the family itself to control the response.

And the system had failed to deliver the request.

Eleanor looked at him.

“Does this change anything?”

“It changes one thing.”

“What?”

“We no longer have to ask whether the originating family had authority to submit F-02.”

He turned the report around.

“We have to ask why an authorised request was never delivered, even though its limits were clear from the beginning.”

That evening, Edward received a separate message from Dr. Cole.

It was not part of the formal report.

She had noticed something during the examination and wanted to ensure it was not overlooked.

**The witness designation on the authority instrument appears to have been used as a functional title rather than a personal job title. The individual may have been authorised to witness family mandates without holding general records authority.**

Edward read the message twice.

A functional title.

Not a hospital officer with broad authority.

Not an identity examiner.

Not someone entitled to compare infants or amend maternity records.

A person trusted to witness a family declaration.

He forwarded the message to Eleanor.

Then he opened the historical St Catherine's liaison register.

The register had not previously been included in the identity investigation because it was not an infant record.

It was an institutional staffing and delegation record.

Edward searched the relevant period.

Three names appeared.

Two had already been ruled out because their recorded duties did not include witnessing family declarations.

The third entry was incomplete.

**Temporary liaison appointment — Family Authority Witness.**

The appointment had begun shortly before F-02 was executed.

The name was partially obscured by an old correction strip.

Edward enlarged the scanned page.

Beneath the strip, the first name was visible.

The surname was not.

He stared at the screen.

Then he noticed the reference in the margin.

It was not an employee number.

It was a cross-reference to a separate appointment file.

**FAW/06 — Witness Authority and Delegation.**

Eleanor leaned over his shoulder.

“Is that the original appointment?”

“Possibly.”

“Does it identify the witness?”

“It should identify the person who was authorised to sign that declaration.”

He opened the request form.

The file had been transferred out of St Catherine's records administration years earlier.

Its destination was listed as:

**Meridian — Restricted Governance Archive.**

Edward sat back.

The family authority had been established.

The witness's function had been established.

But the identity of the witness—and the limits of the witness's appointment—remained unconfirmed.

He closed the register.

“Now we know where to look.”

And for the first time, the investigation had found a document that could explain not merely how the family request was created, but who had been trusted to certify that it was genuine.
