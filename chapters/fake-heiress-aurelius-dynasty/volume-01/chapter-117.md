# **Chapter 117 — The Trust With No Beneficiary**

Dr. Samuel Hale had established two trusts.

Edward had known about the first one for weeks.

The R-04 trust existed to preserve documents concerning children whose identities had not been reconciled. Its beneficiary was deliberately left unassigned. Its funds were intended to maintain the records until the required authorities could complete the verification process.

The second trust was different.

It had been created on day 18, before the alternate recipient schedule, before the independent review, and before Susan Bell refused the proposed allocation.

Its name was the Northbridge Continuity Trust.

And it had no named beneficiary either.

Edward placed the two trust records side by side.

“Why would one person establish two separate trusts for the same unresolved arrangements?”

Jonathan stood opposite him.

“Possibly because they served different purposes.”

“Which were?”

“The R-04 trust preserved the records. The Northbridge trust may have preserved the practical arrangements.”

Edward looked at the documents again.

“That's an assumption.”

“It's a working possibility.”

Edward nodded.

“Then let's find out what the second trust actually did.”

The successor records custodian had agreed to release the Northbridge Continuity Trust file for inspection under the same preservation conditions that applied to the R-04 archive.

The file arrived in a sealed document case.

Edward and Jonathan examined it in the presence of a records officer.

The first document was the original trust deed.

**NORTHBRIDGE CONTINUITY TRUST**

**ESTABLISHED: DAY 18**

**SETTLOR: DR. SAMUEL HALE**

**ADMINISTRATOR: MERIDIAN TRUST SERVICES**

**BENEFICIARY: TO BE DESIGNATED UPON COMPLETION OF RECEIVING IDENTITY VERIFICATION**

Edward read the last line.

“Another unassigned beneficiary.”

Jonathan nodded.

The deed described the trust's purpose.

It was established to preserve resources for the continued care and administration of children whose originating identities had been recorded but whose receiving arrangements had not been independently verified.

The trust could cover:

- Temporary medical and residential expenses.
- Independent records reconciliation.
- Administrative costs associated with lawful family notification.
- Transfer expenses after receiving authority had been appointed.
- Necessary support for a child whose temporary care arrangement had ended.

It could not:

- Determine biological identity.
- Appoint a receiving family.
- Authorise permanent placement.
- Override hospital custody requirements.
- Release a child without the required verification.

Edward turned the page.

“It's a funding mechanism.”

“More than that,” Jonathan said. “It appears to have been designed to keep the temporary arrangements functioning without treating them as permanent.”

“Which would explain why Hale established it.”

Jonathan pointed to the funding provisions.

“The trust could pay for temporary care even if no family had been identified.”

Edward read the section again.

It was a sensible arrangement.

Children needed food, accommodation, medical care, and supervision regardless of whether legal records had been completed.

But the existence of the trust also raised a question.

Who had been paying for the arrangements before it was created?

And why had Hale felt the need to establish a separate financial structure on day 18?

“Check the funding schedule,” Edward said.

The records officer opened the next section.

There were four initial deposits.

Two came from Dr. Hale's personal account.

One came from Meridian Trust Services.

The fourth came through Vale Private Counsel.

Edward looked at the final entry.

“Who authorised that payment?”

“The remittance instruction carries the code VPC-PI.”

“Private Instructions Division.”

“Yes.”

“Does it identify Charles Vale?”

“No individual name.”

Edward made a note.

The money had come from several sources.

But the trust's restrictions were clear.

No permanent beneficiary could be entered until the receiving identity had been verified.

The same condition appeared in the R-04 trust.

The same distinction appeared in Eleanor's instructions.

And the same principle had been repeatedly ignored in later administrative summaries.

The next section contained payment records.

Jonathan read through them.

“Day 22. Temporary medical expenses.”

“Which facility?” Edward asked.

“Northbridge Clinical Review Unit.”

“Child reference?”

“28-B.”

Edward leaned closer.

The first payment covered medical care for Child 28-B.

The second covered temporary accommodation.

The third covered administrative transport.

The fourth was a payment to a private residential provider.

Edward read the details.

“Was the child physically transferred under the trust?”

“No. The trust paid expenses associated with the placement. It did not hold custody.”

“Who was the receiving representative?”

“The payment record doesn't identify one.”

Edward continued reading.

There were payments associated with other references.

STC-A.

IV-204.

M-204.

Several entries were marked as restricted.

Some payments covered medical records retrieval.

Others covered temporary accommodation and transport.

But none of the records established that the infants were the same child.

Edward closed the ledger.

“The trust supported several arrangements.”

Jonathan nodded.

“Possibly separate children.”

“Possibly. We must not combine them because they share a funding source.”

The records officer placed a supplementary schedule before them.

It contained a list of cost centres.

**CHILD 28-B — TEMPORARY CARE**

**STC-A — MEDICAL RECORDS PRESERVATION**

**IV-204 — ORIGINATING MATERNAL RECORDS**

**M-204 — IDENTIFICATION MATERIAL CUSTODY**

**SCHEDULE 4 — ADMINISTRATIVE PRESERVATION**

Edward looked at the final entry.

“Schedule 4 was funded through the trust?”

“Partially.”

“Did the trust pay for the family allocation process?”

“The schedule says administrative preservation and document handling.”

“Not appointment?”

“No.”

Edward nodded.

Again, the same boundary.

Money could support temporary care and record preservation.

It could not create a family relationship.

The trust's correspondence file contained a letter from Dr. Hale.

It was dated day 18.

Edward recognised the handwriting.

**The current arrangements are not suitable for indefinite continuation. Temporary care must be adequately funded while the originating and receiving records are reconciled. No payment from this trust shall be treated as evidence of family acceptance or identity determination.**

The letter continued.

**The trust shall not be used to compel a receiving representative to accept a child, nor shall a proposed recipient be described as a permanent beneficiary before verification is complete.**

Edward read the passage aloud.

Jonathan looked at him.

“Hale was explicit.”

“Yes.”

“Then why did the alternate recipient proposal involve a conditional consent?”

“Because the trust allowed future support for a proposed receiving household, provided the necessary verification was completed.”

Edward turned the page.

There was a schedule attached.

It described how a proposed recipient could be considered.

The process had five stages.

1. A preliminary recipient profile could be prepared.
2. The proposed recipient could express willingness to be considered.
3. An independent reviewer would examine the profile and consent.
4. A receiving identity custodian would verify the receiving arrangement.
5. The trust administrator could designate the beneficiary only after all required approvals.

Edward pointed to the second stage.

“That's where the conditional consent fits.”

Jonathan nodded.

“But the third stage was never completed.”

“Dr. Shaw completed a limited review and refused to recommend appointment.”

“And nobody reached stage four.”

“Correct.”

Edward looked at the final stage.

The beneficiary could only be designated after verification.

The process was not vague.

It was carefully structured.

Yet someone had later attempted to notify an alternate recipient and obtain an acknowledgement without the necessary authority.

“Who drafted this procedure?” Jonathan asked.

Edward checked the signature block.

“Dr. Hale.”

“Did anyone approve it?”

“Charles Vale acknowledged receipt.”

“Did he sign?”

“No. His office stamp appears on the correspondence copy.”

Edward continued reading.

There was a note at the bottom.

**Any departure from this process requires written approval from the trust administrator and the originating representative.**

Edward stopped.

“Who was the originating representative for Child 28-B?”

Jonathan checked the old designation schedule.

“That's the problem. The originating representative field was never completed.”

Edward looked up.

“Then nobody could approve a departure.”

“Not under this procedure.”

Edward placed the schedule beside the day-120 dispatch instruction.

The later instruction had attempted to contact an alternate recipient without an appointed receiving authority.

It had also sought an acknowledgement that might allow the private instruction to be released.

But the trust procedure required both the receiving identity custodian and originating representative.

Neither had been properly established.

The process had been designed to stop exactly that.

There was another document inside the trust file.

It was an internal note from Meridian Trust Services, dated day 35.

**Request received to identify provisional beneficiary for Child 28-B.**

**Request declined. Beneficiary designation cannot precede originating and receiving verification.**

The request had come from Vale Private Counsel.

Edward examined the attached correspondence.

The proposed beneficiary designation had been left blank.

Beside it, someone had written:

**Proposed recipient profile available. Conditional consent received.**

The note was signed by an administrative officer at Meridian.

Edward looked at Jonathan.

“Someone tried to designate the beneficiary on day 35.”

Jonathan nodded slowly.

“Before Dr. Shaw completed her review?”

“Yes.”

“And Meridian refused.”

“Correct.”

The refusal had been filed with the trust records.

But the administrative summary issued on day 47 described the R-12 review as completed and the alternate recipient as retained for continuity.

The two documents did not contradict each other exactly.

One refused a beneficiary designation.

The other preserved the proposal.

But if someone later read only the administrative summary, they might believe the recipient had passed the necessary review.

Edward looked at the refusal.

“Who made the day-35 request?”

The records officer turned to the attached authorisation.

There was a typed sender designation.

**CHARLES VALE — PERSONAL INSTRUCTIONS DIVISION.**

No individual signature.

Edward sighed.

“His office again.”

Jonathan leaned forward.

“Could he have been trying to create a temporary beneficiary rather than a permanent one?”

“Possibly.”

“But the trust deed didn't allow that.”

“No.”

“Then why submit the request?”

Edward looked at the blank beneficiary field.

“Because someone wanted the trust to recognise a recipient before the verification was complete.”

Dr. Hale agreed to meet them the following morning.

He appeared older than Edward remembered, but his manner remained composed.

When Edward placed the Northbridge trust deed on the table, Hale did not look surprised.

“You found it.”

“Yes.”

“I wondered how long that would take.”

“Why did you establish two trusts?”

Hale looked at the documents.

“Because the records and the children required different forms of protection.”

“Explain.”

“The R-04 trust preserved the documentation. It ensured that the original records could not disappear simply because the relevant offices closed or changed ownership.”

“And Northbridge?”

“Northbridge Continuity was intended to fund temporary arrangements. Medical care, accommodation, transport, independent verification. Things that could not wait for an administrative dispute to end.”

“Why did you establish it on day 18?”

Hale folded his hands.

“By then, it was clear that the existing arrangements were becoming unstable. There were children in temporary care, incomplete maternal records, and no properly appointed receiving representatives.”

“Did you know how many children were involved?”

“I knew the administrative references. I did not know whether every reference represented a separate child.”

“Did you know whether Child 28-B was connected to either maternal identity?”

“No.”

“Did you know who the alternate recipient was?”

Hale hesitated.

“I knew a proposal had been prepared.”

“Did you know the person's name?”

“I was not given the name.”

“Why not?”

“Because I was the trust administrator, not the receiving identity custodian.”

Edward placed the day-35 beneficiary request before him.

“Your trust refused this request.”

“Yes.”

“Why did Charles Vale submit it?”

“He believed a provisional beneficiary would help maintain continuity.”

“Did you agree?”

“No.”

“Did he accept your refusal?”

Hale looked down.

“He accepted that I would not designate a beneficiary.”

“Did he attempt to persuade you?”

“Yes.”

“What did he say?”

“That the person had already agreed to be considered. He argued that the trust could record the proposed recipient without treating the entry as final.”

“And your answer?”

“That a beneficiary field is not a place to record a possibility.”

Edward nodded.

“Did he ask you to enter the person under a temporary designation?”

“Yes.”

“Did you?”

“No.”

“Why not?”

“Because the trust deed did not permit it.”

Hale's answer was firm.

Edward placed the day-120 dispatch instruction before him.

“Did you know someone later attempted to notify the alternate recipient?”

Hale examined the reference.

“No.”

“Did you authorise it?”

“No.”

“Did you know Private Instruction 07 was being held separately?”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“Because it contained instructions concerning a proposed receiving arrangement. I was told it could not be opened until the receiving authority and originating representative were present together.”

“Who told you?”

“Charles Vale.”

Edward watched him.

“Did you agree with that restriction?”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“Because it was the only way to prevent the proposed recipient from being treated as a confirmed family before the records were reconciled.”

Edward leaned forward.

“Then why did you permit the Northbridge trust to receive conditional consent from an unidentified recipient?”

Hale's expression changed.

“I did not.”

“The trust file contains an envelope addressed through R-12.”

“That does not mean I received the consent.”

“It was sent to a trust administered by you.”

“Through a correspondence route. That is not the same as my personal receipt.”

Edward looked at Jonathan.

The distinction mattered.

“Do you have a record of who received it?”

Hale shook his head.

“No. The trust's correspondence office handled incoming documents. I did not open the sealed schedule.”

“Did you know the proposed recipient had agreed to be considered?”

“I was told that a conditional consent existed.”

“By whom?”

“Charles.”

“Did you see it?”

“No.”

“Did you verify it?”

“No.”

Edward placed the Dr. Shaw report on the table.

“She found the consent insufficient.”

Hale read the conclusion.

“Then she was right.”

“Did you know she had refused to recommend appointment?”

“I knew her review was incomplete.”

“Did you receive the day-47 administrative summary?”

Hale nodded.

“Yes.”

“Did you consider the review completed?”

“No.”

Edward watched him carefully.

“Why?”

“Because the summary was an administrative status, not a verification report.”

“Did you object?”

“Yes.”

“Where is the objection?”

Hale paused.

“I believe it was sent to Vale Private Counsel.”

“Do you have a copy?”

“I may.”

He turned toward a cabinet and searched through several folders.

After a few minutes, he removed a letter.

It was dated day 48.

**The R-12 preliminary review cannot be recorded as completed for the purpose of receiving identity designation. Dr. Shaw's findings remain unresolved. No beneficiary has been appointed. No recipient consent has been verified by the trust administrator.**

The letter was signed by Samuel Hale.

Edward read it twice.

“Was there a response?”

Hale shook his head.

“Not directly.”

“Did the trust continue to make payments?”

“Yes. Temporary care and preservation expenses continued.”

“Did any payment go to the proposed recipient?”

“No.”

“Did the trust ever designate a beneficiary?”

“No.”

Edward looked at the empty field in the deed.

“Then the trust never recognised the proposed recipient.”

“Correct.”

“And the alternate proposal remained unapproved.”

“Yes.”

For the first time, Edward felt that one part of the investigation had become clearer.

The proposed recipient had not been appointed.

The conditional consent had not been verified.

The trust had refused to designate a beneficiary.

And Dr. Hale had objected when the review was incorrectly recorded as completed.

But someone had continued trying to make the arrangement appear more advanced than it was.

Before leaving, Edward asked Hale one final question.

“Why did you continue working with Charles Vale after these disagreements?”

Hale was silent for a moment.

“Because there were children who needed care.”

“That doesn't explain why you trusted him.”

“I didn't always trust his decisions.”

“Then why cooperate?”

“Because refusing to cooperate would not have helped the children.”

Edward studied him.

Hale continued.

“I could refuse a false beneficiary designation. I could preserve the records. I could decline to certify an identity I could not establish. But I could not simply stop paying for medical care because Charles and I disagreed.”

“And you believed he was trying to protect the children?”

“I believed he was trying to keep the arrangements functioning.”

“Did you believe he was trying to protect the families?”

Hale looked at him.

“I don't know.”

The answer was honest.

And it was not enough.

That evening, Elena's departure arrangements were finalised.

Her suitcase stood near the entrance.

The smaller bag was beside it.

The programme documents were in her hand luggage.

Catherine had checked the travel itinerary twice.

Sebastian had confirmed the airport transfer.

Nicholas had promised to call after she landed.

Julian had already sent another reminder.

Elena stood in the sitting room, looking around.

She had spent eighteen years in the Valmont household.

Even after the DNA result, even after the arrival of Isabelle, even after the changes that had made every room feel slightly different, the house still contained pieces of her life.

The bookshelf in the corner.

The framed photograph from her school graduation.

The small mark on the wall where she had once tried to measure her height.

She touched the edge of the photograph.

Catherine approached quietly.

“Everything ready?”

“Yes.”

“Are you certain you haven't forgotten anything?”

Elena smiled.

“If I have, I'll buy it there.”

Catherine nodded.

Then she looked at the suitcase.

“I know you've wanted this opportunity for a long time.”

“I have.”

“I'm glad you accepted it.”

Elena looked at her.

“Thank you.”

Catherine hesitated.

“Call me when you arrive.”

“I will.”

“And don't feel that you have to come back early if things become difficult. Give yourself time to adjust.”

Elena smiled faintly.

“I'll try.”

Catherine reached out and straightened the collar of Elena's jacket.

For a moment, neither spoke.

Then Catherine said, “I'm proud of you.”

Elena's expression softened.

She had heard those words before.

But they still mattered.

“Thank you, Mom.”

Catherine's eyes briefly filled.

She did not correct the word.

Later, when the house had grown quiet, Elena placed Julian's notebook inside her hand luggage.

She opened it once more.

The final reminder was still there.

**Come home whenever you want.**

She smiled.

Then she closed the notebook.

The following morning, she would leave for Singapore.

It was not an escape.

It was not a rejection of her past.

It was a decision to move forward.

Edward returned to the study and reviewed the Northbridge trust records one more time.

The trust had preserved temporary care arrangements.

It had funded medical expenses.

It had supported records reconciliation.

It had refused to designate a beneficiary.

It had not approved the alternate recipient.

But the day-18 deed contained a clause Edward had not examined closely enough.

He opened the original again.

The clause concerned the release of funds after a receiving arrangement had been completed.

**Where a proposed recipient has provided conditional consent but the receiving authority has not been appointed, the trust may retain funds for future transfer. No funds may be released directly to the proposed recipient or household without written confirmation of appointment.**

Edward turned to the payment ledger.

There were no direct payments to the proposed recipient.

But there was one payment he had not yet examined.

Day 34.

A transfer to an account listed under:

**RECIPIENT PREPARATION EXPENSES — AV-18.**

The amount was not large.

But the payment description included an additional reference.

**DOCUMENT PREPARATION AND PERSONAL ADMINISTRATION.**

Edward called Jonathan.

“Did you see a payment under AV-18?”

“Yes. I was about to ask you about it.”

“Who received it?”

“The payment went to an administrative service provider.”

“Which one?”

Jonathan read the name.

“Cross Administrative Services.”

Edward stared at the page.

Marianne Cross's former administrative company.

The same organisation that had handled restricted notifications.

The same organisation whose records had preserved several of the missing acknowledgements.

“Was the payment made to Marianne personally?”

“No. It went to the company.”

“Who authorised it?”

“Vale Private Counsel.”

Edward looked at the amount.

Then at the date.

Day 34.

The day the alternate recipient schedule was prepared.

He opened the transaction attachment.

It contained a description of the work.

**Preparation of recipient correspondence package.**

**Preparation of conditional acknowledgement form.**

**Preparation of alternate notification materials.**

Edward read the final line again.

“Jonathan.”

“Yes?”

“Someone paid for the preparation of notification materials on the same day the alternate schedule was created.”

Jonathan was silent.

“And Dr. Shaw's report later said no notification was authorised.”

“Correct.”

Edward looked at the payment record.

The money did not prove that anyone had been notified.

It did prove that someone had prepared the materials.

And now he had a reason to examine Cross Administrative Services more closely.

He wrote one final note beneath the payment reference.

**AV-18 — IDENTIFY DOCUMENTS PREPARED, RECIPIENT, AND DISPATCH STATUS.**

Then he closed the trust file.

The alternate recipient remained unknown.

The consent remained sealed.

And the money had already been spent preparing a notification that the verification process had never authorised.
