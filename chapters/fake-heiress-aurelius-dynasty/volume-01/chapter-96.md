# **Chapter 96 — The Notification That Should Never Have Been Sent**

Eleanor Vale did not answer Edward’s question immediately.

She looked at the note he had placed on the table.

**SCHEDULE 4 MUST REMAIN SEPARATE UNTIL RECEIVING AUTHORITY IS CORRECTLY APPOINTED. NO FAMILY NOTIFICATION PERMITTED ON BASIS OF THIS SCHEDULE.**

Her initials appeared beneath it.

E.V.

“I wrote that after the review,” she said at last.

Edward leaned forward. “What did you find?”

“Not what you think.”

“I’m not sure what I think.”

“Good. Keep it that way.”

Jonathan opened his notebook. “You reviewed the seal on day one hundred and eighteen. The schedule had been stored since day sixty-one. Why did you intervene then?”

Eleanor’s gaze moved to him. “Because someone had started using it.”

“Using it how?”

“To tell a family that a child had been allocated to them.”

Edward felt the words settle between them.

“Was the allocation correct?”

“I had no way to know.”

“Who was the family?”

“I don’t know.”

“You don’t know, or you won’t tell us?”

Eleanor’s expression tightened. “I don’t know. The copy I saw did not name them.”

Edward sat back, frustrated by the familiar shape of the answer. Another document had been circulated. Another person had been told something. And the person who might have explained it had been kept outside the part of the record that mattered.

“What did the copy say?” he asked.

“Only that a proposed family arrangement had been entered in Schedule 4, and that the receiving family had been notified.”

“Had they?”

“That was the claim.”

“Did you verify it?”

“I tried.”

Eleanor opened a slim folder she had brought with her. She removed a sheet protected by a transparent sleeve and turned it toward them.

It was a day-118 review record. The printed section at the top identified the material under examination: **Schedule 4 — Current Care Arrangements**. Beneath it, a handwritten note described a discrepancy between the sealed archive copy and a notification receipt.

The receipt itself was not attached.

Edward read the last line twice.

**Recipient identity omitted from retained copy. Notification status recorded as complete. Verification status unresolved.**

“Who made this review record?” he asked.

“I did.”

“And the receipt?”

“I saw it in the file. I was not permitted to retain it.”

“Who had it?”

“The person who requested the review.”

“Name?”

“Not on the copy I was given.”

Edward exhaled through his nose. “You’re asking us to investigate a notification without telling us who sent it, who received it, or what the notification actually said.”

“I’m telling you what I can prove.”

“You could have made a copy.”

“I could have broken the preservation conditions, compromised the archive, and given someone a reason to argue that the record was improperly obtained.” Eleanor folded her hands. “I was already trying to stop an unverified schedule from being treated as a final allocation. I wasn’t going to create another procedural dispute by taking documents I had no authority to remove.”

Jonathan glanced at Edward, then returned to the page.

“Did the receipt refer to a child by name?”

“No. It referred to a proposed arrangement.”

“Then how could a family know which child it meant?”

“That was the question I asked.”

“And?”

“No answer.”

Edward studied the review record. It was spare, almost aggressively so. No explanation, no names, no conclusion beyond the unresolved status.

“Did you know the schedule had been assembled from two different sheets?”

“Yes.”

“Did you know Richard Valmont’s signature had been attached to the revised allocation page?”

“I learned that during the review.”

“Did you know the signature was genuine?”

“I was told it was genuine. I was also told it had been moved from a different page.”

“So the signature was real, but the approval wasn’t.”

“Correct.”

“And someone used the schedule to notify a family anyway.”

“Someone claimed a family had been notified.”

Eleanor’s correction was quiet, but firm.

Edward looked at the handwritten line again. “You’re careful to distinguish those.”

“I have to be.”

“Because you don’t know whether the notification happened?”

“Because I don’t know what happened after the receipt was created. It might have been delivered. It might have been returned. It might have been acknowledged by an intermediary who had no authority to receive it. The record says ‘complete,’ but the verification field was still unresolved.”

Jonathan tapped the page with his pen. “That resembles the earlier R-04 acknowledgement. Marianne Cross said she delivered a notification, but the receipt said identity confirmation was outstanding.”

Eleanor’s eyes lifted.

“You’ve spoken to Marianne?”

“Yes.”

“Did she tell you who she delivered it to?”

“She said Charles Vale concealed the recipient’s name.”

Eleanor looked down again. “Then she was given the same kind of incomplete instruction.”

“Was this the same notification?”

“I can’t confirm that.”

“But you suspect it.”

“I suspect that several people were using the same incomplete process as though it were a completed one.”

The answer was broader than Edward wanted. It was also the first thing Eleanor had said that made the scattered discrepancies feel less like isolated mistakes.

He turned to Jonathan. “The day-118 review. Who requested it?”

“The request was routed through Unit 6,” Eleanor said. “The originating authority was not identified on my copy.”

“Was it Charles Vale?”

“I don’t know.”

“Adrian Holt?”

“I don’t know.”

“Dr Hale?”

“I don’t know.”

“Then what did you know?”

“That the sealed archive contained three separate items: Annex C Part One, Annex C Part Two, and Schedule 4. The schedule had been kept separate because it was not a verified identity record. At some point, a copy had been used to support a notification. I wrote that no family notification could be based on it.”

Edward leaned back. The frustration remained, but it had shifted. Eleanor was not withholding a clear answer. She was describing a system that had repeatedly recorded certainty where none existed.

“Did your note stop the notification?”

“No.”

“Did it stop further notifications?”

“I don’t know.”

“Did it stop someone from treating the schedule as proof?”

“Not everywhere.”

The room went still.

Eleanor reached into her folder again. This time she removed a narrow strip of paper, yellowed at the edges and marked with a reference number.

“This was attached to the review request,” she said. “It was not part of Schedule 4.”

Edward took it carefully.

The strip contained a brief instruction, typed in the same formal style as the other records.

**Do not amend the current care arrangement until the receiving party acknowledges the proposed allocation.**

Below it, in handwriting, someone had added:

**Acknowledgement is not identity confirmation.**

No signature.

No date.

Edward read it once, then again.

“Who wrote this?”

“I don’t know.”

“Was it written before or after the notification?”

“The paper was attached to the request. The request was made after the notification receipt was created. That’s all I can establish.”

“Could the handwriting be Charles Vale’s?”

“I don’t know.”

“Marianne Cross’s?”

“I don’t know.”

“Eleanor—”

“I don’t know, Edward.”

She did not raise her voice. That made the interruption sharper.

“I understand what you need,” she continued. “You want a name. You want a person who made the decision. But the records were designed so that each person could say they had only handled one part. One person prepared the schedule. Another verified medical material. Another held the receiving records. Someone else was supposed to notify the family. No one was meant to complete the process alone.”

“And yet someone did.”

“Someone acted as though they had.”

Edward looked at the handwritten sentence.

**Acknowledgement is not identity confirmation.**

It was the same distinction that had appeared again and again: a signature did not establish what it had been attached to; a band did not establish which infant had worn it; a receipt did not establish that the correct person had received a child; a proposed allocation did not establish a family.

The records were full of things that had been treated as proof because they looked like proof.

“Did you ever see the original Schedule 4?” he asked.

“Yes.”

“Before it was sealed?”

“No. I saw it during the day-118 review.”

“Then how do you know the copy used for the notification was a copy of that schedule?”

“The reference number matched. The wording matched the surviving description. But I did not compare the pages myself.”

“Who did?”

Eleanor paused.

“Dr Samuel Hale was named as the medical records authority in the review request. He was not present when I examined the seal.”

“Was he asked to verify the notification?”

“Not on the material I saw.”

Edward wrote that down.

“Did you speak to him afterward?”

“No.”

“Why not?”

“Because my role was to review the seal and preserve the separation between the records. I was not authorised to reopen the identity process.”

“Someone had already bypassed it.”

“And I could not correct that by pretending I had the authority to decide who the child belonged to.”

Jonathan closed his notebook halfway. “Did you send your note to anyone?”

“To the archive custodian.”

“Who was that?”

“The custodian recorded on the preservation register.”

“Adrian Holt?”

“I can’t confirm the person’s identity from the copy I retained.”

Edward gave a short, humourless laugh. “Every answer leads to another blank.”

“Because the blanks are real,” Eleanor said. “You can dislike them. You cannot fill them with guesses.”

He looked at her for a long moment. Then he lowered his eyes to the review record.

There was one detail he had missed.

“‘Receiving party acknowledges the proposed allocation,’” he read aloud. “Not ‘receiving family.’”

Eleanor nodded.

“Was that deliberate?”

“I believe so.”

“Why?”

“Because the receiving party might have been a residence, a temporary guardian, a representative, or an institution. It did not necessarily mean a biological family.”

Edward felt the next question form before he could stop it.

“Could the receiving party have been the Valmonts?”

“It could have been many people.”

“The Aureliuses?”

“Possibly.”

“Isabelle?”

Eleanor’s expression changed at the name, but only slightly.

“I have no record that names Isabelle Valmont as a recipient.”

“Or Elena?”

“No.”

The answer was immediate.

Edward set the page down.

“Then we still don’t know whether the notification concerned either of them.”

“We don’t.”

“And we don’t know whether the child named in the proposed arrangement was the child who went to that family.”

“No.”

“Or whether a child was ever delivered under that arrangement.”

“No.”

Jonathan wrote for a moment, then looked up. “You said someone had started using Schedule 4. Was that the first time you saw evidence that the proposed allocation was being treated as final?”

Eleanor considered the question.

“No,” she said. “It was the first time I saw evidence that a notification had been recorded as complete while identity verification remained unresolved.”

Edward’s pen stopped.

“Then what was the earlier evidence?”

Eleanor looked toward the window. Outside, the bare branches of a tree shifted in the late-morning wind.

“I saw a reference to a family acknowledgement,” she said. “It was not attached to Schedule 4. It was listed in a separate correspondence register.”

“Where?”

“Vale Private Counsel’s archive.”

“Do you have the reference?”

“Yes.”

She opened the folder to its last page and turned it toward them.

The reference was short.

**VC/NR-118/ACK**

Below it, in a separate column, appeared a line:

**Acknowledgement received. Identity reconciliation not completed.**

Edward read the date.

Day 118.

The same day Eleanor had reviewed the seal.

“Was this the acknowledgement the review request referred to?”

“I don’t know. The register did not identify the document it acknowledged.”

“Did it identify the sender?”

“No.”

“The recipient?”

“No.”

“Then why did you keep it?”

“Because it was the only record I found that used the word ‘acknowledgement’ without claiming that identity had been verified.”

Edward studied the reference. “Can we obtain the original register?”

“You can request it from the archive. I don’t know whether it still exists.”

“Who maintained it?”

“Vale Private Counsel.”

“Charles Vale’s office.”

“Yes.”

The answer was simple. The implication was not.

For eighteen years, documents had travelled through offices, hospitals, private residences, trusts, and archives. Every transfer had produced another number, another seal, another signature. Yet the crucial information—the identity of the child, the authority of the receiving party, the name of the family—had remained absent.

Someone had built a process that could move records without ever completing the question those records were meant to answer.

Edward gathered the pages into a neat stack.

“Why didn’t you tell us about the acknowledgement when we first spoke?”

“Because you asked what I discovered during the review of Schedule 4. I told you.”

“You could have mentioned it.”

“I could have. I did not know whether it belonged to the same notification. I didn’t want you to treat two incomplete records as one confirmed event.”

Edward held her gaze, then nodded once.

That, at least, was a fair objection.

Jonathan closed his notebook. “We need the correspondence register.”

“Yes,” Eleanor said.

“And the original notification receipt.”

“If it still exists.”

“And whoever maintained the register.”

“If you can identify them.”

Edward stood. “We’ll start with the archive.”

Eleanor remained seated.

“One more thing,” she said.

He turned.

“The day-118 review request included a handwritten instruction. It was not on the copy I gave you.”

“What instruction?”

“‘Do not inform the originating family until the receiving identity is confirmed.’”

Edward’s face hardened.

“That sounds like the correct rule.”

“It was.”

“Then why mention it?”

“Because someone had added a second line beneath it.”

Eleanor slid another note across the table. It was a transcription, not an original. The handwriting had been copied carefully, preserving the spacing and punctuation.

**If the receiving identity cannot be confirmed, notify the family already informed.**

Edward stared at the sentence.

“Already informed,” he said. “Not ‘the receiving family.’”

“Correct.”

“Which means someone believed a family had already been told.”

“Or wanted the record to say that.”

Jonathan leaned forward. “Was the instruction signed?”

“No.”

“Was it followed?”

“I don’t know.”

Edward looked from the note to the reference number on the register.

The next step was clear, though the destination was not.

They had to find the acknowledgement. They had to find the notification receipt. And they had to discover why a process that had never confirmed a child’s identity had nevertheless begun referring to a family as already informed.

For the first time, the investigation seemed to be approaching the moment when an administrative error became a decision.

And somewhere in the records, someone had written down that the decision had already been made.
