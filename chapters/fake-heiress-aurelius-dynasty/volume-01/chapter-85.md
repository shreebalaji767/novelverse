# **Chapter 85 — The House That Was Not Supposed to Keep Secrets**

Edward did not go to V-17 immediately.

That was the first thing Jonathan found strange.

For three days, Edward had been carrying the same property reference in his notebook. He had requested the original custody register, obtained the archived ownership records, and asked the legal department to verify whether the property still existed under its old designation.

Yet he had not made a single attempt to enter it.

On the fourth morning, Jonathan finally asked.

"Are we waiting for something?"

Edward closed the folder.

"Permission."

Jonathan looked at him.

"From whom?"

"That is what I am trying to establish."

He turned the folder around.

The first page contained the property designation:

**V-17 — PRIVATE RESIDENTIAL CUSTODY FACILITY**

Below it was an old administrative address, followed by a change-of-ownership notation.

The second page contained a brief description.

**Residential property. Restricted internal storage. No access permitted without the receiving family's written consent.**

Jonathan read it twice.

"Why would a private residence have an archival access condition?"

"Because somebody wanted it to function as more than a residence."

"An archive?"

"Possibly."

Edward tapped the final line.

"But the condition doesn't say the owner must give permission. It says the receiving family."

Jonathan leaned back.

"Which receiving family?"

"Exactly."

The property records were held by a private land registry.

Edward had expected a straightforward ownership trail. Instead, he found three layers of administration.

V-17 had originally belonged to a property company associated with the Vale family. It had subsequently been transferred to a private holding trust. The trust's administrative representative was Charles Vale.

The transfer had taken place shortly before the children's records were moved from the Family Office archive.

There was no indication that the building had been used as a permanent family residence.

It had been occupied intermittently.

A caretaker had been employed. Utilities had been maintained. Security services had been paid. But the property did not appear in the ordinary household expenditure records.

"It was maintained without being lived in," Jonathan observed.

Edward nodded.

"Which suggests storage, temporary accommodation, or both."

"Can we establish what was stored there?"

"Not from the property records."

Edward opened another document.

It was a maintenance inventory prepared several years after the original transfer.

Most of the entries were unremarkable.

Furniture.

Household equipment.

A generator.

Security systems.

A locked interior room.

The final entry was different.

**One sealed administrative package, retained under external custody instruction.**

There was no description of its contents.

No file number.

No originating department.

Only a handwritten note:

**Do not transfer to general household storage.**

Jonathan stared at it.

"That sounds like Annex C."

"It sounds like something someone didn't want mixed with ordinary property records."

"Could it be the same package?"

"We don't know."

Edward placed the inventory beside the V-17 custody register.

The dates were close.

The language was similar.

But similarity was not identification.

He had spent too many months watching people turn administrative references into conclusions.

He was not going to do it again.

The caretaker's name was Arthur Bell.

He had retired eight years earlier and now lived in a small house outside the city.

When Edward and Jonathan arrived, Arthur was repairing a wooden cabinet in his garage.

He listened quietly as Edward explained the investigation.

"V-17," Arthur repeated. "I haven't heard that name in years."

"Do you remember the property?"

"Of course. I looked after it for nearly twelve years."

"Was it occupied?"

"Sometimes. Mr. Vale visited. A few other people came with him. Mostly administrative people."

"Did you ever see files or documents being stored there?"

Arthur considered the question.

"There was a room."

"What kind of room?"

"Not a bedroom. It was at the end of the west corridor. The previous owners had used it as a study, I think. When I started working there, the windows were sealed from the inside."

"Was it locked?"

"Always."

"Who had access?"

"Mr. Vale. A man from the security company. And occasionally another gentleman who came with a leather case."

Edward exchanged a glance with Jonathan.

"Can you describe him?"

"Older than Mr. Vale. Tall. Thin. Usually wore a dark suit. He didn't speak much."

"Do you remember his name?"

"No."

"Did he carry documents?"

Arthur nodded.

"Every time."

"Did he ever remove anything?"

"Once."

Edward sat forward.

"When?"

"I can't give you the exact date. It was a long time ago."

"Approximately?"

Arthur looked toward the garage door, as though the answer might be written somewhere outside.

"Several years after the property first opened. Maybe three. Maybe four."

"Did he carry a package?"

"Not a package. A folder."

"What did it look like?"

"Brown. Thick. Had a string around it."

Edward felt his attention sharpen.

"Did you see any markings?"

Arthur shook his head.

"No. I wasn't supposed to look."

"Who told you that?"

"Mr. Vale."

"What exactly did he say?"

Arthur paused.

"He said the room contained material belonging to people who had never been informed that it existed. He told me that opening it would create trouble for everyone."

Jonathan frowned.

"Did he explain who those people were?"

"No."

"Did you ask?"

"Once."

Arthur's expression changed.

"He told me that I was employed to maintain the house, not to understand why it was being maintained."

Edward made a note.

"Did Mr. Vale ever mention a receiving family?"

Arthur looked up.

"Yes."

Both men became still.

"What did he say?"

Arthur rubbed his hands against a cloth.

"He said the room could only be opened when the receiving family agreed."

"Did he identify the family?"

"No."

"Did he say whether it was one family or more than one?"

"No. But I remember thinking it was strange."

"Why?"

Arthur gave a small, humourless smile.

"Because he never used the word family when he spoke about business. He usually said client, representative, or beneficiary. That was the only time I heard him use it."

Edward wrote down the exact phrase.

"Do you remember anyone giving permission?"

Arthur hesitated.

"I remember a letter."

"What letter?"

"Mr. Vale received it. He read it in the hallway, then told the security man to keep the room sealed."

"Was the letter granting permission?"

"I couldn't see it."

"Who sent it?"

"I don't know."

"Was it delivered by hand?"

Arthur nodded.

"By a woman."

"Can you describe her?"

"Not properly. Dark hair. Middle-aged. She had a brown case."

Edward stopped writing.

"A brown leather case?"

Arthur looked at him.

"Yes."

The room became quiet.

Jonathan leaned forward.

"Are you certain?"

"Quite certain. I remember because she nearly left it in the car. She came back for it before entering."

Edward did not immediately respond.

A woman with a brown leather case had appeared in several accounts.

But the description was not unique. It could not establish identity.

"Did you ever see her again?"

"No."

"Did she enter the locked room?"

Arthur shook his head.

"She spoke to Mr. Vale in the sitting room. Then she left."

"What happened to the letter?"

"Mr. Vale took it into his office."

"Did the room remain locked?"

"Yes."

"Was the sealed package still there?"

Arthur frowned.

"I don't know."

On the drive back, Jonathan remained unusually quiet.

Finally, he said, "We have a woman with a brown leather case, a letter, and a sealed room."

"We have a caretaker's recollection."

"You think the woman was Marianne Cross?"

"I think it is one possibility."

"Or Eleanor Vale?"

"Another possibility."

"Or someone else entirely."

Edward nodded.

"That is why we don't write a name beside her description."

Jonathan looked out of the window.

"Then what do we do?"

"We find the letter."

The next request went through the property's former security contractor.

The company had changed ownership twice, but its archived records had survived.

After several days, Edward received a scanned copy of an access report.

The report concerned the west corridor.

It recorded three entries on the date Arthur had described.

The first belonged to Charles Vale.

The second belonged to an unnamed visitor.

The third belonged to the security supervisor.

No entry was recorded for the woman with the brown case.

That did not necessarily mean Arthur was mistaken. Visitors could be recorded under the name of the person who authorised their entry.

But the report contained another detail.

**WEST ROOM — ACCESS RESTRICTED.**

**Opening condition: Written acknowledgement from receiving representative.**

And beneath it:

**Acknowledgement received. Access not granted.**

Edward read the last two lines several times.

The acknowledgement had not opened the room.

Someone had acknowledged the condition without satisfying it.

The distinction mattered.

"Who signed it?" Jonathan asked.

The signature had been obscured by a scanning defect.

Only the final initials remained visible.

**R.V.**

Jonathan frowned.

"Receiving Verification?"

"Possibly."

"Could be the same designation from Annex C."

"It could."

Edward enlarged the image.

The initials were followed by a short handwritten note.

**Identity of receiving party remains subject to confirmation.**

Jonathan stared at it.

"So even after receiving acknowledgement, they still didn't know who the receiving party was?"

"Or they knew the party's administrative designation but had not confirmed its identity."

"Which means the room remained sealed."

"Yes."

Edward returned to the access report.

There was another line at the bottom.

**Subsequent access requires originating authority and receiving authority to be present together.**

He sat back.

For eighteen years, the records had been separated, transferred, sealed, and withheld.

Now they had found a physical room governed by almost the same restrictions.

It was becoming difficult to believe that the administrative confusion had been accidental.

But difficulty was not proof.

At the Aurelius Estate, Victoria was reading the latest summary when Alexander entered.

She had already read it twice.

"V-17," she said.

Alexander placed his tablet on the desk.

"Edward believes it may have held the complete Annex C file."

"Does he know?"

"No. He has only found a sealed administrative package and a restricted room."

Victoria lowered the report.

"Someone kept information about our daughter in a house for eighteen years."

"We don't know that the package concerned her."

"It concerned the records that were supposed to establish where our daughter went."

Alexander did not argue.

Victoria turned toward the window.

"I signed that general authority because I believed the Family Office was preserving medical records. I did not know it would be used to release one part of a file I had never seen."

"I know."

"I want the original authority examined again."

"It already has been."

"Then examine the person who prepared it."

Alexander's expression hardened slightly.

"Edward is doing that."

Victoria looked down at the report.

"Alexander, I don't want another explanation about administrative procedure. I want to know who decided that our family could be kept outside the truth."

He remained silent for a moment.

Then he said, "So do I."

That evening, Edward received an unexpected communication from Charles Vale's solicitor.

The message was brief.

Charles Vale had been informed of the inquiry concerning V-17.

He acknowledged that the property had once been used for restricted administrative storage.

He also confirmed that a sealed room had existed in the west corridor.

However, he disputed the suggestion that the room had contained Annex C.

The solicitor attached a statement.

**The property was maintained under an arrangement involving multiple originating and receiving representatives. No individual family was entitled to access the restricted material without completion of the relevant verification process.**

Edward read the statement.

Then he read the next paragraph.

**The client further confirms that the room was formally cleared following the conclusion of the original custody arrangement.**

Jonathan looked up from his desk.

"Cleared?"

"Apparently."

"When?"

Edward scrolled down.

The date was listed.

It was eighteen years earlier.

Only a few months after the original transfers.

Jonathan stood.

"Who cleared it?"

Edward continued reading.

The solicitor had included the name of the person who signed the clearance certificate.

**Adrian Holt.**

Neither man spoke.

Holt had already admitted receiving both parts of Annex C.

He had also admitted transferring the material to Unit 6 and later to V-17.

Now his signature appeared on a certificate declaring the room cleared.

But the certificate did not list the documents removed.

It did not identify the person who received them.

It did not even confirm that the room had been emptied of every restricted item.

At the bottom was a final handwritten notation.

**One item retained outside the property inventory. Custody transferred separately.**

Edward read it aloud.

"One item retained outside the property inventory."

Jonathan slowly sat down.

"What item?"

"There is no description."

"Where was it transferred?"

Edward looked at the blank space beneath the notation.

There was no destination.

No recipient.

Only a reference number.

**RC-18/FINAL.**

For the first time in several days, Edward felt that V-17 had not brought them closer to the end of the investigation.

It had shown them that someone had moved the evidence again.

And this time, the missing item had never been entered into the property records at all.
