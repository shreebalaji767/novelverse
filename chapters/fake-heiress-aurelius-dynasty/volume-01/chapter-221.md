# **Chapter 221 — The Authority That Had Never Been Assigned**

The authority matrix changed the direction of the investigation.

Edward had expected another administrative record.

Instead, he had found a document that defined precisely what the original system was supposed to prevent.

It did not identify a child.

It did not name a receiving family.

It did not appoint a final identity authority.

But it established something important: the absence of a final identity determination was not an oversight that later offices were free to correct through administrative wording.

It was an intentional safeguard.

At 8:15 the following morning, Edward placed the matrix before Eleanor.

“Dr. Hale signed the preservation section,” he said. “The medical records authority signed the source-custody section. The independent review authority signed the comparison section. The receiving administration representative signed the coordination section.”

“And the final identity authority?”

“Blank.”

“Was it supposed to remain blank?”

“Until a competent authority was separately designated.”

Eleanor read the final paragraph again.

“Then we need to find out whether anyone was ever appointed.”

“That's today's first task.”

The designation register was held by the Restricted Personal Records Authority.

Its director, Helen Ward, joined the meeting remotely.

She had already received Edward's written request.

“I searched the appointment register using the matrix reference,” she said. “There is no completed designation for the final identity determination function.”

“None?” Edward asked.

“None under the original authority framework.”

“Was there a later appointment?”

“There are several administrative references to proposed appointments, but none that satisfies the designation requirements.”

Eleanor asked, “What requirements?”

Helen shared the relevant section.

A valid designation required:

1. Identification of the competent appointing authority.
2. Written acceptance by the proposed appointee.
3. Confirmation of independence from the receiving route.
4. Defined access to the original source materials.
5. A documented scope of examination.
6. A separate record of the conclusion and its limitations.

“Was any individual appointed under all six conditions?” Eleanor asked.

“No.”

Edward looked at the register.

“What about Dr Evelyn Shaw?”

“Dr Shaw declined the OR-28 identity review. Her separate review of the alternate recipient's conditional response did not constitute a final identity appointment.”

“Dr Samuel Reed?”

“He reviewed administrative material and later examined certain records. He was not appointed as the final identity authority.”

“Dr Helen Foster?”

“She examined the physical band material. Her appointment did not extend to biological identity.”

“Dr Miriam Cole?”

“She examined the sealed family communication attachment. That examination did not authorise her to determine infant identity.”

Edward wrote each name down.

Four specialists.

Four distinct responsibilities.

None had been appointed to make the final determination.

Eleanor asked, “Could the final authority still be appointed now?”

“Yes, but only through the correct process. The original source custodians must confirm what materials exist and what permissions apply. The appointing authority must be competent and independent. The proposed examiner must accept the defined scope.”

“Can the RPRA appoint that person?”

“We can coordinate the process. We cannot independently decide which family a protected individual belongs to.”

Edward nodded.

“Then we need the original materials inventory.”

Helen opened another file.

“I anticipated that request.”

The inventory arrived at 9:06.

It contained five preservation categories.

**HIST-27/06-A:** Original maternity records.

**HIST-27/06-B:** Original neonatal records.

**HIST-27/06-C:** Identification materials.

**HIST-27/06-D:** Personal identity comparison documents.

**HIST-27/06-E:** Chain-of-custody records.

The entries were not interchangeable.

A maternity register could establish what had been recorded about a delivery.

A neonatal card could establish what had been recorded about an infant's clinical care.

An identification band could provide a physical reference.

A personal identity comparison document could record a previous examination.

A chain-of-custody record could establish how an item had been handled.

None alone necessarily established that two separate records concerned the same person.

Edward studied the inventory.

“Which originals remain available?”

Helen answered carefully.

“Some remain in their original custodial locations. Others are held under the Meridian restricted archive arrangement. Several have incomplete custody intervals.”

“Can the original St Catherine's materials be examined?”

“The source custodian has indicated that an independent comparison may be considered, subject to approval from the medical records authority and the preservation framework.”

“Has that approval been requested?”

“Not yet.”

Eleanor leaned forward.

“Why not?”

“Because the previous identity review was suspended before the required permissions were completed. We cannot assume that the earlier request remains valid.”

Edward understood.

The investigation could not simply take old paperwork and send everything to a laboratory.

The original custodians had to authorise access.

The examining authority had to be appointed.

The purpose and limits of the examination had to be written down.

And any biological comparison would require a separate, properly consented process.

He wrote the next request.

**Preservation confirmation and authorisation for independent examination of original identification materials.**

At St Catherine's, the records office was unusually quiet.

Dr. Samuel Hale stood beside the locked archive cabinet while Helen Price reviewed the original register.

Helen had retired from medical records administration years earlier, but she had agreed to assist with locating the historical entries.

She pointed to a line.

“Here. The original maternity entry.”

Edward examined it through the archive viewing panel.

The time was 2:17 a.m.

The recorded infant was female.

The original entry had not been erased.

A later annotation appeared in the receiving-reference column.

**Proposed receiving reference: STC-A.**

Edward looked at the amendment record.

“This was the Day-6 change.”

“Yes,” Helen said.

“Who confirmed that STC-A belonged to this infant?”

“No one confirmed it in this record.”

“Why was it entered?”

“For administrative continuity. We were told the receiving desk would reconcile it.”

“Did the receiving desk confirm it?”

Helen looked at the response attached to the original amendment file.

“No. They said identity remained unconfirmed and original identification material was required.”

“Was that response returned to St Catherine's?”

“It was received.”

“Was it entered into the later summary?”

“No.”

Edward looked at Dr. Hale.

“Did you know the later Day-28 summary described STC-A as an established receiving identity?”

“I learned that much later.”

“Did you authorise that description?”

“No.”

Helen Price spoke quietly.

“I didn't either.”

Edward turned to her.

“You entered the administrative amendment.”

“Yes.”

“Did you believe you were confirming the infant's identity?”

“No. I believed the receiving office would do that separately.”

“Did you see the original identification material?”

“No.”

“Did you compare the maternity record with the neonatal band?”

“No.”

She looked at the register.

“I was entering a reference that had been supplied to us. I was not making a medical or identity determination.”

Edward recorded her answer.

He did not treat it as proof of innocence or guilt.

It was a statement that could be checked against the original task record, the amendment history, and the receiving office's response.

The original identification materials were brought into the viewing room later that afternoon.

They remained separately sealed.

The first item was the maternity identification slip.

The second was the neonatal band material.

Edward did not ask for them to be opened.

Dr. Hale explained the restrictions.

“The maternity slip is part of the delivery register's original identification material. The band material arrived separately. They were not deposited together, and their chain-of-custody histories are different.”

“Can the two be compared?” Edward asked.

“Not by simply placing them side by side. First, the custodians must confirm the originals and their seals. Then the independent examiner must be appointed. The examination must establish what can be reliably compared.”

“What if the markings match?”

“That would establish a relationship between the identification materials only if the match is sufficiently distinctive and the chain of custody supports it. It would not, by itself, establish a biological relationship to a family.”

Eleanor asked, “Could it help identify the infant?”

“It could help determine whether the maternity and neonatal records were originally linked. That is an important question. But it is not the same as proving parentage.”

Edward nodded.

The distinction mattered.

The first problem was whether the original records could be connected to one another.

The second was whether the infant described in those records could be connected to a particular person.

The third was whether that person could be biologically linked to a family.

Three questions.

Three different evidentiary requirements.

At 7:20 p.m., Elena finished revising her Singapore pilot documentation.

The six applications scheduled for the supervised pilot would be handled through separate roles.

The administrator would receive submissions.

The reviewer would assess them.

The decision committee would make decisions.

The delivery officer would record the distribution of approved grants.

Elena had added a section describing what the platform would not do.

It would not automatically approve applications.

It would not decide eligibility.

It would not turn a document upload into verification.

It would not transfer permissions to a replacement officer without a recorded authorisation.

She sent the revised document to Ms. Tan.

A reply arrived ten minutes later.

**Ms. Tan:** The role separation is useful. Please bring the revised version to Friday's pilot preparation meeting.

Elena typed back.

**Elena:** I will. I also want the administrator and reviewer to test the same application independently before the pilot begins.

**Ms. Tan:** Agreed.

She closed her laptop.

Her phone displayed another message.

This time, it was from Isabelle.

**Isabelle:** The foundation director asked whether you would be willing to advise on the procedure after Tuesday's meeting.

Elena considered the wording.

**Elena:** I can explain the platform and identify workflow problems. I won't agree to become responsible for their grant administration.

**Isabelle:** I told her that.

**Elena:** Thank you.

**Isabelle:** You don't have to thank me for respecting a boundary.

Elena smiled faintly.

That was a sentence she had not expected to hear from Isabelle a few months earlier.

The following morning, Edward received an answer from the original source custodian.

The maternity identification slip could be examined under a new independent preservation appointment.

The neonatal band material could also be examined, but Meridian required a separate confirmation of its custody history.

The personal identity comparison documents would remain sealed until the scope of the new examination was approved.

No biological samples would be taken from the historical materials.

No family identity conclusion would be issued from the initial physical comparison.

The proposed scope was narrow:

**Determine whether the original maternity identification slip and the preserved neonatal band material can be reliably associated with the same historical record or infant, taking account of the documented custody limitations.**

Edward read the proposed scope.

“This is enough to begin.”

Eleanor asked, “Who will examine them?”

“The source custodians have proposed an independent forensic records examiner.”

“Have they accepted?”

“Not yet.”

“Then we don't have an appointment.”

“Correct.”

Edward sent the acceptance request.

At 3:45 p.m., the examiner responded.

Dr. Miriam Cole had accepted the appointment.

Her previous work on F-02/SOURCE/01 would be disclosed as part of the independence assessment.

She would not examine the family communication material again.

She would examine only the physical and documentary relationship between the original maternity identification slip and the neonatal band material.

Her acceptance included a written limitation:

**I will not infer biological parentage, family membership, or the identity of any living individual from the proposed examination.**

Edward read the sentence.

Then he forwarded it to the source custodians.

The appointment was not yet complete.

The custodians still had to approve the access arrangements and confirm the original seals.

But for the first time, the investigation had a specialist willing to examine the actual historical identification materials under a defined scope.

Eleanor looked at the appointment request.

“Do you think this will finally tell us who the child was?”

“No,” Edward said.

“Not even if the materials match?”

“It may tell us whether two records belong together. It won't tell us who the child became.”

Eleanor was silent for a moment.

“Then we still need a separate biological comparison.”

“Yes.”

“And Elena?”

Edward closed the file.

“Until we have a lawful basis to approach her, informed consent, and a properly authorised comparison, we don't involve her.”

He looked again at the blank final identity designation on the original authority matrix.

The old system had allowed people to speak as though an identity had been established.

But the final authority had never been appointed.

The original materials had never been properly compared.

And the person they were ultimately trying to identify had never been asked to participate.

Edward placed the new appointment request beside the old matrix.

This time, he would not allow a completed administrative field to stand in for an answer.

**The investigation could proceed. The identity remained unknown.**
