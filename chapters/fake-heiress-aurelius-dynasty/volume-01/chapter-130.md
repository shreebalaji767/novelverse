# **Chapter 130 — The Meeting That Had Never Taken Place**

Victoria Aurelius read the notification three times.

The first time, she examined the date.

The second time, she examined her signature.

The third time, she read the handwritten qualification beneath it.

*I acknowledge receipt of notice only. I have not attended a joint review, approved any receiving arrangement, or accepted the appointment of a receiving authority.*

She placed the document on Alexander's desk.

“I remember this.”

Edward, seated opposite them, looked up.

“You remember receiving it?”

“Yes. It arrived at the Family Office on day 46.”

“Do you remember who delivered it?”

“A member of the correspondence department. I don't remember his name.”

“Did you attend a meeting that day?”

“No.”

“Did anyone ask you to attend a joint custody review?”

“Not personally.”

“Did you authorise anyone to represent you?”

“No.”

Alexander examined the document.

“Then why did you sign the acknowledgement?”

Victoria answered without hesitation.

“Because I received a notice. I was acknowledging that the document had reached me, not agreeing with its contents.”

Edward pointed to the handwritten qualification.

“Did you add this?”

“Yes. The original notice described the event as a joint custody review. I had not attended one, so I made that distinction before signing.”

“Did you send it back?”

“I did.”

“Did you receive a response?”

“No.”

Edward looked at the date again.

Day 46.

The same day the second contact request had been rejected.

The same day the supposed joint custody review had been recorded.

And the same day Victoria had explicitly denied participating in it.

Alexander opened the Family Office correspondence register.

“Search the day-46 entries,” he told his assistant.

A few minutes later, a digital copy appeared on the screen.

The notice had been sent by the Personal Instructions Division.

Its subject was:

**PR-06 / JOINT CUSTODY REVIEW — NOTICE OF PROPOSED REVIEW**

The wording differed slightly from the copy Edward had found.

The original said a review had been proposed.

The later archive entry described it as initiated.

That was not a small difference.

“Who changed the description?” Alexander asked.

Edward examined the document history.

“The original notice was issued before the meeting date. The completed-review entry was created afterwards.”

“By whom?”

“We're still tracing the account.”

Victoria looked at the document.

“Did they ever send me the outcome?”

“No,” Edward replied.

“Then how could they claim I had participated?”

“That is what we need to establish.”

She turned to Alexander.

“I want the original correspondence preserved. No one should alter the records because they believe they understand what happened.”

Alexander nodded.

“Agreed.”

The first person Edward interviewed was the former head of the Personal Instructions Division, Helen Ward.

She remembered the notice.

“I prepared the original wording,” she said.

“Did you organise the joint review?”

“No.”

“Who did?”

“I was told it had been requested by Charles Vale's office.”

“Were the participants confirmed?”

“No. The receiving identity custodian had not been appointed.”

“Then why was the notice sent to Victoria?”

“Because she was listed as an originating family representative.”

“Was she invited to attend?”

“Not in the usual sense. It was a notice of proposed review, not a confirmed meeting invitation.”

Edward placed the two versions of the record in front of her.

“Why does the later version say the review was initiated?”

Helen examined them.

“I didn't write that.”

“Who did?”

“I don't know.”

“Did you receive Victoria's acknowledgement?”

“Yes.”

“Did you interpret it as approval?”

“No. Her qualification was explicit.”

“Did you enter the review as completed?”

“Absolutely not.”

Helen returned the documents.

“The distinction between a notice, an acknowledgement, and an approval was one of the reasons I kept the correspondence register separate.”

Edward looked at her.

“Then who changed the record?”

Helen hesitated.

“There was a temporary administrative officer assigned to the division during that period.”

“Name?”

“Peter Ashford.”

Edward exchanged a glance with Jonathan.

The same Peter Ashford who had admitted amending the PR-06 delivery instruction under Eleanor's account.

“Did he have authority to close a joint review?”

“No.”

“Could he alter the administrative status?”

“He could update the migration index. He could not create a legal or procedural approval.”

“Did he have access to the joint custody records?”

“Only the index.”

Edward wrote down the answer.

Peter's name had appeared again.

But the fact that he could alter an index did not establish that he had created the underlying decision.

Peter Ashford arrived two days later.

He looked uncomfortable when Edward showed him the joint custody entry.

“I remember the reference,” he said.

“JC-46.”

“Yes.”

“Did you enter this?”

Peter studied the document.

“I updated the index.”

“What did you enter?”

“Review initiated.”

“Why?”

“Because that was the status on the instruction sheet.”

“Did you see an actual meeting record?”

“No.”

“Did you see minutes?”

“No.”

“Did you see an attendance register?”

“No.”

“Then what was your basis for marking the review initiated?”

Peter looked down.

“The request said the joint review had been arranged.”

“Who supplied the request?”

“Mr Greer's office.”

“Thomas Greer?”

“Yes.”

“Did you speak to him?”

“No. The instruction came through the legal correspondence desk.”

Edward placed the Victoria acknowledgement beside the index.

“Did you see this?”

Peter nodded.

“I remember receiving the returned notice.”

“What did you do with it?”

“I attached it to the file.”

“Did you read the qualification?”

“Yes.”

“Then why did the index later say the review was completed?”

Peter shook his head.

“I didn't enter completed.”

Edward pointed to the entry.

The record contained two separate status changes.

**Review initiated — Day 46.**

**Review completed — Day 47.**

The first had been entered under Peter's migration access.

The second had been entered under a shared Personal Instructions account.

Peter leaned closer.

“I didn't make the second change.”

“Who did?”

“I don't know.”

“Could you have done it using the shared account?”

Peter hesitated.

“Technically, yes. But I didn't.”

Edward did not accuse him.

“Did you have access to the original joint custody file?”

“No. Only the index.”

“Did you know the proposed review had not taken place?”

Peter looked at Victoria's acknowledgement.

“I knew she had not attended.”

“Did you know whether the receiving identity custodian had attended?”

“There wasn't one.”

Peter paused.

“So no, I didn't believe a joint review had actually taken place.”

The second status change led back to the Family Allocation Review system.

Martin Keane recovered the audit from the migration backup.

The entry was more detailed than the previous summary.

**Day 47 — 09:32**

**Account:** PERSONAL-INSTRUCTIONS

**Operator:** SYSTEM MIGRATION

**Action:** Update joint custody status

**Previous status:** Proposed

**New status:** Completed for administrative purposes

**Reference:** JC-46

**Supporting record:** Victoria Aurelius acknowledgement

**Approval reference:** Not supplied

Edward stared at the screen.

“System Migration isn't a person.”

“No,” Martin replied.

“Who could use that account?”

“Migration administrators and two authorised department supervisors.”

“Could the account update a status without an approval reference?”

“Yes.”

“Should it have?”

“No. The system should have rejected the change.”

“Why didn't it?”

Martin examined the audit.

“Because the field was configured to accept an administrative completion status. It wasn't designed to distinguish a completed meeting from a completed administrative task.”

Edward looked at Jonathan.

Another system had allowed an administrative action to appear like a substantive decision.

“Who requested the change?”

Martin opened the attached task record.

The request had come from Thomas Greer's office.

Its wording was precise.

*For administrative continuity, record the joint custody review as completed for administrative purposes. No permanent receiving appointment is implied.*

Edward read the final sentence.

No permanent receiving appointment was implied.

It was another qualification.

But the heading still said **Joint Custody Review — Completed**.

“Did Greer authorise the use of Victoria's acknowledgement as supporting evidence?” Edward asked.

Martin checked the attachment list.

“Yes. Her acknowledgement was attached.”

“Did the system record her qualification?”

“No. Only the front page of the acknowledgement was indexed.”

Edward looked at the audit.

The system had retained the fact that Victoria received the notice.

It had omitted the fact that she denied participating in the review.

The result was misleading, even if the administrative instruction had not explicitly claimed that she approved the arrangement.

Thomas Greer received Edward's call that afternoon.

He did not deny submitting the request.

“I wanted the administrative task closed,” he said.

“Why did you call it a completed joint custody review?”

“Because the correspondence had been processed.”

“Correspondence processing is not a joint review.”

“I understand that.”

“Did you know Victoria had expressly denied attending?”

“Yes.”

“Then why attach her acknowledgement?”

“To show that the originating representative had received the notice.”

“Not to show that she approved the review?”

“Correct.”

Edward looked at the audit.

“Did you know the system would omit her handwritten qualification?”

“No.”

“Did you check the resulting entry?”

“I did not see the final index.”

“Did you know the receiving authority had not been appointed?”

“Yes.”

“Then why submit a request concerning a joint custody review?”

Greer took a breath.

“Because the private instruction required a joint review before the protected material could be accessed. The administration needed to record that the review process had been initiated and that the originating representative had received notice.”

“But the receiving authority was missing.”

“Yes.”

“Then the review could not take place.”

“Not in its final form.”

Edward leaned back.

“Was there any meeting at all?”

“No.”

“Any joint decision?”

“No.”

“Any approval from Victoria?”

“No.”

Greer was quiet.

“I believed the administrative record would make the distinction clear.”

“It didn't.”

“No.”

That evening, Edward briefed the Aurelius family.

Alexander read the day-47 task request.

“So Greer did not claim that the review had actually occurred.”

“Not in his original instruction,” Edward replied.

“But the system recorded it as completed.”

“Yes.”

Victoria looked at her acknowledgement.

“And my qualification was removed.”

“From the index, yes. The original document still exists.”

She looked troubled.

“I remember writing that sentence because I was afraid someone might treat my signature as approval.”

Edward nodded.

“You were right to make the distinction.”

Alexander placed the document down.

“Then the joint review never happened.”

“Correct.”

“And the second contact remained unauthorised.”

“Yes.”

Victoria looked towards the window.

“Someone kept using our acknowledgements to create decisions we never made.”

No one answered immediately.

The pattern was becoming difficult to dismiss as a series of unrelated mistakes.

But Edward was careful.

A misleading administrative record did not automatically establish that every person involved intended to deceive the families.

He needed the original decision trail.

In Singapore, Elena received a message from Isabelle.

**The foundation committee approved the pilot changes. They want to use the revised application review process for the next grant cycle.**

Elena replied:

**That's excellent. Did they approve the separate status history too?**

**Yes. Apparently, the committee liked that part most.**

**Then make sure someone is responsible for checking the history records. A system can preserve changes without anyone noticing incorrect ones.**

Isabelle sent a short response.

**You really don't trust administrative systems.**

Elena smiled.

**I trust well-designed systems. I don't trust people who think the system removes the need to check their work.**

A moment later, Isabelle replied:

**Fair enough.**

Elena put her phone down.

She returned to the prototype and began preparing the final demonstration.

The project was no longer just an assignment.

Her supervisor had suggested that the team consider developing it further after the programme.

Elena had not made any decision.

But the possibility interested her.

The next morning, Jonathan found an unexpected entry in the V-17 custody records.

It concerned the original joint custody document.

**JC-46 / ORIGINAL**

**Removed:** Day 58.

**Collected by:** Charles Vale.

**Return status:** Not recorded.

**Related document:** JC-46 / ATTENDANCE SCHEDULE.

Edward looked up.

“Attendance schedule?”

Jonathan opened the linked record.

It was a single-page list.

The heading read:

**PROPOSED PARTICIPANTS — JOINT CUSTODY REVIEW**

Two positions were listed.

**Originating family representative: Victoria Aurelius.**

**Receiving identity authority: To be appointed.**

The second line was blank.

There was no name.

No signature.

No appointment reference.

And at the bottom, someone had written:

*Meeting may proceed for administrative review purposes in the absence of receiving identity authority.*

Edward read the sentence slowly.

“Who wrote this?”

Jonathan enlarged the scanned handwriting.

“Not sure. The document examiner will need the original.”

“Does the schedule show that a meeting took place?”

“No. It only lists proposed participants.”

Edward looked at the blank receiving authority field.

The document confirmed what they already knew.

There had been no appointed receiving authority.

No valid joint review.

No joint approval.

But the final sentence suggested someone had tried to permit a meeting without the second required participant.

Edward turned the page.

A small reference had been written in the margin.

**R-04 / ALTERNATE PARTICIPANT — PENDING.**

He stared at it.

“R-04?”

Jonathan nodded.

“The same correspondence facility.”

“Was an alternate participant ever nominated?”

“We haven't found a completed nomination.”

Edward looked at the blank field again.

The original instruction required two authorities to be present.

The administrative schedule had proposed proceeding without one.

And now R-04 appeared to have been asked to provide an alternate participant.

“Find the original R-04 request,” Edward said.

Jonathan began searching.

Then he stopped.

“There is one.”

Edward moved beside him.

The request was dated day 46.

It had been submitted before the joint custody review was marked completed.

Its subject was:

**REQUEST FOR SUBSTITUTE RECEIVING PARTICIPANT — P-04.**

Edward read the first paragraph.

*In the absence of an appointed receiving identity custodian, an alternate representative may attend the administrative review on behalf of the proposed recipient, provided the protected principal's authority has been independently confirmed.*

He looked at Jonathan.

“Who was nominated?”

Jonathan opened the final page.

The name was partially obscured by a preservation mark.

Only the first letter remained visible.

**S.**

Edward stared at it.

Another S.

Another protected identity.

And another person who might have been given authority to speak for someone else.

The document ended with a handwritten instruction:

*Do not contact the proposed substitute until the principal's position has been established.*

Edward closed the file.

The substitute had apparently been identified before the principal had ever been given the opportunity to respond.

And someone had ensured that neither of them could speak.
