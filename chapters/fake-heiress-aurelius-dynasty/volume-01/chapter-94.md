# **Chapter 94 — The Decision Nobody Had the Authority to Make**

Edward read the handwritten question again.

**If no one can certify the identity, who gave permission to decide it?**

It was written on the reverse of the surviving meeting minutes from day 50.

There was no signature.

No official stamp.

No indication of whether the note had been written during the meeting or added afterward.

But its meaning was unmistakable.

Someone had recognised the danger.

Someone had understood that the available evidence did not support a permanent identity decision.

And someone had considered the possibility that a decision had nevertheless been made.

Jonathan placed the other documents beside it.

"The independent auditor refused to certify the schedule."

"Yes."

"Margaret Voss refused."

"Yes."

"Holt refused."

"Yes."

"Marianne Cross was rejected."

Edward nodded.

"And Helena Brooks never accepted the nomination."

Jonathan looked at the list.

"How many people does that make?"

"Five proposed authorities, depending on how we count the original nomination attempts."

"All of them failed."

"Not necessarily failed. Some refused because the evidence was insufficient. Others were never properly appointed."

Jonathan pointed to the handwritten question.

"Then who decided?"

Edward did not answer.

He was beginning to suspect that the answer would not be found in a document labelled *Final Approval*.

It would be hidden in the ordinary administrative records created after someone decided that approval was no longer necessary.

The independent auditor was still alive.

Her name was Dr Evelyn Shaw.

She had retired from the National Medical Records Standards Council several years earlier and now worked occasionally as an adviser on archival integrity.

Edward contacted her through the council.

She agreed to meet them.

Her office was in a modest building shared by several professional organisations. There were no elaborate reception areas or private security arrangements.

Dr Shaw was a small woman with silver hair and a habit of listening without interrupting.

When Edward introduced himself, she looked at him carefully.

"Valmont?"

"Yes."

"Then this concerns the old St Catherine's matter."

"You remember it?"

"I remember the records."

She invited them to sit.

Edward placed the day-49 appointment document on the table.

Dr Shaw examined it.

"That is my nomination."

"You declined."

"Yes."

"Why?"

She looked at him.

"Because I was asked to certify something that the evidence could not establish."

"What exactly were you asked to certify?"

"The identity continuity between the originating neonatal records and the proposed receiving arrangements."

"Could you have done it if you had been given more time?"

Dr Shaw shook her head.

"Time was not the problem."

"What was?"

"The records were not properly connected."

She removed her glasses.

"Imagine two hospitals. One records a newborn under a temporary number. Another receives a newborn under a different temporary number. A transfer note says the records are related. But the original identification material is missing, the receiving acknowledgement is incomplete, and the maternal record has not been independently matched."

Edward listened.

"Could you certify that the two records referred to the same child?"

"No."

"Could you certify that they referred to different children?"

"Not without further evidence."

"Then what could you certify?"

"Only that two separate records existed and that someone had proposed connecting them."

Jonathan made a note.

Dr Shaw continued.

"That distinction is not a technicality. It is the foundation of the entire process."

Edward looked at her.

"Did you believe someone was trying to force a particular identity?"

"I believed someone wanted the administrative process completed before the evidence justified it."

"Did you tell them that?"

"Repeatedly."

"Who?"

"Charles Vale was present at one meeting. A representative from Vale Private Counsel attended another. I also communicated with Dr Samuel Hale."

"Did Charles accept your conclusion?"

"No."

"What did he say?"

Dr Shaw thought for a moment.

"He said the children were already in care and that uncertainty was causing unnecessary distress to the families."

Edward's expression tightened.

"Did he tell you which families?"

"No."

"Did he identify the children?"

"No."

"Then how did he expect you to certify the arrangements?"

"He expected me to accept the placement schedule as an administrative starting point."

"But it was supposed to be the result of verification."

"Exactly."

Dr Shaw opened a drawer and removed an old correspondence file.

"I kept this because I was concerned that my refusal might later be misrepresented."

Edward looked at the file.

"You kept the original?"

"Not the original schedule. My correspondence copies."

She placed three letters on the table.

The first was a request for certification.

The second was her refusal.

The third was a response from Vale Private Counsel.

Edward read the third letter.

It did not directly challenge her findings.

Instead, it argued that the receiving arrangements were already operating in practice and that the administrative schedule should reflect the existing position.

A sentence near the end caught his attention.

*The absence of a final receiving identity certificate should not prevent the recording of the current family allocation.*

Edward looked up.

"Family allocation?"

Dr Shaw nodded.

"That was the phrase they used."

"What did it mean?"

"I asked the same question."

"And?"

"They said it referred to the families currently responsible for the children."

"Temporary guardians?"

"That was not clear."

"Did they provide a list?"

"Yes."

Edward leaned forward.

"Do you still have it?"

Dr Shaw looked through the file.

"I have a reference to it."

She found the relevant page.

**CURRENT FAMILY ALLOCATION — SCHEDULE 4.**

The document itself was not attached.

"Was this the same as the recipient schedule?"

"No. It was described as a separate administrative record."

"Who prepared it?"

"Vale Private Counsel."

"Who approved it?"

Dr Shaw hesitated.

"That is where the problem began."

The letter contained a request for Dr Shaw to acknowledge that Schedule 4 represented the existing care arrangements.

She had refused.

Her response stated that she could not verify the schedule because it contained no supporting evidence of the children's identities or the legal authority of the receiving families.

But there was a second page.

It recorded a handwritten amendment.

*Schedule 4 may be maintained for administrative continuity only. It must not be treated as a final identity determination.*

The amendment bore the initials:

**S.H.**

Samuel Hale.

Edward looked at Jonathan.

"Hale amended it?"

"According to this document," Dr Shaw said.

"Did you see him write it?"

"No. The amendment was attached to the copy sent to me."

"Did you speak to him about it?"

"Yes."

"What did he say?"

"He said he had agreed that the schedule could be retained as a temporary administrative reference, provided it was not used as proof of biological or receiving identity."

Edward examined the page.

"Did Charles accept that limitation?"

Dr Shaw gave a humourless smile.

"He accepted the words."

"And ignored their meaning?"

"I cannot prove what he did afterward."

Edward looked down at the document.

The pattern was becoming clearer.

A provisional schedule had been created.

A medical authority had allowed it to be retained for administrative continuity.

An independent auditor had refused to certify it as an identity record.

And the people arranging the placements had continued to use it.

"Doctor," Edward asked, "did you ever see Schedule 4 itself?"

"No."

"Did you know where it was kept?"

"I was told that the originating and receiving records would be held separately."

"Under Annex C?"

"Possibly. I was never given the complete file."

"Then you cannot tell us which families were listed."

"No."

Edward nodded.

It was another lead, not an answer.

Before they left, Dr Shaw offered one final observation.

"There was something unusual about the way the meetings were conducted."

"What?"

"The people arranging the placements kept asking the medical representatives to certify that the children had been placed with the correct families."

"That was the purpose of the process."

"Yes. But they never asked us to determine who the correct families were."

Edward looked at her.

"What did they ask instead?"

"They asked us to confirm that the proposed arrangements could be recorded."

Jonathan frowned.

"That sounds like a distinction without a difference."

"It isn't."

Dr Shaw leaned forward.

"Determining the correct family requires evidence. Recording a proposed arrangement requires an instruction. They wanted the second to be treated as the first."

Edward slowly closed the file.

That was the decision nobody had the authority to make.

Not a single dramatic order.

Not a signed declaration.

A series of ordinary administrative steps that gradually transformed an unverified arrangement into something that looked official.

Back at the office, Edward requested every surviving reference to Schedule 4.

The results were frustrating.

The document was mentioned in four separate records.

A day-50 meeting note.

A day-61 preservation acknowledgement.

A financial reserve schedule.

And a later residential review.

None contained the complete schedule.

But the references provided one important detail.

Schedule 4 had been divided into two sections.

**SECTION A — CURRENT CARE ARRANGEMENTS.**

**SECTION B — PROPOSED PERMANENT FAMILY ALLOCATION.**

The distinction had not appeared in the earlier summaries.

Jonathan examined the financial reserve schedule.

"Could Section A be the temporary placements?"

"Yes."

"And Section B the proposed permanent arrangements?"

"That is what the wording suggests."

"Then why were they kept together?"

"Because someone wanted the administrative record to show both."

Jonathan tapped the second section.

"Do we know whether the proposed permanent arrangements were ever approved?"

"No."

"Do we know whether they were implemented?"

Edward looked at the residential review reference.

"Not yet."

The residential review had been conducted on day 92.

It was the same review that had proposed the name Elena for Child 28-B.

The report had been prepared at Meridian.

It described the child as medically stable and suitable for continued temporary care.

A later administrative note referred to Schedule 4, Section B.

The note stated:

*Proposed permanent family allocation not to be entered until originating identity verification is complete.*

That was important.

The residential review had not authorised a permanent family assignment.

It had explicitly prohibited one.

Edward requested the original review file.

The surviving copy was incomplete, but a handwritten notation appeared beside the restriction.

**Do not use the name proposed by the placement office as proof of identity.**

Edward read it twice.

The name Elena had been proposed for Child 28-B.

But the residential officer had refused to make it permanent.

And the officer had specifically warned against treating the proposed name as evidence.

Edward placed the page beside the earlier schedule.

"Someone knew," Jonathan said.

"Yes."

"Someone knew that the name and identity were not established."

"Yes."

"Then why did the name continue appearing in later records?"

Edward looked at the reference.

"Because a name can travel farther than the evidence supporting it."

At the Valmont estate, Elena was having a different argument.

It concerned luggage.

Catherine had placed three suitcases on the bed.

Elena stood in the doorway, looking at them.

"Mother."

"Yes?"

"Why are there three?"

"One for clothing, one for personal items, and one for anything you may need during the programme."

"That is not how luggage works."

Catherine looked at the suitcases.

"They are all reasonable sizes."

"Together, they are practically a small relocation."

Catherine smiled.

"You are going abroad for several months."

"I know. But I don't need to take my entire room."

Sebastian entered carrying a folder.

"I brought the document checklist."

Elena looked relieved.

"Thank you."

He placed it on the desk.

"Passport, admission letter, financial documents, insurance, accommodation confirmation, emergency contacts, academic certificates."

"All done."

"Medical records?"

"Those too."

"Copies?"

"Two."

"Digital backups?"

"Three."

Sebastian nodded approvingly.

Catherine looked between them.

"Are you both preparing for a university programme or an international audit?"

Elena laughed.

"He's the one who gave me the checklist."

"I merely made sure nothing important was overlooked," Sebastian said.

Elena picked up the folder.

"Thank you. This is actually useful."

Sebastian looked at the suitcases.

"Are you taking all three?"

"No."

Catherine gave him a mildly disappointed look.

"One large suitcase and one smaller bag," Elena explained.

"That sounds sensible," Sebastian said.

Catherine sighed.

"At least let me buy you a few things when you arrive."

Elena smiled.

"That would be nice."

She did not want to reject every gesture of affection.

She simply wanted to have a say in her own life.

And the people around her were slowly learning the difference.

Later that afternoon, Isabelle visited the foundation office.

She had been given permission to observe a general programme review.

Catherine introduced her to the department heads but made it clear that Isabelle was there to learn, not to participate in decisions.

Isabelle listened carefully.

She asked questions about the application process, funding limits, and how the foundation evaluated projects.

One manager explained that some applications were rejected not because the projects lacked merit, but because the organisations submitting them could not provide reliable financial records.

Isabelle wrote that down.

"Would a small organisation be allowed to reapply after improving its documentation?" she asked.

"Certainly," the manager replied. "We often encourage that."

"Do you provide guidance?"

"We do, although the amount of support depends on the programme."

Isabelle looked at the application guidelines.

"Could the foundation offer a simple record-keeping template?"

The manager considered it.

"We have discussed something similar."

"Would that help?"

"Potentially. But it would need to be tested with the organisations using it."

Isabelle nodded.

She did not know whether the idea would become anything.

But she had found something that interested her beyond the question of who her biological parents were.

She wanted to understand how institutions worked.

And how they could work better.

The next breakthrough came from an unexpected source.

A former administrative clerk at Vale Private Counsel had responded to Edward's request for information.

Her name was Laura Bennett.

She had worked in the firm's records department for less than two years and had left before the investigation became widely known.

She remembered Schedule 4.

Not its contents.

Its preparation.

Edward met her in a public office building.

Laura arrived carrying a thin envelope.

"I don't have the original document," she said immediately.

"What do you have?"

"A copy of the routing sheet."

She handed it to him.

The routing sheet contained a list of departments that had handled Schedule 4.

The first entry was Vale Private Counsel.

The second was Meridian Medical Centre.

The third was a private residential administration office.

The fourth was R-04.

But the fifth entry was different.

**AURELIUS FAMILY OFFICE — RECEIVING ACKNOWLEDGEMENT REQUESTED.**

Edward looked up.

"Was the schedule sent to the Aurelius Family Office?"

"According to this, yes."

"When?"

"Day 52."

"Who sent it?"

"Someone from the legal administration department."

"Was it received?"

Laura pointed to the next column.

**DELIVERY CONFIRMED.**

"Who acknowledged it?"

She shook her head.

"The name wasn't on the routing sheet."

Edward examined the copy.

The acknowledgement reference was:

**AFO/RCV/052.**

Jonathan looked at him.

"That could be important."

"Yes."

Edward turned back to Laura.

"Do you remember what happened afterward?"

"Not clearly. I remember a problem with the document."

"What problem?"

"It was returned."

"Why?"

Laura hesitated.

"I think the Family Office said they had not authorised the receiving arrangement."

Edward felt his attention sharpen.

"Did you see the return notice?"

"Yes."

"Do you remember its wording?"

"Not exactly."

"Anything?"

She thought for a moment.

"There was a sentence about the Family Office being an originating representative, not a receiving authority."

Edward remained still.

"Are you certain?"

"Not word for word. But I remember because someone in the office was annoyed that the schedule had been sent to them."

"Who?"

"Charles Vale."

Edward requested the original routing file.

Laura agreed to provide a written statement confirming what she remembered.

The routing sheet itself was already in the firm's archived records, but the acknowledgement and return notice had been separated.

The legal team obtained permission to inspect the correspondence.

The first document confirmed delivery to the Aurelius Family Office on day 52.

The second was a return acknowledgement dated day 54.

It contained a short statement.

*The Aurelius Family Office acknowledges receipt of the proposed schedule solely for preservation and review. Receipt does not constitute acceptance of any receiving identity, placement, or family allocation.*

Below it was a second paragraph.

*The Family Office's authority is limited to its role as originating family representative. It cannot act as receiving identity custodian.*

The document was signed by Helena Brooks.

Edward read it carefully.

Helena had not accepted the schedule.

She had explicitly rejected the implication that the Aurelius Family Office could serve as the receiving authority.

The return notice also contained an instruction.

*Any reference to the Aurelius family as a receiving party must be supported by independent receiving verification.*

Edward looked at Jonathan.

"This is important."

"Because the Aureliuses rejected the schedule?"

"Because they rejected the role assigned to them."

He placed the document beside the day-47 nomination register.

The same problem had appeared repeatedly.

The Family Office had been asked to act as an authority it did not possess.

The hospital had refused to certify identities without complete records.

The independent auditor had refused to certify proposed arrangements.

The trust had never received a valid custodian.

And still, the administrative schedule had continued moving.

Victoria Aurelius received the report that evening.

She read Helena's acknowledgement slowly.

Then she read it again.

"Alexander."

He looked up from his papers.

"Yes?"

"The Family Office rejected the receiving schedule."

He took the document.

"On day 54."

"Yes."

"Helena signed it."

"She did."

Victoria sat back.

For years, she had wondered whether someone had used the Aurelius name to move their daughter.

Now she had evidence that the Family Office had been presented with a proposed receiving arrangement and had refused to accept it.

It did not prove that their daughter had not been placed elsewhere.

But it removed one assumption.

The Aureliuses had not formally accepted a child under that schedule.

Alexander placed the document on the table.

"Someone may have continued using our name after the rejection."

Victoria looked at him.

"Or they may have used our name before we were ever asked."

"Both are possible."

She folded her hands.

"Then we need to know who received the schedule after it was returned."

Alexander nodded.

"And who acted on it."

Edward was asking the same question.

The routing sheet showed that Schedule 4 had been sent to the Aurelius Family Office on day 52 and returned on day 54.

But it had not returned to R-04.

It had been sent to a different address.

A private correspondence office associated with Vale Private Counsel.

The receiving stamp was dated day 55.

The person who signed for it had used only initials.

**C.V.**

Charles Vale.

Edward stared at the page.

Charles had received the schedule after the Aurelius Family Office explicitly refused to act as receiving custodian.

He had also known that the independent auditor would not certify it.

He had known that Margaret Voss refused.

He had known that no valid receiving authority existed.

Yet the schedule had continued circulating.

Edward called Jonathan.

"Find every copy of Schedule 4."

"All surviving versions?"

"Every version, including the ones held outside the hospitals and Family Office."

"Why?"

"Because I want to know whether Charles changed the schedule after the Aurelius rejection."

Jonathan was silent for a moment.

"Do you think he altered the family allocation?"

"I don't know."

Edward looked again at the return acknowledgement.

The Aurelius Family Office had rejected the schedule on day 54.

Charles had received it on day 55.

And on day 61, Dr Hale had returned Part One of Annex C to Adrian Holt.

Six days separated those events.

Six days in which someone had access to a schedule that no authorised person had accepted.

Edward closed the file.

"Find the day-55 copy."

That night, the trustee sent an additional notification.

A previously unindexed document had been discovered inside a separate correspondence bundle.

It was a one-page amendment to Schedule 4.

The document was dated day 56.

The title was simple.

**REVISION TO CURRENT FAMILY ALLOCATION.**

The first section retained the existing temporary care references.

The second section, however, contained a new instruction.

*Proposed receiving identities may be entered provisionally pending final verification.*

At the bottom was a signature.

Not Charles Vale's.

Not Adrian Holt's.

Not Marianne Cross's.

It belonged to someone who had never appeared in the earlier investigation.

Edward read the name.

**RICHARD VALMONT.**

For several seconds, he did not move.

Richard had always maintained that he had never authorised a child placement.

And the document was not a placement order.

But it appeared to carry his signature on an amendment allowing proposed receiving identities to be entered provisionally.

Edward looked at the date.

Day 56.

The same period in which the identity records were still incomplete.

He examined the signature carefully.

It looked genuine.

But the wording beneath it was troubling.

*Approved for administrative continuity only. No biological identity determination implied.*

The final line was handwritten.

**Do not convert provisional allocation into permanent family identity without independent confirmation.**

Edward leaned back.

Richard had apparently signed something.

But what exactly had he approved?

And had someone later removed the limitation that made his approval conditional?

The next interview would not be with another hospital administrator.

It would be with Richard Valmont himself.

Because eighteen years ago, someone had placed a document bearing his signature into the very process he now insisted he had never authorised.

And Edward needed to know whether his father had been misled, careless, or simply unaware of what his name had been used to approve.
