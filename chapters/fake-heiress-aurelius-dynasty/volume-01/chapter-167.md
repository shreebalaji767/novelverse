# **Chapter 167 — The Person Assigned to Explain**

Thomas Greer found the letter at 8:17 a.m.



It was not in the PR-06 folder.



It was not in the R.V. registration file.



It had been archived under a general correspondence category titled **COMMUNICATION RESPONSIBILITIES — PRELIMINARY ARRANGEMENTS**.



The classification explained why Edward had not found it earlier.



The letter concerned the protected principal's communication arrangements, but it had never been attached to the formal procedure.



Thomas sent a scanned copy with a brief message.



*I believe this is the correspondence I mentioned. The original is being preserved separately.*



Edward opened the attachment.



The date was day 29.



Two days before the R.V. route had been registered.



The sender was Eleanor Vale.



The recipient was the originating records authority.



The subject line read:



**Responsibility for Explaining Restricted External Communication Route**



Edward began reading.



*Before the proposed external route is activated, please identify the person responsible for explaining its purpose and limitations directly to the protected principal.*



A second paragraph followed.



*The route may receive and preserve restricted documents. It does not provide personal representation authority, does not confirm identity, and does not establish that the principal has received or understood any document placed within it.*



Edward stopped.



The wording was familiar.



It was the same distinction that had appeared in every later objection.



But this letter went further.



*The principal must be informed that the route exists, how it can be accessed, what it can and cannot do, and how to request direct communication with the originating office.*



The final paragraph was underlined.



*Please do not treat registration of the external recipient as completion of this responsibility.*



Edward read the letter again.



Then he checked the reply.



There was one.



It was dated day 30.



The originating records authority had responded:



*Responsibility for initial explanation will remain with the designated communication coordinator. The coordinator will arrange delivery of the necessary information through the existing protected channel.*



No name appeared.



No appointment reference appeared.



No delivery instruction was attached.



Edward opened the correspondence index.



There was no later confirmation.



He called Thomas.



"Who was the designated communication coordinator?"



"I don't know."



"Was that a formal position?"



"Not necessarily. It may have referred to someone assigned to manage the initial communication."



"Was the person named anywhere?"



"Possibly in the original channel configuration."



"Then why wasn't the name included in the response?"



"I can't answer that."



Edward looked at the final sentence.



*The coordinator will arrange delivery of the necessary information through the existing protected channel.*



The route did not yet exist.



It was registered the following day.



The letter did not identify a person.



And there was no evidence that the principal had ever received the explanation.



"Thomas, was Eleanor satisfied with the response?"



"I don't know."



"Did she follow up?"



"Let me check."



Edward waited.



Thomas returned several minutes later.



"Yes. Day 32."



"What did she say?"



"I'll send it."



The day-32 message was shorter.



*Thank you for confirming that a communication coordinator will be responsible for the initial explanation.*



Then:



*Please provide the coordinator's name, the date of appointment, and the method by which the principal's understanding of the arrangement will be recorded.*



Edward looked at the attachment list.



There were none.



The originating office had replied two days later.



*The communication arrangement remains in preparation. The coordinator's details will be included in the final implementation record.*



There was no final implementation record.



Edward leaned back.



He had now found three separate stages.



Day 29: Eleanor asked who would explain the route.



Day 30: the originating office promised that a coordinator would do so.



Day 32: Eleanor asked for the coordinator's name and proof of the explanation.



The office replied that the details would be recorded later.



They never were.



The R.V. route was registered on day 31, between those exchanges.



It had been created before the responsibility for explaining it had been documented.



Edward wrote the sequence on his notepad.



**Registration: day 31.**



**Coordinator: unidentified.**



**Initial explanation: unverified.**



**Principal's understanding: unrecorded.**



He circled the final two entries.



The system had created a route for a protected principal without establishing whether that principal knew the route existed.



It was possible that someone had explained it informally.



It was possible that the explanation had been delivered through another channel.



But the available records did not prove either possibility.



And without that proof, the later delivery receipts could not be treated as evidence that the principal had personally accessed anything.



Edward opened the investigation timeline.



A new question had emerged.



Who had been assigned to explain the route?

At the Valmont estate, Isabelle was preparing for a foundation review meeting.



She had arranged three application files on the dining table, each with a different coloured tab.



Catherine looked at them.



"Why are there three versions?"



"Because the first one is what the applicant submitted, the second is what the administrative office entered, and the third is the corrected version."



"Was the first one wrong?"



"Not exactly. The administrative office shortened the project description and removed the eligibility explanation."



"Why?"



"Character limit."



Catherine raised an eyebrow.



"That sounds familiar."



"It is. But the shortened version made the applicant's project look different from what they actually proposed."



Catherine sat opposite her.



"What did you do?"



"Asked them to confirm the revised description before it went to the reviewers."



"Good."



Isabelle looked at the files.



"I also added a field showing who made the change."



Catherine smiled faintly.



"You're learning."



"I was already learning."



"Of course."



Isabelle gave her a look.



Then she noticed Edward entering.



"You're early."



"I need to speak with you."



"That sounds ominous."



"It isn't about the foundation."



"That sounds more ominous."



Edward placed a copy of the day-29 letter on the table.



Catherine read the heading.



"Is this Eleanor's correspondence?"



"Yes."



Isabelle glanced at the page.



"What is it?"



"She asked who was responsible for explaining the R.V. communication route to the protected principal."



"And did they answer?"



"They said a designated coordinator would do it."



"Who?"



"Nobody was named."



Isabelle read the second page.



"So the route was created without a documented person responsible for explaining it."



"Correct."



"Was the principal informed?"



"We don't know."



Isabelle looked up.



"Then why did later records treat the route as if the principal could use it?"



Edward said nothing.



Catherine placed the letter down.



"Was the arrangement intended to protect the principal?"



"That was its stated purpose."



"Then someone should have made sure the principal understood it."



"That is precisely what Eleanor was asking."



Isabelle turned back to the letter.



"Did she receive an answer to her second question?"



"No."



"Then the office acknowledged the responsibility without identifying who would carry it out."



"Yes."



Isabelle considered the wording.



"That is worse than forgetting to fill in a field."



Edward looked at her.



"Why?"



"Because someone noticed the missing field and asked for it to be completed. The office promised to complete it later. Then the system continued."



She tapped the date.



"Whoever was responsible knew it was unresolved."



Edward nodded.



That was the problem.



It was not merely an accidental omission.



The omission had been raised.



It had been acknowledged.



And then it had been left unresolved while other parts of the arrangement proceeded.

At noon, Thomas called Edward back.



"I found another document," he said.



"About the coordinator?"



"Yes. It's a draft assignment sheet from day 30."



Edward opened the file.



The heading read:



**INITIAL COMMUNICATION RESPONSIBILITY — PROPOSED**



There were three fields.



**Communication coordinator:** Pending



**Principal notification method:** Pending



**Confirmation of understanding:** Pending



Beneath them was a handwritten instruction.



*Do not activate the route as a substitute for direct explanation.*



Edward recognised the phrase.



It was Eleanor's handwriting.



"Was this assignment sheet approved?"



"No."



"Was anyone named?"



"No."



"Was it attached to the day-31 registration?"



"No."



"Then why was the route activated?"



Thomas paused.



"The registration record says the external recipient's restricted access requirements were complete."



"That isn't the same as the principal's communication requirements."



"No."



Edward examined the sheet.



A box at the bottom contained a system status.



**EXTERNAL RECIPIENT CONFIGURATION: READY**



**PRINCIPAL COMMUNICATION: NOT READY**



The two statuses had been recorded separately.



Edward stared at them.



"Thomas, who changed the overall route status to active?"



"I don't know."



"Was it changed manually?"



"The status history may tell us."



"Find it."



Thomas promised to do so.



Edward ended the call and looked again at the assignment sheet.



This was the first document that clearly showed the difference between the external recipient being ready and the protected principal being informed.



Someone had known the difference.



Someone had recorded it.



And yet the route had still become active.



He made a note to request the complete status history.

In Singapore, Elena was sitting in a small university study room with her prototype open.



She had received a message from the academic programme office.



**Your application has passed the initial completeness check.**



A second line followed.



**Eligibility review is pending.**



She smiled.



Not a large smile.



Just enough to make Clara, sitting opposite her, notice.



"Good news?"



"Completeness check passed."



"See? Your application is progressing."



"One stage."



"One stage is better than zero."



Elena nodded.



She opened the application status page.



The system displayed four stages.



**Academic programme application**

1. **Submission received**

   Complete
2. **Document completeness check**

   Passed
3. **Eligibility review**

   Pending
4. **Final decision**

   Not started

Clara leaned over.



"That's actually quite clear."



"Yes."



"You're studying the status page like it's a work of art."



"It is a work of art."



"It's four boxes."



"Four useful boxes."



Elena clicked the eligibility stage.



A short explanation appeared.



*The application has been forwarded for eligibility assessment. This status does not indicate acceptance or rejection.*



Elena nodded.



"Exactly."



Clara gave her a curious look.



"What?"



"Nothing. I just like systems that don't pretend a process has finished when it hasn't."



Clara returned to her notes.



Elena opened her prototype again.



The same principle had to be built into her platform.



An application could be received.



A reviewer could be assigned.



A document could be checked.



But none of those events meant the applicant had been accepted.



She added a new requirement to the development list:



**Status labels must describe completed actions, not imply outcomes.**



Then she added another:



**Any change to an application status must retain the previous value, the new value, the timestamp, and the responsible account.**



She stared at the last phrase.



Responsible account.



That was not enough.



If several people shared one account, the audit trail could still fail to identify who made the change.



She added:



**Where shared accounts are permitted, individual operator identification must be retained separately.**



Clara looked over.



"Another feature?"



"Another problem I don't want to create."

At 2:35, Jonathan delivered the status history.



Edward opened it immediately.



The route had been activated on day 31 at 11:08 a.m.



The status change had been entered through a shared administrative account.



**VPC-ADMIN-04**



The entry contained a short note:



*External recipient configuration complete. Route activated for continuity.*



Edward checked the status before the change.



**External recipient configuration:** Ready.



**Principal communication:** Not ready.



**Overall route status:** Pending.



The next entry was the change.



**Overall route status:** Active.



There was no separate confirmation that the principal communication field had changed.



It had remained marked **NOT READY**.



Edward stared at the screen.



The route had not been activated because the principal had been informed.



It had been activated because the external recipient was ready.



The system had allowed an overall status to become active while one of its essential components remained incomplete.



"Who authorised the status change?" Edward asked.



Jonathan checked the record.



"No named authoriser."



"Was it an automatic system action?"



"No. The log identifies a manual status update."



"Then who entered it?"



"The shared account."



"Which operator?"



"No individual operator field."



Edward stood.



"Again."



Jonathan nodded.



"Again."



Edward looked at the next entry.



Day 33.



PR-06 procedure prepared.



Day 35.



Restricted preliminary review copy distributed.



Day 42.



Verification key generated.



Day 44.



Control copy collected by Charles Vale.



The route had become active before the original procedure was ready.



The procedure had then been prepared.



But there was still no proof that the principal had been told how to access the route.



And no proof that the route's external recipient had personally received the documents.



Edward turned to Jonathan.



"Find out whether the status change was challenged."



Jonathan searched the record.



"There is one note from day 32."



"Who wrote it?"



"The system identifies the originating records authority."



"What does it say?"



Jonathan read aloud.



*Overall activation is premature while principal communication remains outstanding. The route may be prepared, but its operational use must remain restricted until the initial explanation is completed.*



Edward took the page.



"Who submitted this?"



"Not identified in the ordinary register."



"Was it accepted?"



"No."



"Was it rejected?"



"Not formally."



"What happened?"



"The status remained active."



Edward looked at the date.



Day 32.



One day after activation.



The objection had been raised almost immediately.



Someone had recognised the problem.



But the route had remained active.

At 5:10, Edward received a call from Rachel Dunn, the independent preservation officer.



"I have an additional record relating to your request," she said.



"About the day-31 activation?"



"Yes. It was included in the original preservation material but not in the ordinary configuration extract."



"Can you send it?"



"I can provide a description first."



"Go ahead."



"The record is a handwritten instruction attached to the day-32 objection."



"Whose handwriting?"



"We cannot identify it from the available samples."



"What does it say?"



Rachel read slowly.



*The route may remain prepared for preservation purposes. It must not be treated as operational for personal communication until the principal has received an explanation and the means of direct access.*



Edward closed his eyes briefly.



The instruction was consistent with Eleanor's position.



But the handwriting had not been identified.



"Was it followed?"



"No."



"How do you know?"



"Because the overall route status remained active, and later records continued to use the external channel."



"Was the instruction ever removed?"



"No. It remained attached to the preserved original."



"Was it included in the distributed summary?"



"No."



Edward looked at the day-142 meeting record on his desk.



Another warning omitted.



Another original preserved.



Another administrative summary that gave a more convenient impression than the underlying documents supported.



"Ms Dunn, I need the complete attachment."



"I will send it through the restricted records process."



"Thank you."



After the call ended, Edward looked at Jonathan.



"There's something I want to establish."



"What?"



"Whether the person who activated the route was the same person who later changed R-17's status."



Jonathan nodded.



"The shared account was used for both."



"That is not enough. We need individual operator records."



"There may not be any."



"Then we need to establish whether they were deliberately omitted or never collected."



Jonathan took the instruction.



"I'll investigate."



Edward looked down at the status history.



The route had been activated.



The principal communication had remained incomplete.



The objection had been filed.



The warning had been preserved.



And still, the system had proceeded.



He had spent months trying to understand who had converted temporary custody into personal representation authority.



Now he was beginning to see that the conversion might have been only one part of a much earlier decision.



The first questionable step had occurred before Charles Vale collected the PR-06 control copy.



Before R-17 was recorded as declined.



Before the replacement search.



Before the altered meeting summary.



It had occurred when someone marked a communication route active despite a recorded warning that the principal had not been informed.



Edward opened the original day-31 registration again.



At the bottom was a field he had not previously examined.



**ACTIVATION BASIS: EXTERNAL RECIPIENT READY.**



Beneath it, in smaller type:



**PRINCIPAL COMMUNICATION REQUIREMENT: TO BE COMPLETED UNDER SEPARATE RESPONSIBILITY.**



The system had not confused the two requirements.



It had recorded them separately.



Someone had chosen to proceed anyway.



And the question Edward now needed answered was no longer simply who had operated the shared account.



It was who had decided that the principal's missing explanation could be left for later.
