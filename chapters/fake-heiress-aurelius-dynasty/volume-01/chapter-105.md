# **Chapter 105 — Patricia Moore's Second Statement**

Patricia Moore had always disliked being described as a secretary.

She had been a junior administrative clerk at Vale Private Counsel when the infant records were being transferred. She had processed correspondence, maintained registers, prepared schedules, and handled instructions from people whose titles were considerably more impressive than hers.

But she had never considered herself a decision-maker.

That distinction had shaped every statement she had given Edward.

She had followed instructions.

She had prepared documents.

She had corrected errors when she noticed them.

And she had not understood the larger implications of the work.

Edward intended to find out whether that account was accurate.

Patricia arrived at the meeting accompanied by her solicitor.

She was in her late forties now, with short hair and a composed expression. She carried a folder containing copies of her previous statements.

“Mr Valmont,” she said.

“Thank you for coming.”

“I understand this concerns the old Northbridge records.”

“It concerns the OR-28 independent review.”

Her expression changed slightly.

“I thought that matter had already been discussed.”

“We have found the original R-04 distribution register.”

Patricia placed her folder on the table.

“And?”

“It confirms the independent review was never completed. It also confirms that the receiving identity custodian was never appointed.”

“I believe I said that previously.”

“You did.”

“Then I don't understand why we're meeting again.”

Edward placed a copy of the access log in front of her.

“Because the administrative status was changed.”

Patricia looked at the page.

For several seconds, she said nothing.

Then she turned it toward her solicitor.

“I see.”

“Do you recognise the approval code PM-06?”

“Yes.”

“What does it refer to?”

“My supervisor approval code.”

“Was it assigned exclusively to you?”

“Yes.”

“Could another person use it?”

“Not without my authorisation.”

Edward waited.

“Did you approve the OR-28 status change on day fifty-six?”

Patricia looked at the log again.

“I don't remember the specific entry.”

“Did you have authority to approve it?”

“I had authority to approve administrative status changes.”

“Even when the underlying review had not been completed?”

“That depended on the type of change.”

“Was this one permitted?”

Patricia did not answer immediately.

“I would need to see the original instruction.”

Edward slid the preserved correspondence across the table.

It was Eleanor's query, the reply from VPC-ADMIN-04, and the original R-04 acknowledgement.

Patricia read them carefully.

Then she looked up.

“The account was not supposed to close the review.”

“Yet it was used to record the review as administratively closed.”

“I can see that.”

“Did you approve it?”

“I need to distinguish between approving a document and approving its contents.”

Edward remained silent.

Patricia continued.

“I may have approved the processing of a status update. That does not necessarily mean I authored the wording.”

“Who did?”

“I don't remember.”

“Did Charles Vale instruct you?”

“He gave instructions about the general handling of the records.”

“Did he tell you to mark OR-28 closed?”

“No.”

“Did he tell you that the independent review was no longer required?”

“He said the administrative process was continuing under Schedule 4.”

“That is not the same thing.”

“I understand that now.”

Edward leaned forward.

“Did you understand it then?”

Patricia looked down.

“I understood that there were outstanding requirements.”

“Which requirements?”

“The independent review and receiving identity confirmation.”

“Then why did you approve a status change suggesting the review was closed?”

“I did not approve a statement that the identities had been confirmed.”

“You approved a statement that the review was administratively closed.”

“Those are different things.”

“Were they different to the people reading the record?”

Patricia had no immediate answer.

Edward opened another folder.

It contained the original Schedule 4 revisions.

“Let's discuss day fifty-six.”

Patricia's eyes moved to the document.

“You have already asked me about that.”

“Yes. You told us Charles Vale instructed you to combine Richard Valmont's temporary-care approval with a revised family allocation schedule.”

“That is correct.”

“Did you assemble the document?”

“I assembled the administrative copy.”

“Did you know Richard Valmont had approved only temporary-care preservation?”

“Yes.”

“Did you know the revised schedule included proposed permanent family allocation?”

“I knew there was a section for it.”

“Did you know his signature was not approval of that section?”

Patricia hesitated.

“I understood that his signature related to the temporary-care arrangements.”

“Then why did you place it on the revised schedule?”

“Because the document was being assembled as a consolidated administrative record.”

“Who instructed you to do that?”

“Charles Vale.”

“Did he tell you to make it appear that Richard had approved the family allocation?”

“No.”

“Did he tell you to leave the allocation section blank?”

“Yes.”

Edward placed the examiner's report beside the document.

“The examiner found that the page had been assembled from two separate sheets. The signature was genuine, but it had been attached to a different page.”

Patricia examined the report.

“That was an administrative consolidation.”

“Was Richard Valmont asked to approve the consolidated document?”

“I don't know.”

“Did you send it to him?”

“No. I received the approval and prepared the consolidated copy.”

“Did you verify that the signature remained attached to the correct authorisation?”

Patricia's expression tightened.

“No.”

Edward let the silence remain.

That was the first clear admission.

She had not forged Richard's signature.

But she had assembled a document in a way that could make a genuine signature appear to authorise something it did not.

“Now let's return to day fifty-six,” Edward said.

Patricia looked tired.

“Do we have to?”

“Yes.”

He placed the terminal access log before her again.

“Terminal Two. Family Allocation Desk. VPC-ADMIN-04. Supervisor approval PM-06.”

Patricia read it.

“Who prepared the status wording?”

“I don't remember.”

“Who entered it?”

“I don't know.”

“Who approved it?”

“I may have approved the processing.”

“What did you believe you were approving?”

“A closure of the administrative review task.”

“What did that mean?”

“That the external review process had been suspended and the documents would continue under Schedule 4.”

“Was the independent review actually completed?”

“No.”

“Was the receiving identity custodian appointed?”

“No.”

“Then why was the status recorded as closed?”

Patricia took a breath.

“Because the review task had been suspended.”

“Suspended and completed are not the same thing.”

“I know that.”

“Did you know that on day fifty-six?”

“Yes.”

Edward looked at her.

She had changed her answer.

“Then why did you approve the entry?”

Patricia's solicitor interrupted.

“My client has answered that she does not recall approving the specific wording.”

Edward nodded.

“Then I will ask a different question. Did you have the authority to approve a closure that implied the independent review was no longer required?”

Patricia looked at the solicitor, then back at Edward.

“No.”

“Did you approve it anyway?”

“I cannot confirm that I approved the exact wording.”

Edward closed the folder.

He had not obtained a confession.

But he had obtained something useful.

Patricia acknowledged that she understood the distinction between suspension and completion, and that she did not have authority to declare the independent review unnecessary.

The access log still needed independent verification.

And her involvement in the administrative process was no longer merely clerical.

Jonathan entered with another document.

“There's a problem with the terminal log.”

Edward looked up.

“What?”

“The archive copy is incomplete.”

Patricia glanced at the page.

Jonathan continued.

“It records the supervisor approval code, but not the individual login session. The original system had a separate access card record.”

“Where is it?”

“Northbridge retained the access card register. But the electronic session records were transferred to a different preservation archive.”

“Which one?”

“Vale Private Counsel's successor records office.”

Edward looked at Patricia.

“Do you know where those records were stored?”

She shook her head.

“No.”

“Were you ever issued a personal access card?”

“Yes.”

“Did it carry the PM-06 code?”

“Yes.”

“Could it have been used by another person?”

“My card was assigned to me. I was expected to keep it secure.”

“Did you ever lend it?”

“No.”

“Did anyone else have access to your workstation?”

Patricia paused.

“Yes.”

“Who?”

“My supervisor. Charles Vale's office also had administrative access.”

“Could they use your account?”

“Not without my credentials.”

Edward wrote that down.

“Did you ever leave your card at the terminal?”

“I don't remember.”

“Did you ever give someone your password?”

“No.”

“Did anyone ask you to?”

Patricia looked at the table.

“Not that I recall.”

Edward noticed the hesitation.

“What is it?”

She rubbed her forehead.

“There was one occasion.”

“When?”

“During the week the Schedule 4 records were being consolidated.”

“What happened?”

“My terminal had stopped recognising my access card. I was asked to leave the card with the systems administrator so the access permissions could be checked.”

“Who asked?”

“I don't remember his name.”

“Was he employed by Northbridge?”

“I believe so.”

“Did you receive the card back?”

“Yes.”

“Did you know whether it had been used while it was out of your possession?”

“No.”

Edward looked at Jonathan.

That possibility changed the investigation.

The approval code identified Patricia's account.

It did not necessarily establish that Patricia had personally used it.

But she had been responsible for safeguarding the card.

And someone had apparently had access to it.

After Patricia left, Jonathan reviewed the access records again.

“Do you think she's telling the truth?”

Edward considered the question.

“I think she is being careful.”

“That's not an answer.”

“It's the only answer we have.”

Jonathan placed the interview notes beside the terminal log.

“She admits she knew the review was incomplete. She admits she approved administrative processing. But she won't confirm approving the closure wording.”

“And she says her card was once out of her possession.”

“Could be an excuse.”

“Could be true.”

Jonathan leaned back.

“What do we do?”

“Find the systems administrator.”

“Northbridge has changed owners twice.”

“Then find the original staff register.”

Jonathan nodded.

Edward looked at the access code.

PM-06.

It was a small designation.

A few letters and numbers.

But it had appeared in connection with a decision that had helped turn an incomplete process into an apparently completed one.

He wanted to know who had actually used it.

At the Aurelius Estate, Adrian was reviewing a different part of the investigation.

He had asked Helena Brooks to prepare a chronology of every document the Family Office had received during the original period.

The result was a long table.

Some entries were routine.

Some were incomplete.

Several were marked preservation only.

He stopped at day fifty-two.

**Schedule 4 — Current Care Arrangements.**

**Received from Vale Private Counsel.**

**Purpose: Preservation and review.**

**Receiving identity: Not accepted.**

Adrian read the entry.

“Why was this sent to us?”

Helena sat opposite him.

“Because the Aurelius Family Office had been listed as a proposed originating representative.”

“Not a receiving family.”

“Correct.”

“Who authorised the preservation copy?”

“General records authority. It was received by the archive department.”

“Did anyone in the family see it?”

“I cannot confirm that.”

Adrian looked at the next entry.

Day fifty-four.

**Return acknowledgement: Preservation accepted. Receiving identity not accepted.**

He tapped the table.

“Then why did Vale Private Counsel continue using our office name in the recipient field?”

“Because the original schedule had not been corrected.”

“Or because someone wanted the record to look as though we had accepted responsibility.”

Helena did not disagree.

Adrian closed the file.

“Send a formal notice to Vale's successor office. Every record referring to the Aurelius Family Office as a receiving family must be corrected or supported by an original acceptance document.”

“I'll arrange it.”

“And make sure they understand that preservation receipt is not acceptance of a child.”

“I will.”

Adrian looked toward the window.

His family's search had become an exercise in separating what they had actually authorised from what other people had claimed they had authorised.

It was an exhausting distinction.

But he would not allow another false certainty to enter the investigation.

That evening, Victoria found Julian sitting in the family library.

He had a notebook open in front of him.

“What are you working on?”

Julian immediately closed it.

“Nothing.”

Victoria smiled.

“An interesting nothing.”

“It's private.”

“Then I won't ask.”

He looked at her.

“You're not going to ask?”

“No.”

Julian considered this disappointing outcome.

Then he opened the notebook again.

“It's a list.”

Victoria sat beside him.

“Of what?”

“Things I want to do with my sister.”

Victoria looked at the page.

There were forty-nine numbered items.

Some were ordinary.

Some were ridiculous.

Some were surprisingly thoughtful.

“Have you added anything recently?”

“Not yet.”

“Why not?”

“Because I don't know what she likes.”

Victoria looked at him gently.

“Then perhaps you should leave some spaces.”

Julian frowned.

“Why?”

“So she can decide.”

He considered that.

Then he took his pen and wrote a new line at the bottom.

**50. Ask her what she wants to do, instead of deciding everything myself.**

Victoria smiled.

“That's a good addition.”

Julian looked at the page.

“I think I can manage that.”

She rested a hand briefly on his shoulder.

“I'm sure you can.”

The next morning, Jonathan received the Northbridge staff register.

The systems administrator's name was listed.

**Martin Keane — Records Systems Supervisor.**

He had worked at Northbridge during the period when the OR-28 status was changed.

His personnel file showed that he had left the organisation several years later.

His last known employer was a private data preservation company.

Edward examined the employment history.

There was something else.

Martin Keane had been transferred to Northbridge from Vale Private Counsel on day thirty.

He had not been an ordinary Northbridge employee.

He had been seconded from Charles Vale's office.

Jonathan looked at the file.

“So he was already familiar with VPC-ADMIN-04.”

“Yes.”

“And he had access to the system.”

“Yes.”

“Did he have authority to approve family allocation records?”

“No. He was a systems supervisor.”

“Could he use Patricia's approval card?”

“We don't know.”

Edward turned to the final page.

Martin Keane's transfer record included a handwritten instruction.

**Temporary access to restricted family allocation terminals permitted for system maintenance.**

The authorisation carried Charles Vale's office reference.

Edward read the note again.

A systems administrator from Charles's office had been given access to the family allocation terminal.

Patricia's card had been temporarily removed from her possession.

The shared account had been used to alter the OR-28 status.

And the supervisor approval code belonged to Patricia.

There were now two possible explanations.

Patricia had approved the change herself.

Or someone with access to the terminal had used her credentials to make the change appear authorised.

Edward picked up his phone.

“Find Martin Keane,” he told Jonathan.

“And Patricia?”

“Don't approach her again yet.”

“Why?”

“Because I want to know whether Martin remembers the day he borrowed her card.”

Edward looked at the personnel record.

The last note on the page was dated day sixty-one.

**System maintenance completed. Access permissions restored.**

Below it was a handwritten remark.

**Original user credentials retained for continuity.**

Edward went still.

“Jonathan.”

“Yes?”

“Read that line again.”

Jonathan leaned over.

His expression changed.

The credentials had supposedly been restored to Patricia after maintenance.

But the note said they had been retained.

Not returned.

Retained for continuity.

Which meant the person who had altered the records might not have needed Patricia's approval at all.

He might have been able to use her authority whenever he wanted.

And if that was true, then the initials PM-06 did not tell them who had made the decision.

They told them whose authority had been used.
