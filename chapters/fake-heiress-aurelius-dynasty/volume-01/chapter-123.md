# **Chapter 123 — The Original Card Anna Refused to Ignore**

Anna Mercer-Smith arrived twenty minutes early.

Edward noticed this before he noticed anything else.

She entered the private meeting room carrying a small handbag and a folder secured with an elastic band. Her hair was shorter than it had been in the photograph from her employment file, and she wore simple glasses.

She did not look like someone who had spent eighteen years hiding a secret.

She looked like someone who had spent eighteen years trying not to be asked about it.

Jonathan introduced himself.

Anna acknowledged him with a brief nod.

Then she turned to Edward.

“Did you bring the original?”

“Yes.”

“Not a copy?”

“The recovered original card.”

“Has it been examined since recovery?”

“Yes. Its physical condition and preservation history have been documented.”

Anna placed her folder on the table.

“Then I will tell you what I remember.”

Edward did not immediately open his document case.

“Before we begin, I want to establish something. We are investigating the handling of two newborns and the records associated with them. We have not established either child's biological identity.”

Anna nodded.

“Good.”

“Do you understand that?”

“I understand it better than most people who handled those files.”

Edward studied her expression.

“Then let's begin.”

The original St Catherine's card was placed on the table.

Anna did not touch it.

She leaned forward and read the identification fields.

**INFANT A**

**Female**

**02:17 a.m.**

**STC-A**

She looked at the reverse.

Then at the lower edge.

“That's the original.”

Edward watched her carefully.

“How can you tell?”

“The card stock.”

She pointed to a faint blue line near the lower corner.

“St Catherine's used two different forms during that period. The later administrative copies had a printed transfer box. The original neonatal cards did not.”

Jonathan leaned closer.

“Could that distinction have been reproduced?”

“Possibly. But this card has the original pressure marks from the hospital's registration stamp.”

Anna indicated the faint impression.

“Look here.”

Dr Sen's earlier examination had recorded the same mark, but Anna identified something different.

“The stamp was applied before the card was separated from the maternal record packet.”

Edward frowned.

“Was that standard procedure?”

“Yes. The original neonatal card and the corresponding maternal record were prepared as part of one registration process. They could later be separated for medical or administrative reasons, but the initial registration happened together.”

“Does that mean this card belonged to Victoria Aurelius?”

Anna shook her head.

“No.”

The answer came immediately.

“It means the card was originally prepared in the same registration process as the maternal record associated with Infant A. It does not prove the child later transferred under that reference was the same child.”

Edward nodded.

“Explain the difference.”

Anna looked at the card.

“The card records an infant at a particular moment. Once that infant is transferred, the receiving institution must establish that the child arriving is the same child who left. That requires an uninterrupted identification process.”

“And St Catherine's?”

“St Catherine's began that process.”

“Did it finish?”

“No.”

Edward leaned forward.

“How do you know?”

Anna pointed to the reverse of the card.

A small box contained a handwritten entry.

**Original receiving identification: pending.**

“That's the problem,” she said.

Anna opened her folder.

Inside were several old administrative forms.

She placed one beside the original card.

“This was the transfer checklist used by St Catherine's.”

Edward examined it.

The form had five sections:

1. Infant identification.
2. Maternal record reference.
3. Medical condition at transfer.
4. Receiving facility acknowledgement.
5. Receiving identity continuity confirmation.

The fifth section had two separate signatures.

One from the releasing institution.

One from the receiving institution.

Anna pointed to the final section.

“Both had to confirm the identification chain.”

“Was that completed for Infant A?”

“No.”

“Why?”

“The receiving acknowledgement was incomplete.”

Edward looked at her.

“Was the child physically transferred?”

“Yes. A child was transferred under the administrative reference.”

“Then why wasn't the identification chain completed?”

Anna hesitated.

“Because the receiving facility did not receive the complete originating material.”

“Which material was missing?”

“The original neonatal identification card and the corresponding maternal record.”

Edward glanced at Jonathan.

The investigation had already established that the original card was withheld from the sixth-day comparison dispatch.

But Anna was explaining why that mattered.

“Was the receiving facility aware?”

“I believe so.”

“Which facility?”

“Northbridge Clinical Review Unit.”

Edward opened his notebook.

“Who informed them?”

“Marianne Cross delivered the transfer material. I believe Gabriel Vale later collected the original card.”

“That is consistent with the records.”

Anna nodded.

“But the important point is that a receiving acknowledgement does not establish identity continuity.”

Edward looked at her.

“Did you tell Charles Vale that?”

“Yes.”

“What did he say?”

“He said the children were receiving care and that the administrative records could be reconciled later.”

“Did you agree?”

“No.”

“Why not?”

“Because the longer an unidentified child remains under a temporary administrative reference, the easier it becomes for that reference to be treated as the child's identity.”

She looked directly at Edward.

“That is exactly what happened.”

Edward placed the day-40 conditional response before her.

“Did you write this?”

Anna read it.

“No.”

“Did you deliver it?”

“No.”

“Do you know who did?”

“I know the person who brought it to V-17.”

“Marianne Cross?”

Anna nodded.

“She was carrying a sealed envelope. I did not see the contents.”

“Did you know the sender?”

“I knew who had asked for the response to be prepared.”

“Who?”

Anna looked at the document.

“Margaret Voss.”

Edward's pen stopped.

“Margaret Voss?”

“Yes.”

“Are you certain?”

“I met her at St Catherine's. She was responsible for the restricted records register.”

“Did she write the response?”

“I don't know.”

“Did she ask you to deliver it?”

“No. She asked that the records remain separate until the originating identification had been established.”

Edward placed the day-40 cover sheet beside the statement.

“Why was the sender's identity withheld?”

“Because the response was not supposed to become part of the ordinary recipient file.”

“Why not?”

“Because it contained information about a proposed receiving arrangement, and the person responding had not been given the child's verified identity.”

Edward looked at her.

“Was that person Margaret Voss?”

Anna shook her head.

“I don't know.”

“Then how do you know Margaret requested the response?”

“Because she asked me to prepare the wording.”

“What wording?”

Anna took a folded sheet from her folder.

It was an old handwritten draft.

The paper had been preserved in a plastic sleeve.

At the top was a reference:

**OR-28 / CONDITIONAL RESPONSE**

The wording matched the day-40 statement almost exactly.

The signature field was blank.

Edward read the first sentence.

*I am willing to consider further information once the originating identity and authority of the proposed arrangement have been independently established.*

He looked at Anna.

“Who wrote this?”

“Margaret Voss dictated it. I wrote it down.”

“Was the final statement signed in your presence?”

“No.”

“Did you know who signed it?”

“No.”

“Then how did it reach Cross Administrative Services?”

“I gave the draft to Margaret. She said the response would be arranged through the protected correspondence channel.”

Edward studied the paper.

“Why did you keep this?”

“Because I was concerned the wording would be changed.”

“Did you believe it might be changed?”

“I had already seen people change the meaning of administrative documents by removing one sentence.”

She looked at him.

“I wanted the original wording preserved.”

Jonathan examined the draft.

“Why does it refer to OR-28?”

“Because the response was connected to the independent recipient review,” Anna said.

“Was it connected to Child 28-B?”

“Not necessarily.”

Edward nodded.

“Explain.”

“OR-28 was a review of proposed receiving identities. It included several records. Child 28-B was one of the administrative references, but the review was not limited to that child.”

“Did Margaret tell you which child the proposed recipient was being asked to receive?”

“No.”

“Did she tell you the biological family?”

“No.”

“Did she tell you whether the proposed recipient was a relative?”

“No.”

Anna paused.

“She was very particular about that.”

“About what?”

“She said that the proposed recipient must not be told they were receiving a child from a particular family until the originating records had been verified.”

Edward looked down at the draft.

“Did she believe the proposed recipient might be willing to accept the child?”

“I don't know.”

“Did she believe the proposed recipient had already agreed?”

“No. She told me the response was conditional.”

“Did she say who the proposed recipient was?”

Anna shook her head.

“Not to me.”

Edward turned to another document.

It was the original restricted register entry from Margaret Voss.

**OR-28 — Recipient response requested.**

**Status: conditional.**

**Permanent appointment: not authorised.**

**Originating verification: incomplete.**

A later line had been added.

**Response received through CA-17.**

Edward pointed to it.

“Was this written by Margaret?”

Anna examined the handwriting.

“I believe so.”

“Can you be certain?”

“No.”

Edward placed the entry beside the day-47 administrative summary.

The summary stated:

**Alternate recipient retained for continuity. Consent recorded. Further independent review not required for administrative preservation.**

Anna read it.

Her expression changed.

“Who wrote this?”

“We have not established that.”

“This is wrong.”

Edward looked at her.

“Which part?”

“All of it.”

She pointed to the second sentence.

“Consent recorded.”

Then the third.

“Further independent review not required.”

She looked up.

“The response was conditional. It was not consent. And the independent review was not completed.”

Edward remained silent.

Anna returned to the first sentence.

“Retained for continuity?”

“That was the administrative description.”

“It should have said the proposal remained under consideration.”

“Was that the wording Margaret used?”

“Yes.”

Anna's voice remained calm, but her expression had hardened.

“Someone changed a conditional response into consent.”

Edward placed the day-47 summary aside.

“Did you confront Charles Vale?”

“Yes.”

“When?”

“After I learned the administrative summary had been issued.”

“What did he say?”

“He said the summary did not determine identity.”

“Did you accept that explanation?”

“No.”

“Why?”

“Because the wording could still be used to justify later action.”

Anna folded her hands.

“I told him that if the recipient's consent had not been verified, the record must say so. He said the file was being closed for administrative continuity, not identity determination.”

Edward looked at her.

“Did he say the recipient had been notified?”

“He said the proposed recipient had been approached.”

“Was that true?”

“I don't know.”

“Did you ask?”

“Yes.”

“And?”

“He said the communication had been handled through the appropriate office.”

“Which office?”

“He did not specify.”

Edward glanced at Jonathan.

The statement echoed the earlier uncertainty surrounding the day-118 notification.

A communication could be prepared, a response could be received, and a status could be recorded without anyone establishing that the correct person had been informed.

“Did you believe Charles was deliberately misleading people?” Edward asked.

Anna considered the question.

“I believed he was trying to keep an arrangement moving despite the fact that its conditions had not been satisfied.”

“That isn't the same thing.”

“No.”

She looked at him.

“Charles believed the children needed stable arrangements. He was not wrong about that. But he began treating administrative continuity as though it could substitute for verified identity.”

Edward wrote the sentence down.

It was a more nuanced explanation than a simple accusation.

Charles might have believed he was preventing children from remaining in temporary care indefinitely.

But his decisions had allowed temporary references and proposed arrangements to acquire the appearance of permanence.

The meeting continued for another hour.

Anna identified several records in the file.

She recognised the original St Catherine's transfer checklist.

She confirmed that the restricted neonatal card had been withheld from comparison.

She identified Margaret Voss's handwriting on the OR-28 draft.

She confirmed that the day-40 statement was based on Margaret's wording.

But she could not identify the person who signed the final response.

Nor could she establish which child the proposed recipient had been asked to consider receiving.

Edward closed his notebook.

“One final question. Why did you leave St Catherine's?”

Anna looked toward the window.

“Because I was asked to certify a transfer that I could not verify.”

“Which transfer?”

“The original neonatal identification material.”

“Did you refuse?”

“Yes.”

“What happened?”

“My assignment was ended.”

“By whom?”

“The hospital administration.”

“Was Charles Vale involved?”

“He requested that my access be restricted while the matter was reviewed.”

“Did you appeal?”

“No.”

“Why not?”

Anna looked down at her hands.

“Because I had no proof that the child had been misidentified. I only knew the records were incomplete.”

Edward nodded.

“And after you left?”

“I kept copies of the documents I was authorised to retain. I did not remove patient records.”

“Did you ever contact either family?”

“No.”

“Did you know the Aurelius family was searching?”

“Not at the time.”

“Did you know the Valmont family?”

“No.”

Edward stood.

“Thank you for meeting us.”

Anna did not immediately rise.

“There is something I want to make clear.”

Edward turned back.

“I never saw a document proving that Infant A was Victoria Aurelius's biological child. I never saw a document proving that IV-204 belonged to the same infant. And I never saw a document proving that Child 28-B was either of them.”

“We understand.”

“Good.”

She closed her folder.

“Because I have seen people build entire conclusions from a number written on a piece of paper.”

The interview report was completed that evening.

Edward summarised the findings.

**Established:**

- Anna Mercer-Smith worked as a temporary records liaison at St Catherine's.
- Her access to neonatal records was administrative and restricted.
- She knew the original neonatal identification material had not been included in the sixth-day comparison dispatch.
- She refused to certify identity continuity without the original records.
- She prepared the original wording of the OR-28 conditional response at Margaret Voss's request.
- The response was conditional, not acceptance or consent.
- She did not sign the final response.
- She could not identify the proposed recipient.
- She could not establish the biological identity of any child.

Edward added one final note.

**Unresolved:**

*Who signed the conditional response, and why was the original wording changed to “consent recorded”?*

Jonathan read the report.

“Margaret Voss is the next person.”

“Yes.”

“Do you think she signed it?”

“I don't know.”

“Anna believes she requested it.”

“That doesn't mean she signed it.”

Jonathan closed the folder.

“What about the original response?”

“We need to ask Margaret whether she retained a signed copy.”

Edward looked at the recovered draft.

The words had been carefully chosen.

They were not an acceptance.

They were a request for information.

And someone had later turned them into something else.

At the Singapore campus, Elena sat in the university library with her laptop open.

Her first orientation session had finished.

She had met students from several countries, collected her programme documents, and spent nearly an hour trying to locate the correct department building.

The campus was larger than she had expected.

She had also discovered that the library had several floors dedicated to different academic disciplines.

That alone had improved her opinion of the university.

Her phone vibrated.

A message from Isabelle.

**I read your comments on the foundation proposal. I made the changes to the eligibility review section.**

Elena opened the attachment.

The revisions were thoughtful.

She typed a reply.

**This is much clearer. I like that you've separated initial screening from final approval. It will make responsibility easier to track.**

Isabelle replied:

**I was thinking about what you said. A person should know whether they're reviewing an application or actually approving it.**

Elena smiled.

**Exactly. Those are different responsibilities.**

She put the phone aside.

Then she returned to her own project.

The first prototype was not ready.

The interface needed work, the document tracking function was incomplete, and the audit log still had several problems.

But she had time.

For the first time, her schedule was being shaped by her own decisions rather than by a family crisis.

She opened a new page.

**Prototype priorities — Week One.**

And began writing.

At the Aurelius Estate, Victoria received the interview summary.

She read the established findings first.

Then the unresolved questions.

“Anna prepared the response wording?” she asked.

Alexander nodded.

“Under Margaret Voss's instruction.”

“Did she know which child the proposed recipient was being asked to consider?”

“No.”

Victoria looked troubled.

“So even the person preparing the correspondence didn't know.”

“Apparently.”

She returned to the report.

“The original card was withheld from comparison because the records were incomplete.”

“Yes.”

“And nobody completed the comparison afterward.”

“Not successfully.”

Victoria closed the file.

For a moment, neither spoke.

Then she asked, “How many people knew that the records were incomplete?”

Alexander considered the question.

“More than we initially believed.”

“And how many of them were allowed to make decisions anyway?”

“Some made administrative decisions. Others tried to prevent those decisions from being treated as final.”

Victoria looked toward the garden.

“I wonder whether our daughter was ever treated as a person in those offices.”

Alexander did not answer immediately.

“I hope she was.”

Victoria nodded.

“So do I.”

Edward was preparing the next interview request when a message arrived from Margaret Voss.

She had received a copy of the Anna Mercer-Smith interview summary.

Her reply contained only two sentences.

**I did not sign the final conditional response.**

**But I know who did.**

Edward read the message twice.

He called Jonathan.

“Prepare the original OR-28 correspondence file.”

“Why?”

Edward looked at the message.

“Because Margaret has finally identified the person behind the signature.”

He paused.

“And we need to establish whether that person had any authority to speak for the proposed recipient.”

The conditional response had never been consent.

The original review had never been completed.

And the person who signed the response might have been acting for someone who had never been properly informed.

Edward opened his notebook.

At the top of a fresh page, he wrote:

**WHO SIGNED THE CONDITIONAL RESPONSE?**

Beneath it, he added:

**WHO GAVE THEM THE AUTHORITY?**

For the first time, those two questions might finally have separate answers.
