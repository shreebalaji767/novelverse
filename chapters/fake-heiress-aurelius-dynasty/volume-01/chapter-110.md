# **Chapter 110 — The Person Who Was Not Allowed to Read the Report**

The words were faint, but their meaning was difficult to ignore.

**COPY TO ORIGINATING REPRESENTATIVE: WITHHELD PENDING CONSENT.**

Edward placed the routing cover beneath the examination lamp again.

The document had already revealed that Eleanor Vale prepared the original OR-28 review request and that Charles Vale authorised its restricted circulation.

Now it suggested that someone had been deliberately excluded from receiving a copy.

The question was no longer simply who had written the warning.

It was who had been prevented from reading it.

“Jonathan, we need the definition of originating representative used in the day-34 procedure.”

Jonathan opened the relevant archive folder.

“The terminology changes between documents. In some records, it refers to the medical institution. In others, it refers to the person authorised to act for the originating family.”

“Which meaning applied here?”

“I'll check the original authority schedule.”

Edward pointed at the routing cover.

“And find out whether consent was actually requested. The phrase pending consent could mean it was requested and refused, or that nobody had approached the person.”

“Understood.”

Edward looked again at the date.

Day 34.

The original review request had been prepared before the later administrative changes.

If the originating representative had been entitled to receive the report, withholding it could have prevented that person from challenging an incorrect proposed allocation.

But he would not assume that was what happened.

He needed the underlying authority.

The original OR-28 authority schedule was recovered from the Vale Private Counsel archive two days later.

It consisted of six pages.

The first established the purpose of the independent review.

The second identified the documents that could be examined.

The third defined the permitted reviewers.

The fourth described the responsibilities of the originating medical authority.

The fifth concerned the receiving identity custodian.

The sixth covered communication with originating and receiving representatives.

Edward read the sixth page carefully.

There were two distinct categories.

**ORIGINATING FAMILY REPRESENTATIVE**

A person authorised to receive information concerning the originating medical record and to provide consent for the release of protected maternal and neonatal information.

**RECEIVING FAMILY REPRESENTATIVE**

A person authorised to acknowledge a proposed receiving arrangement, subject to independent verification of the receiving identity.

The categories were separate.

Neither could automatically exercise the authority of the other.

Edward turned to Jonathan.

“So the originating representative wasn't necessarily the person who would receive the child.”

“Correct.”

“And the receiving representative wasn't necessarily the biological parent.”

“Correct.”

“Who was listed as originating representative?”

Jonathan checked the attached schedule.

“Two entries.”

Edward waited.

“Victoria Aurelius and Isabella Vale.”

He looked at the page.

“Both?”

“Yes. Separate originating records. Separate authorisations.”

“Were both entitled to receive the OR-28 report?”

“The schedule says each was entitled to receive the findings relevant to her originating medical record, subject to the necessary consent and identity safeguards.”

Edward read the provision again.

The distinction was precise.

Victoria was entitled to information concerning her maternity record.

Isabella was entitled to information concerning hers.

Neither could automatically receive the other's protected medical information.

And neither was automatically the receiving representative for a proposed placement.

“Was consent requested from either woman?”

Jonathan examined the correspondence index.

“Victoria's general preservation authority was signed on day 39. But the day-34 request predates it.”

“What about Isabella?”

“There is a separate consent record from day 16 authorising comparison of her maternity record with the relevant neonatal material.”

“Then why was the copy withheld?”

Jonathan continued searching.

“I found a note attached to the day-34 correspondence.”

He enlarged the scan.

**Originating representative notification deferred. Pending confirmation that the review will not disclose receiving identity information outside the authorised scope.**

Edward frowned.

“That sounds like a safeguard.”

“It could be.”

“Was the safeguard ever cleared?”

Jonathan checked the subsequent entries.

“No formal clearance appears.”

Edward looked at the authority schedule again.

The report had not been withheld because the originating representatives had refused to receive it.

It appeared to have been withheld because someone had not confirmed what information could safely be disclosed.

That was a different explanation.

It did not make the omission harmless.

But it meant the investigation had to distinguish a protective restriction from a deliberate concealment.

“Find out who was responsible for clearing the restriction.”

Eleanor Vale agreed to another interview.

This time, Edward placed the original authority schedule in front of her.

She read the sixth page without touching it.

“Yes,” she said. “That is the procedure I remember.”

“You prepared the OR-28 request.”

“Yes.”

“Why did you request an independent review?”

“Because the proposed receiving arrangements were being discussed before the originating records had been reconciled.”

“Who was discussing them?”

“Vale Private Counsel and the Family Allocation Administration department.”

“Charles?”

“He was involved.”

“Did you believe he intended to allocate children to the wrong families?”

Eleanor looked at him.

“I did not have evidence of that.”

“What did you believe?”

“I believed that administrative arrangements were beginning to acquire a permanence they had not earned.”

Edward waited.

Eleanor continued.

“Children had been moved between facilities. Temporary names had been proposed. Receiving representatives had been discussed. Financial instructions had been issued. But the original maternity and neonatal records had not been reconciled.”

“And you wanted the review to stop the process?”

“I wanted the review to establish what could actually be confirmed.”

“Why were the originating representatives not sent copies?”

“Because the proposed review involved records belonging to more than one family. The receiving identity information was protected, and the two originating records could not simply be combined.”

“Was that your decision?”

“Partly.”

“Who else was involved?”

“Dr Samuel Hale advised on the medical records. Charles authorised the restricted circulation. The legal office was supposed to determine the permitted disclosure.”

“Was a final decision made?”

Eleanor shook her head.

“Not before the review was suspended.”

Edward leaned forward.

“Did you personally ask Victoria Aurelius for consent?”

“No.”

“Did you ask Isabella Vale?”

“No.”

“Why not?”

“Because I did not have a complete report to give them. I was not going to send partial information that could make one woman believe a child had been identified when no such determination had been made.”

Edward studied her.

“Then why does the routing cover say consent was pending?”

“Because the legal office wanted confirmation before any originating representative received the report.”

“From whom?”

“I cannot remember whether the request was directed to the medical records authority or the legal office.”

“Could it have been directed to Charles?”

“Yes.”

“Could it have been directed to you?”

“Yes.”

“Did you refuse?”

“No.”

Eleanor's answer was firm.

“I never refused to let an originating representative receive a properly prepared report. I refused to let an incomplete comparison be presented as a confirmed identity.”

Edward made a note.

“Did you know the review would be suspended on day 47?”

“No.”

“Did you know the administrative allocation procedure would continue?”

“I learned that afterward.”

“Did you object?”

“Yes.”

“Was your objection recorded?”

“Some of it.”

Edward placed another document before her.

The day-47 withdrawal instruction.

Eleanor read the sentence:

**OR-28 independent review suspended. Existing administrative allocation procedure to continue under Schedule 4. No further external reviewer required.**

Her expression changed.

“This is not how I understood the decision.”

“What did you understand?”

“That the review was temporarily suspended because the original materials were incomplete. I did not understand that the administrative allocation procedure would be allowed to proceed as though the review had been completed.”

“Did you receive this instruction?”

“No. I saw the later record.”

“Who told you?”

“Marianne Cross.”

Edward looked at her.

“Did Marianne tell you the review was incomplete?”

“Yes.”

“Did she tell you the R-04 register still showed the receiving authority as unappointed?”

“Yes.”

“Then why did you wait until day 61 to review the records at Unit Six?”

Eleanor considered the question.

“Because I did not have access to the complete preservation package before then.”

“Who controlled access?”

“Adrian Holt.”

“Did he refuse you?”

“No. He was following the custody restrictions. I was authorised to inspect the seals and preservation conditions, not to open the restricted recipient material.”

Edward returned to his notes.

Eleanor had prepared the review, maintained the separation of records, and objected to the later administrative closure.

But she had not personally confirmed the identity of any infant.

She had not appointed a receiving representative.

And she had not been able to force the process back onto the original path.

“Do you remember who first proposed that the originating representatives should not receive copies?”

Eleanor was silent for several seconds.

Then she answered.

“Charles.”

Edward looked up.

“What did he say?”

“He said the families should not receive incomplete information that might cause them to make demands on the wrong child.”

“Was that an unreasonable concern?”

“Not by itself.”

“But?”

“But it became a reason not to inform them at all.”

Edward closed his notebook.

That was the first direct account connecting Charles's caution about disclosure with the continued absence of family notification.

It still did not establish why he had later permitted the proposed allocation to be treated as complete.

But it gave Edward another question to pursue.

At the Aurelius Estate, Victoria received a call from Alexander.

He was in his office, reviewing a financial report when his private assistant informed him that the investigation team had requested an additional authorisation record.

Victoria joined him.

“What is it?”

“An old consent document.”

“For the maternity records?”

“Yes.”

She sat opposite him.

Alexander placed the scan on the table.

It was a general preservation authority signed by Victoria on day 39.

She recognised her signature.

“I remember this.”

“What do you remember?”

“They said some of the original hospital records had been recovered, but they were incomplete. I signed permission for them to be preserved and examined.”

“Did they tell you about OR-28?”

“No.”

“Did anyone explain that the review involved a proposed receiving family?”

Victoria looked at him.

“No.”

Alexander remained quiet.

The authority had allowed preservation and limited examination.

It had not authorised a permanent placement.

It had not identified a child.

It had not permitted someone to act as receiving custodian.

Victoria read the document again.

“I thought I was helping them find our daughter.”

“You were.”

“But I didn't know what they were doing with the records.”

Alexander reached across the desk and placed his hand beside the document.

“We know more now than we did yesterday.”

Victoria gave a small nod.

“Do we know whether Isabella Vale received the same kind of request?”

“Not yet.”

“Then we should find out.”

Alexander agreed.

He had no intention of allowing the investigation to treat one woman's maternity records as evidence of another woman's child.

Both women deserved accurate information.

And neither deserved to be given an answer merely because someone had decided that the investigation needed one.

Edward's next lead came from the legal office.

The consent restriction had been assigned to a solicitor named Peter Langford.

This was not the same Peter Langford who had later served as compliance director in the Aurelius Family Office. The two men had identical names, but their professional records showed no connection.

The Vale Private Counsel solicitor had retired years earlier.

He agreed to provide a statement.

Peter Langford arrived carrying a personal archive box.

“I kept some of my old files,” he explained. “I don't know whether they will be useful.”

Edward showed him the day-34 routing cover.

Langford examined it.

“I remember this matter.”

“Were you responsible for approving disclosure to the originating representatives?”

“I was asked to review the disclosure rules.”

“Did you approve them?”

“No.”

“Why?”

“Because the medical records were not sufficiently reconciled.”

“Was the restriction intended to prevent the mothers from receiving information?”

“No. It was intended to prevent the disclosure of information that could not be properly attributed.”

Edward pointed to the phrase pending consent.

“Whose consent?”

Langford opened his box.

“Let me find something.”

After several minutes, he produced a thin folder.

The label read:

**OR-28 — DISCLOSURE REVIEW.**

Inside was a memorandum dated day 37.

Langford placed it on the table.

The first paragraph stated that the two originating representatives could receive separate reports concerning their own maternity records.

The second prohibited disclosure of the other originating representative's protected information.

The third required a verified medical comparison before any infant identity could be stated.

Edward read the final paragraph.

**No originating representative shall be asked to acknowledge or accept a proposed receiving allocation until the receiving identity custodian has been appointed and the relevant identity continuity has been independently verified.**

He looked up.

“This is clear.”

“Yes.”

“Was this memorandum circulated?”

“To the legal office, the medical records authority, and Charles Vale.”

“Did Charles object?”

“Not to the memorandum itself.”

“What happened afterward?”

Langford removed another page.

A handwritten note had been attached.

**Do not issue the originating reports until the recipient schedule is settled. Families must not be given information that could interfere with current care arrangements.**

Edward examined the handwriting.

“Who wrote this?”

Langford looked at it.

“I believe Charles.”

“Do you recognise the writing?”

“Yes. I worked with him for years.”

“Did you agree?”

“I objected.”

“Why?”

“Because the memorandum did not require the recipient schedule to be settled before the originating reports could be issued. It required the reports to be limited to each woman's own records.”

“Did you tell him?”

“I did.”

“What did he say?”

Langford looked at the paper.

“He said the families were already under strain and that releasing incomplete information could create further confusion.”

“And?”

“I told him that withholding information could create a different kind of harm.”

Edward waited.

Langford continued.

“He said he would reconsider.”

“Did he?”

“I never received a revised instruction.”

Edward turned the page over.

There was a small notation at the bottom.

**Disclosure deferred. Review authority to confirm.**

The initials were unfamiliar.

“Who wrote this?”

“I don't know.”

“Did you sign it?”

“No.”

“Did you approve the deferral?”

“No.”

Edward closed the folder.

The memorandum established that the originating representatives could have received limited reports without being told the identity of any receiving child.

Someone had chosen to defer even that limited disclosure.

And Charles had been directly involved.

But the final notation was unsigned.

The person who had formally deferred the reports remained unidentified.

That evening, Elena sat at the dining table with Catherine and Sebastian.

Her travel documents were arranged neatly beside her plate.

Catherine looked at them.

“Everything is ready?”

“Almost.”

“What remains?”

“I need to confirm the final transport details and collect one document from the programme office.”

“Have you checked your accommodation address?”

“Yes.”

“Emergency contact?”

“Yes.”

“Medical insurance?”

“Yes.”

Sebastian looked up from his phone.

“She has checked everything more times than our finance department checks a quarterly report.”

Elena smiled.

“That's because my budget is smaller.”

“Your budget is not the issue.”

“It is when you keep suggesting upgrades.”

Sebastian placed his phone down.

“I suggested one better travel option.”

“You suggested the most expensive option.”

“It was more convenient.”

“It was also unnecessary.”

Catherine laughed quietly.

Sebastian gave a small shrug.

“Fine. You win.”

Elena looked at her documents.

“I want to manage my expenses properly. If I start accepting every convenience, I won't know whether I can handle things independently.”

Catherine studied her for a moment.

“You don't have to prove that you can do everything alone.”

“I know.”

“Do you?”

Elena looked up.

Catherine's voice was gentle.

“You can be independent and still accept help. Those things are not opposites.”

Elena considered that.

“I suppose so.”

Sebastian pushed a small envelope across the table.

“What is this?”

“Something for your journey.”

Elena opened it.

Inside was a prepaid emergency travel card with a modest balance.

She looked at him.

“Sebastian.”

“It's not part of your programme budget. It's an emergency reserve.”

“I have savings.”

“I know.”

“Then why?”

“Because an emergency reserve is useful even when you have savings.”

Elena stared at the card.

She knew he was trying to help.

She also knew that refusing every gesture would turn ordinary family interactions into another argument about what she was entitled to receive.

“Thank you,” she said finally.

Sebastian nodded.

“Keep it separate from your regular expenses.”

“I will.”

Catherine smiled.

For the rest of dinner, they discussed the programme schedule, the city, and the academic project Elena wanted to develop.

Nobody mentioned the investigation.

For a little while, the family allowed her to be simply a young woman preparing to study abroad.

Edward returned to the office the following morning.

A new document had arrived from the successor archive.

It was a copy of the day-37 disclosure memorandum's circulation record.

Three recipients had signed for it.

- Dr Samuel Hale — Medical Records Authority.
- Eleanor Vale — Preservation and Separation Review.
- Charles Vale — Vale Private Counsel.

A fourth recipient had been added manually.

**M. VOSS — ORIGINATING REGISTER CUSTODY.**

Margaret Voss.

Edward looked at Jonathan.

“Did she receive the memorandum?”

“Apparently.”

“Ask her.”

Margaret agreed to speak by telephone.

When Edward read the memorandum's final paragraph to her, she was quiet.

“I remember that instruction,” she said.

“Did you agree with it?”

“No.”

“Why?”

“Because the originating families were entitled to accurate information about their own records.”

“Did you know the reports had been deferred?”

“Yes.”

“Did you challenge the decision?”

“I sent a written objection.”

“Do we have it?”

“I don't know whether it was retained.”

Edward asked her to describe it.

“I said the records could remain separate without withholding the existence of the records from the originating representatives. I also said no receiving identity could be confirmed through the temporary placement documents.”

“Did Charles respond?”

“He said the issue was under legal review.”

“Did you receive a final decision?”

“No.”

Edward made a note.

Another person had objected.

Another person had preserved the distinction between originating identity and receiving identity.

Yet the disclosure remained deferred.

“Margaret,” he said, “did you know whether Victoria Aurelius or Isabella Vale was ever told that the independent review had been suspended?”

“No.”

“Did either woman receive the day-37 memorandum?”

“Not through my office.”

“Did anyone tell you that one of them had been informed?”

“No.”

Edward ended the call.

Jonathan looked at the growing collection of documents.

“Eleanor, Langford, Margaret, Marianne. They all understood the same distinction.”

“And they all lacked the authority to complete the process alone.”

“Charles had more authority than any of them.”

“Yes.”

“But the records still don't show that he appointed a receiving custodian.”

“Correct.”

Edward looked at the day-37 memorandum.

The investigation was beginning to establish something more precise than a general administrative failure.

The originating representatives had not received the reports they were entitled to receive.

The receiving identity custodian had never been appointed.

The independent review had been suspended.

The administrative allocation procedure had continued.

But the reason for withholding the reports had been recorded as a pending legal review.

And the person who made the final decision had not signed the record.

Edward turned to Jonathan.

“Find the unsigned deferral notation.”

Jonathan nodded.

“Any idea where to start?”

Edward pointed to the circulation list.

“Everyone who received the memorandum had an opportunity to challenge it. Someone outside that list may have been given the authority to defer it.”

Jonathan examined the document.

“There is another name written on the reverse.”

Edward turned the page.

A faint impression appeared beneath the paper.

It was a reference, not a signature.

**FAMILY OFFICE — EXTERNAL LIAISON REVIEW.**

Below it was a code.

**EFO-SL/07.**

Edward stared at the code.

The same external liaison reference had appeared throughout the investigation.

It had been used in hospital transfers, protected identity instructions, and correspondence between Vale Private Counsel and the Family Office.

He had once assumed it referred to a single administrative channel.

Now he was beginning to suspect it had been used to conceal the identity of the person making decisions.

“Jonathan,” he said quietly, “find every person who had access to EFO-SL/07 on day 37.”

Jonathan looked at the reference.

“That could be a long list.”

“Then start with the people who could authorise a disclosure restriction.”

Edward returned to the memorandum.

The two originating mothers had not been told that their records were under independent review.

They had not been told that the review was suspended.

And they had not been told that the proposed receiving arrangements were still unverified.

The missing report was no longer just an administrative detail.

It was the information that might have allowed someone to challenge the entire process before the provisional arrangements became entrenched.

And someone had made sure it never reached them.
