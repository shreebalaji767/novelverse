# **Chapter 197 — The Authority Issued Before the Seal**

At 8:14, Edward received the PAO/49/01 index.

It contained only four entries.

**Reference:** PAO/49/01.

**Issue date:** Day 47.

**Responsible office:** Programme Authority Office.

**Subject:** Preservation of restricted originating and receiving-route records.

**Custody arrangement:** Separate controlled preservation.

**Individual author:** Restricted.

**Execution status:** Confirmed.

Edward read the subject again.

“Originating and receiving-route records,” Sophie said.

“Yes.”

“Not just OI-07.”

“No.”

The instruction had been issued on the same day Dr Evelyn Shaw declined Adrian Holt as an independent verifier.

It had also been issued on the day OC-19 became officially vacant.

Edward opened the related-reference list.

Four documents were connected to PAO/49/01.

- OI-07 supporting authorisation.
- PR-06 original procedure.
- OC-19 candidate review.
- Receiving-route preservation schedule.

The list did not include the day-46 C-19 note.

It did not include the day-190 reconciliation.

It did not include the later approval certificate.

The preservation authority had existed before those records.

“Can we establish what prompted it?” Sophie asked.

“Not from an index.”

“Do we have the original?”

“Not yet.”

Edward opened the request tracker.

The originating office had acknowledged the request for PAO/49/01's authorisation record.

Its release status was pending.

He added a second request.

**Please identify the records included in the initial preservation schedule, the date each record entered custody, and whether the preservation instruction was triggered by a specific event or issued as a general precaution.**

He sent it.

Then he opened the chronology.

Day 47 had become an increasingly important point.

Adrian Holt's proposed independent appointment was declined.

OC-19 remained vacant.

A preservation authority was issued.

But the existence of the preservation authority did not establish that the rejection had caused it.

There could have been an earlier concern.

There could have been a planned preservation review.

There could have been a routine custody process that happened to fall on the same day.

Edward wrote all three possibilities in the margin.

He did not choose one.

At 9:30, Miriam Cole arrived.

She was in her early thirties, dressed formally, and carried a document folder.

She greeted Edward and Sophie politely.

“Thank you for sending the transmission record.”

“Of course,” Edward said. “We want to establish exactly what you did and what you were authorised to do.”

Miriam placed her folder on the table.

“I understand.”

Edward opened the interview record.

“Were you employed by Restricted Correspondence Administration on day 44?”

“Yes.”

“What was your role?”

“Correspondence support officer. I handled incoming document registration, dispatch preparation and controlled forwarding.”

“Could you authorise an appointment?”

“No.”

“Could you act as a personal representative for P-04?”

“No.”

“Could you make substantive decisions concerning protected-principal communication?”

“No.”

“Could you prepare correspondence for transmission?”

“Yes, within the instructions assigned to my desk.”

Edward showed her the RC-44/OC19 transmission envelope.

“Do you recognise this?”

Miriam examined it.

“Yes.”

“Did you transmit it?”

“My credential was used to transmit it.”

“Did you personally operate the terminal?”

She looked at the record again.

“I remember sending something that afternoon. I can't say from memory whether this was the exact document.”

“Do you recognise the attachment?”

He showed her the preliminary independence query.

Miriam read it slowly.

“I remember this wording.”

“Did you write it?”

“No.”

“Do you know who did?”

“I don't know.”

“Who gave you the document?”

“It was already in the correspondence queue when I opened the task.”

“Was there an instruction attached?”

“There was a routing instruction.”

“From whom?”

“The task was generated through the shared administrative system.”

“Was a named operator listed?”

“No.”

Edward showed her PA-44/03.

Miriam nodded.

“That is the kind of task we received.”

“Did you know it had originated from GOV/OC19/02?”

“I don't remember the reference.”

“Did you know C-19 was a correspondence contact location?”

“Yes.”

“Did you believe C-19 had authority to nominate an independent verifier?”

“No.”

“Did anyone tell you it did?”

“No.”

Edward placed the day-46 note in front of her.

“Did you create this entry?”

Miriam examined it.

“No.”

“Did you transmit it?”

“No.”

“Did you prepare an alternative candidate proposal?”

“No.”

“Did you receive a candidate's name?”

“No.”

“Did you receive an instruction to nominate someone?”

“No.”

Her answers were consistent.

But Edward wanted to establish one more detail.

“When you transmitted RC-44/OC19, what did you understand the message to be?”

“A request for the criteria that would apply to an independent verifier.”

“Did you understand it as a nomination?”

“No.”

“Did you understand it as an instruction from P-04?”

“No.”

“Did you understand it as a communication sent on behalf of the protected principal?”

Miriam hesitated.

“I understood it was being forwarded through the designated correspondence route. I wasn't told who had prepared it or whose question it represented.”

Edward noted the wording.

“Did you see any personal authority attached?”

“No.”

“Any signature?”

“No.”

“Any written instruction from P-04?”

“No.”

“Any instruction from Gabriel Vale?”

“I don't remember seeing his name.”

“Was his communication credential involved?”

“I don't know. The transmission record shows my administrative credential.”

“Could you see which credential prepared the original document?”

“No. That information was not displayed in my task view.”

Edward closed the folder.

“Thank you. That's helpful.”

Miriam looked at him.

“May I ask something?”

“Of course.”

“Is this being treated as evidence that I sent an unauthorised instruction?”

“No. The current record establishes that your credential transmitted the query. It does not establish that you authored it or that you exceeded your assigned task.”

She nodded, visibly relieved.

“I was concerned because I remember the question being quite specific. I wouldn't have written something about independent verification. That was outside my role.”

“Do you remember anyone discussing the question with you?”

“Not clearly.”

“Take your time.”

Miriam thought for a moment.

“There was a discussion at the correspondence desk about whether the question should be sent through C-19 or returned to Programme Administration.”

Edward leaned forward.

“Who was involved?”

“I can't give you names.”

“Why not?”

“Because I don't remember who was sitting where. There were several people working that afternoon.”

“What was the discussion about?”

“Whether C-19 was only a forwarding route or whether it was the proper contact for the question.”

“And what was the conclusion?”

“That it could be used to forward the question, but the originating authority had to remain separate.”

Edward exchanged a glance with Sophie.

The distinction had been understood at the correspondence desk.

At least by someone.

“Do you remember who said that?”

“No.”

“Was the instruction written?”

“I think it was on the task screen.”

“Do you remember the wording?”

Miriam looked down.

“Something like: 'Use the existing contact route for transmission only. Do not attribute the query to the contact location.'”

Edward stopped writing.

“Are you sure?”

“Not word for word. But that was the meaning.”

“Did you see that instruction before transmitting the query?”

“Yes, I believe so.”

“Could it be in the task history?”

“It might be.”

Edward turned to Sophie.

“Request the full task-screen capture and version history.”

Sophie wrote it down.

The interview had not identified the author.

But it had established something important.

The correspondence officer who transmitted the query understood that C-19 was not its author.

The day-46 classification had been entered later, elsewhere.

At 11:25, Edward received the task history.

It contained three versions.

The first was created at 13:51.

The second was modified at 14:22.

The third was released to the correspondence queue at 14:31.

Edward opened the first version.

**Instruction:** Forward the independence-criteria query through the designated correspondence route.

**Attribution:** Originating office to remain separately recorded.

The second version contained a change.

**Instruction:** Forward the independence-criteria query through C-19.

**Attribution:** C-19 to be recorded as transmission source only.

The third version retained that wording.

Edward examined the modification history.

The change had been made at 14:22 through a Programme Administration shared account.

The named operator field was blank.

But the system retained the reason for the change.

**Clarification requested by receiving department.**

“Receiving department,” Sophie said.

“Which one?”

“Programme Administration.”

Edward opened the task's routing details.

The clarification had been entered by the Programme Administration Review Desk.

It did not identify an individual.

The wording was precise.

C-19 was to be recorded as the transmission source only.

Not the originating authority.

Not the author.

Not the nominating authority.

The same distinction Daniel had later written in his day-151 note.

Edward looked at the day-46 classification again.

Someone had changed the description after the query was transmitted.

The original task had been clear.

The correspondence desk had been clear.

The later classification had not.

“Can we see who accessed the task after day 44?” Sophie asked.

Edward opened the access history.

There were six access events.

Three belonged to administrative support.

One belonged to Daniel Mercer.

One belonged to Patricia Moore's supervisor account.

The final access belonged to a restricted Programme Administration credential.

It had opened the task on day 46 at 10:09.

Twenty-seven minutes before the alternative candidate note was entered.

Edward looked at the credential.

**PA-REVIEW-06.**

He knew the code.

It was the shared review credential associated with Patricia Moore's supervisory function.

But a shared credential did not identify the person who used it.

“Request the underlying access log,” he said.

Sophie added it to the list.

Edward looked at the time again.

10:09.

The alternative candidate note had been recorded at 10:42.

A difference of thirty-three minutes.

The task had been accessed shortly before the misleading source classification appeared.

That was a sequence worth investigating.

It was not proof of authorship.

At 13:00, Elena received the test results from the grant programme.

Ten of the twelve test applications had passed.

The remaining two exposed problems in the evidence-assessment workflow.

She opened the first failed case.

A reviewer had marked a document as accepted before the document's source had been verified.

The system had allowed it because the reviewer assignment was valid.

Elena frowned.

“That should not happen.”

Clara, sitting beside her at the library table, looked over.

“What happened?”

“The reviewer had authority to assess the evidence, but the source verification was still pending.”

“Then the system should block acceptance.”

“Not necessarily. It should allow the reviewer to record a provisional assessment, but not final acceptance.”

Elena added a new status.

**Provisional assessment — source verification pending.**

Then she added a rule.

A provisional assessment could be saved.

It could not be converted into final acceptance until the required verification was completed.

Clara read the new rule.

“Does that mean the reviewer can still explain what they think?”

“Yes. But the system won't confuse an opinion with a verified finding.”

“That's better.”

Elena opened the second failed application.

This one involved a document received by the programme office but not entered into the applicant's file.

The system showed the document as received.

It did not show who had attached it.

Elena shook her head.

“Another problem.”

She added an attachment history.

The system now recorded the person who uploaded the document, the account used, the time, the application reference and the document's subsequent status.

Clara smiled.

“Are you going to fix every possible mistake?”

“No.”

“Good.”

“I'll fix the ones I can reproduce.”

“That sounds more manageable.”

Elena saved the changes.

For the first time that afternoon, she looked satisfied.

At 15:10, Patricia Moore's office confirmed that she would attend an interview.

The confirmation included a short statement.

**I recall the PA-REVIEW-06 task, but I did not personally create the day-46 alternative candidate note. I would like to review the access history before commenting further.**

Edward read it.

“She knows the task.”

“Or remembers the shared credential.”

“Either way, she wants the logs.”

He approved the release of the access history, with unrelated records redacted.

Then he added a question to the interview outline.

**Who requested the clarification that C-19 was to be recorded as a transmission source only?**

The answer might identify the person who understood the distinction.

Or it might identify the person who had changed the task.

Edward was careful not to assume those were the same person.

At 16:40, Eleanor sent the original PAO/49/01 preservation authority.

The document was longer than the index suggested.

It contained a preservation schedule and an explanation of the records to be protected.

The first section was general.

It required the preservation of original records concerning the protected-principal communication procedure, independent verification and receiving-route administration.

The second section was more specific.

**Reason for preservation:** A discrepancy had been identified between the administrative representation of certain receiving-route records and the authority recorded in the original appointment documents.

Edward sat up.

“That's new.”

Sophie leaned closer.

The next paragraph read:

**The preservation requirement is precautionary. It must not be interpreted as a finding of misconduct, identity substitution or deliberate alteration.**

Edward read it again.

A discrepancy had existed.

Someone had noticed it.

But the instruction expressly warned against assuming wrongdoing.

He continued.

**The following records must be preserved in their original form:**

- Original receiving-route appointment instruments.
- Rejected and incomplete candidate proposals.
- Correspondence concerning independent verification.
- Records showing the separation of personal representation from receiving authority.
- Original communication procedures and their associated acknowledgement records.

The final section listed the preservation custodian.

Dr Samuel Hale.

The transfer was dated day 47 at 18:15.

Edward checked the chronology.

The preservation authority had been issued earlier that afternoon.

The records had been transferred to Dr Hale's custody the same evening.

The document included a receipt signed by him.

Not as author.

Not as an approver.

As custodian.

Edward placed the receipt beside the preservation authority.

“Dr Hale received the records on the day Adrian Holt was rejected.”

“Yes,” Sophie said.

“And the reason was a discrepancy in receiving-route authority records.”

“Do we know which discrepancy?”

“Not yet.”

Edward looked at the schedule.

It listed the relevant records but did not identify the discrepancy itself.

There was one more reference.

**Triggering review:** RR-47/02.

He searched the index.

A result appeared.

**RR-47/02 — Receiving-Route Authority Discrepancy Report.**

**Created:** Day 47.

**Originating office:** Restricted Governance Review.

**Status:** Preserved.

**Author:** Restricted.

Edward read the date.

The report had been created on the same day as the preservation authority.

It might explain why the records were preserved.

It might also explain why Adrian Holt's proposal had been rejected.

But neither conclusion was established.

He opened the report's release conditions.

**Disclosure requires confirmation from the originating office and the custodian of the original receiving-route appointment record.**

Edward leaned back.

“Who is the custodian?”

Sophie checked the index.

“Not Eleanor.”

“Who?”

The record listed a different office.

**Receiving-Route Records Custodian: Vale Private Counsel — Restricted Archive.**

Edward looked at the screen.

Charles Vale's office.

Again.

At 18:03, Edward sent the request to Vale Private Counsel.

He asked for the RR-47/02 discrepancy report and the original receiving-route appointment records that supported it.

He made the scope explicit.

No personal identity information.

No protected-principal communications.

No disclosure of sealed family records.

Only the authority instruments and the discrepancy identified in the report.

The acknowledgement arrived at 18:11.

**Request received.**

**Original appointment records located.**

**Disclosure review pending.**

Then a second line appeared.

**The receiving-route discrepancy report was not commissioned by Vale Private Counsel.**

Edward read it twice.

He looked at Sophie.

“That's an interesting clarification.”

“Did we ask whether they commissioned it?”

“No.”

“Then why did they volunteer that?”

Edward turned back to the message.

The report had been created by Restricted Governance Review.

The original appointment records were held by Vale Private Counsel.

The preservation authority had been issued by the Programme Authority Office.

Three separate offices.

Three separate responsibilities.

And now all three were connected to the same discrepancy.

Edward opened a fresh page in the chronology.

He wrote:

**Day 47 — Receiving-route authority discrepancy identified.**

**Report created by Restricted Governance Review.**

**Preservation authority issued by Programme Authority Office.**

**Original appointment records held by Vale Private Counsel.**

He paused.

Then he added:

**The discrepancy itself remains unknown.**

At the bottom of the page, he wrote the report reference.

**RR-47/02.**

For the first time, the investigation had found a document created specifically because someone had noticed a problem with the receiving-route records.

And the next person they needed to question was not the person who had preserved those records.

It was the person who had first reported the discrepancy.
