# **Chapter 62 — The Woman Who Believed She Had Given Birth**

Edward did not immediately ask another question.

He looked at the photograph on his desk.

Isabella Vale.

The woman had appeared in several places throughout the investigation, yet her name had never been properly connected to the children's movements.

She had worked for Charles Vale.

She had signed the ninth-day amendment.

She had visited Eleanor.

She had collected the children's papers after Eleanor's death.

And, according to Margaret Voss, she believed Infant A was her own child.

Edward finally spoke.

"Margaret, I need you to explain something."

"I know."

"How could Isabella believe she had given birth to a child when she hadn't?"

"I didn't say she hadn't."

Edward became still.

"What?"

Margaret exhaled.

"Isabella was pregnant."

For a moment, Edward said nothing.

"Then where is her child?"

"I don't know."

"Was her child one of the two infants?"

"I don't know that either."

"Margaret, you have been holding information about this case for eighteen years. You cannot keep answering every question with those three words."

"I understand."

"Then tell me what you know."

Margaret remained silent for several seconds.

Finally, she said, "You need to find her medical records."

"Where?"

"At Vale Private Counsel. Charles kept a separate file for Isabella. It wasn't part of the children's records."

"What was it called?"

"Maternal Placement."

Edward wrote the words down.

"Was she the mother of Infant A?"

"No. Not according to the records I saw."

"Then why did she believe she was?"

"Because someone told her that her baby had been taken."

The following morning, Edward and Jonathan visited the former Vale Private Counsel archive.

The building had been converted into offices years ago, but a small records department still maintained its historical collection.

Their request was initially refused.

The archive administrator explained that the relevant records were subject to a confidentiality agreement and that the original client had never authorised their release.

Edward presented the court-approved investigative order.

The administrator read it twice before calling the legal department.

Almost forty minutes passed before they were allowed into the restricted records room.

A staff member brought them a grey archive box.

The label read:

**ISABELLA VALE — MATERNAL PLACEMENT**

Jonathan examined the label.

"This is separate from the infant records."

"That is precisely why we need it."

Edward opened the box.

Inside were medical correspondence, payment records, employment documents, and several sealed envelopes.

The first medical document was dated eighteen years earlier.

It confirmed that Isabella Vale had received maternity care at a private medical facility.

Her expected delivery date was recorded.

There was also a note concerning a premature delivery.

Edward turned the page.

The next document was an admission summary.

**PATIENT: ISABELLA VALE**

**ADMISSION: 01:12 A.M.**

**DELIVERY: 2:31 A.M.**

**OUTCOME: LIVE FEMALE INFANT**

Edward read the line again.

Jonathan leaned closer.

"Two thirty-one."

"Yes."

"Victoria's original record says 2:17."

"And the hospital's original record lists Infant A at that time."

Edward placed the documents side by side.

The times were close.

But they were not identical.

Jonathan looked at him.

"Could the records have been confused?"

"Possibly."

"Could Isabella's child have been one of the two infants?"

"Possibly."

Edward turned another page.

The discharge section was incomplete.

The infant's name had been left blank.

The receiving guardian field was empty.

And beside the discharge status, someone had written:

**TRANSFER ARRANGED THROUGH EXTERNAL FAMILY REPRESENTATIVE.**

The representative's name was missing.

Edward continued searching.

A separate note had been attached to the medical summary.

*Patient has been informed that the infant requires additional observation. No final receiving arrangement has been disclosed.*

There was no mention of death.

No mention of adoption.

No mention of a permanent separation.

Jonathan looked at the note.

"She may have been told her child was alive."

"Or she may have been told something else later."

Edward closed the file.

"Find the medical facility."

Isabella Vale had been admitted to a private maternity clinic in the northern district.

The clinic had since merged with a larger medical group.

Its former medical director, Dr. Peter Lang, was already known to Edward through the earlier hospital investigation.

He had retired and lived outside the city.

Edward contacted him.

Dr. Lang agreed to meet, but only after receiving a copy of the investigative order.

Their meeting took place in a quiet consultation room at the medical group's administrative building.

Dr. Lang read the documents carefully.

"Isabella Vale," he said.

"Do you remember her?" Edward asked.

"I remember the case."

"She gave birth to a girl?"

"Yes."

"Was the child healthy?"

"Initially, yes. There were concerns about the mother's condition, but the infant did not require prolonged intensive care."

"Where was the child transferred?"

Dr. Lang looked at the paperwork.

"That is difficult to answer."

"Why?"

"Because I did not authorise the final transfer."

"Who did?"

"The external representative."

"Isabella Vale?"

"No. The representative was a man."

"Charles Vale?"

Dr. Lang considered the name.

"I cannot say with certainty."

Edward showed him the old photograph of Charles.

Dr. Lang looked at it for several seconds.

"Yes. I believe that is the man."

"What did he want?"

"Initially, he said the family had arranged temporary protection for the child. He provided legal documentation and an authority reference."

"Was the child's mother informed?"

"She was told the child would be transferred for additional observation."

"Did she agree?"

Dr. Lang looked uncomfortable.

"She was exhausted after delivery. She was also receiving treatment. I cannot tell you whether she fully understood the arrangement."

"Did she sign anything?"

"Yes."

"What?"

"A temporary medical transfer consent."

"Not an adoption agreement?"

"No."

"Not a permanent guardianship agreement?"

"No."

Edward looked at Jonathan.

The distinction was becoming increasingly important.

Dr. Lang continued.

"About a week later, Isabella asked to see her baby."

"What happened?"

"She was told the child was still under observation."

"Was that true?"

Dr. Lang hesitated.

"At that point, I cannot confirm where the child was."

Edward leaned forward.

"Did Isabella believe her baby had died?"

Dr. Lang looked down at the documents.

"She was eventually told that the child had not survived."

The room became silent.

Edward's voice hardened.

"Who told her?"

"I don't remember."

"Was there a death certificate?"

"No."

"Was there a neonatal death record?"

"No."

"Was there a post-mortem?"

"No."

Dr. Lang removed his glasses.

"That is why I never understood the final report."

"What final report?"

"The one that arrived from Vale Private Counsel."

He searched through the pages Edward had brought.

Then he pointed to a small reference number.

"This."

The document number matched an entry in the archive.

**MATERNAL NOTIFICATION — DECEASED INFANT.**

Edward stared at it.

"Was it medically verified?"

"No."

"Then who issued it?"

"An external legal representative."

Dr. Lang looked at Edward.

"Someone informed a mother that her child was dead without a medical death record."

At the Aurelius Estate, Victoria was reading the preliminary forensic report.

The examiner had confirmed that the signature on the Category 17 authorisation was not an original handwritten signature.

It was a mechanically reproduced image.

The ink distribution, pressure pattern, and alignment were consistent with a copied signature placed onto a later document.

However, the examiner could not yet identify the source document from which it had been reproduced.

Alexander read the report.

"So someone copied her signature."

Adrian nodded.

"And Gabriel Vale verified it as genuine."

"Did Gabriel know it was copied?"

"We don't know."

Victoria put the report down.

"Isabella Vale."

Alexander looked at her.

"Edward has identified her as the person who signed the ninth-day amendment."

"She gave birth to a child?"

"Apparently."

Victoria looked towards the window.

"Then perhaps she was another mother who was told her child was somewhere else."

Adrian nodded.

"That's one possibility."

Victoria turned back.

"How many people were told that they had lost a child?"

Nobody answered.

Edward received a call from Margaret Voss that afternoon.

"I heard you found Isabella's medical file," she said.

"Yes."

"Then you know she gave birth."

"Yes."

"Did you find out what happened to her baby?"

"No. But we found evidence that she was eventually told the child had died."

Margaret was silent.

"Did you know?"

"I knew she believed her child was dead."

"Did you know there was no death certificate?"

Another pause.

"No."

Edward looked at the documents.

"Margaret, why did Isabella claim Infant A was hers?"

"Because she was shown a photograph."

"What photograph?"

"A photograph of a newborn."

"Whose newborn?"

"I don't know."

"Did the photograph have an identification band?"

"Yes."

"What did it show?"

"Only a letter. I think it was A."

Edward stood.

"Who showed her the photograph?"

"Eleanor."

"Why?"

"Because Eleanor believed Isabella had been given information that was not true."

"Did Eleanor tell her Infant A was her child?"

"No."

"What did she tell her?"

Margaret's answer came slowly.

"She told Isabella that the child in the photograph might be hers."

Edward closed his eyes.

Might.

Not was.

Not definitely.

Might.

"And Isabella believed her?"

"She wanted to believe her."

"Did Eleanor know the child's identity?"

"I don't think so."

"Then why show her the photograph?"

"Because she wanted Isabella to understand that her baby might still be alive."

Edward returned to his chair.

"Where is that photograph now?"

"I don't know."

"Who has it?"

"Isabella."

That evening, Edward received an unexpected message.

It came from the legal representative who had handled Isabella Vale's employment and personal affairs after her departure from Vale Private Counsel.

The message contained a single attachment.

A letter.

It was addressed to Eleanor Vale.

The date was eighteen years earlier, shortly after the ninth-day release.

The handwriting was unmistakably Isabella's.

*Mrs. Vale,*

*You told me that the baby in the photograph might be mine.*

*I have thought about that every day.*

*I understand that you cannot promise me anything. I understand that the records are incomplete. But I cannot accept that my daughter died when nobody can show me a medical record proving it.*

*If the child is not mine, I will accept that.*

*But if she is mine, I want to know where she is.*

*Please do not tell me to wait indefinitely.*

*I have already waited long enough.*

*Isabella.*

Edward placed the letter on the desk.

There was a handwritten note beneath it.

It had been added by Eleanor.

**I cannot tell her which child is hers. I have no proof.**

Edward looked at the date.

The letter had been written after the ninth-day release.

But before Eleanor received Infant B.

That meant Isabella had already been searching for her daughter while the two infants were still moving between temporary guardians.

And Eleanor had known.

Edward turned the page over.

A second sentence was written on the back.

It was in Isabella's handwriting.

*If I cannot find my daughter, please at least tell me whether she is still alive.*

Edward sat motionless.

For eighteen years, the investigation had revolved around two children whose identities had been repeatedly obscured.

Now there was a third possibility.

A mother whose child had disappeared from a separate maternity clinic.

A photograph connecting her to Infant A.

And a legal arrangement that had treated a copied signature as permission to move two newborns.

Edward reached for his telephone.

He called Jonathan.

"We need to find Isabella Vale."

"Did you locate her?"

"No."

"What happened?"

Edward looked at the letter.

"She wasn't simply working for Charles Vale."

He paused.

"She was searching for her daughter."

At the same time, in a small apartment several hundred kilometres away, a woman opened an old wooden box.

Inside were photographs, medical papers, and a faded hospital bracelet.

She picked up the bracelet.

The name had been written in blue ink.

**BABY GIRL — I. VALE**

The woman held it for a long moment.

Then she removed a photograph from beneath the papers.

It showed a newborn infant wrapped in a white blanket.

On the back, someone had written:

**INFANT A — 2:17 A.M.**

She placed the photograph beside the bracelet.

The two documents did not match.

The bracelet belonged to a child born at 2:31 a.m.

The photograph belonged to a child recorded at 2:17 a.m.

The woman looked at them both.

Then she whispered:

"Which one of you was my daughter?"

She closed the box.

For the first time in eighteen years, Isabella Vale had decided to stop waiting for someone else to tell her the truth.
