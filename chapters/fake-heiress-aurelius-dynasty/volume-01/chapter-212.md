# **Chapter 212 — The Band That Passed Through Meridian**

The examination began at 8:30.

Edward arrived twenty minutes early.

The Medical Records Preservation Office had prepared a separate room for the inspection. There were no windows, only a long table, two document cameras and a locked cabinet containing the original materials.

Dr Miriam Cole stood beside the cabinet.

“Before we begin,” she said, “I want to confirm the scope.”

Edward placed the authorisation on the table.

“We are examining the physical condition, original markings, chronology and custody history of the preserved materials. We are not determining biological identity.”

“Correct.”

“The examiner will not remove anything from preservation.”

“Correct.”

“And no original item will be placed beside another in a way that could imply an identity match.”

Dr Cole nodded.

“Then we can proceed.”

The independent examiner, Dr Helen Foster, arrived shortly afterward.

She had spent twenty years examining historical medical records and institutional archives.

She introduced herself, checked the authorisation and read the restrictions.

Then she asked for the first container.

The original maternity register was removed from its protective case.

Dr Foster examined the binding, the page edges and the ink.

The 2:17 a.m. entry was intact.

The original field had not been erased.

The amendment was written in a different hand and recorded in the margin.

Dr Foster photographed both.

“Do we know when the amendment was physically entered?” Edward asked.

“The date is recorded as day 6,” she replied. “The ink and handwriting are consistent with the surrounding administrative entries, but that does not independently establish the precise hour.”

She moved to the supporting schedule.

The handwritten STC-A reference was present.

No later alteration was visible.

The receiving desk's separate objection was also preserved.

Dr Foster placed the documents in separate protective sleeves.

“The documents are genuine historical records,” she said. “Their contents remain separate evidential questions.”

Edward nodded.

That was exactly what he needed.

At 9:45, the second container was opened.

Inside was the original neonatal band material.

It was smaller than Edward expected.

The band had been cut or separated at some point.

Its edges were uneven.

A printed reference remained partially visible.

Dr Foster examined it under magnification.

“Some of the printed characters are readable.”

“Can you identify the reference?” Edward asked.

“Not yet. I need to photograph it under controlled lighting.”

She adjusted the equipment.

The image appeared on the monitor.

The visible characters were:

**M-204**

Edward looked at Sophie.

The reference was familiar.

M-204 had appeared in the historical record concerning the transfer from St. Catherine's to Meridian.

But the band was not automatically proof that the child associated with M-204 was the infant recorded at 2:17 a.m.

Dr Foster continued her examination.

“There is no complete name on the surviving material.”

“Any hospital mark?”

“A partial institutional stamp.”

She enlarged it.

The stamp read:

**MERIDIAN RECEIVING UNIT**

Edward looked at the custody reference again.

MC-06/11.

The band had passed through Meridian.

That was now physically supported.

The identity of the infant remained unestablished.

At 10:20, Dr Foster examined the band packaging.

The original container had been sealed under reference MC-06/11.

The seal was intact when received by the preservation office.

However, the accompanying custody sheet showed a gap.

The item had been collected from Meridian's receiving unit on day 11.

The person who collected it had signed the receipt.

The next recorded custody entry was day 14.

There was no documented explanation for the intervening three days.

“Where was it held?” Edward asked.

“The record does not say.”

“Could it have remained at Meridian?”

“Possibly.”

“Could it have been transferred elsewhere?”

“Also possible.”

Dr Foster pointed to the original receipt.

“The collection signature establishes that someone received the item. It does not establish where the item remained afterward.”

Edward wrote it down.

Three days.

No documented custody location.

He had seen similar gaps before.

But this one involved an original identification item.

At 11:05, Dr Cole provided the Meridian Clinical Receiving Log.

The log had been preserved separately from the band.

Its day-11 entry was clear.

**Item received:** Original neonatal band material.

**Reference:** M-204.

**Condition:** Partial separation.

**Purpose:** Retention pending identity reconciliation.

**Person presenting item:** Restricted institutional representative.

**Receiving officer:** J. Bell.

Edward read the purpose line.

“Identity reconciliation,” Sophie said.

“Yes.”

“Was that the same reconciliation requested at St. Catherine's?”

“We don't know.”

He checked the date.

Day 11 was five days after the original amendment.

The hospital had entered STC-A on day 6.

Meridian had received the M-204 band material on day 11.

The two records might be related.

But the custody gap prevented a complete reconstruction.

Edward requested the original Meridian collection instruction.

At 11:50, the collection instruction arrived.

It was short.

**MC-06/11 — Original Band Retention Request**

The request instructed Meridian to preserve the band material until the originating hospital could confirm the relationship between the original maternity reference and the receiving identification.

The request was not an instruction to alter the band.

It did not authorise a new patient identity.

It did not authorise permanent transfer of the neonatal record.

The issuing office was identified as:

**St. Catherine's Medical Records Administration.**

Edward looked at Sophie.

“So St. Catherine's asked Meridian to preserve the band.”

“Yes.”

“After the receiving desk had refused to confirm the proposed reference.”

“Exactly.”

The hospital had not considered the matter resolved.

It had requested preservation of the original identification material.

The later summaries had made the reconciliation appear more complete than it was.

At 12:30, Dr Foster finished the physical examination.

Her preliminary report contained five findings.

1. The maternity register's original 2:17 a.m. entry remained intact.
2. The day-6 amendment was physically present and had not visibly replaced the original entry.
3. The neonatal band material contained the partial reference M-204.
4. The band carried a partial Meridian Receiving Unit stamp.
5. The original packaging and custody records contained an unexplained interval between day 11 and day 14.

The report included one limitation.

**The examination does not establish that the maternity entry, STC-A reference and M-204 band material relate to the same infant.**

Edward signed the acknowledgement.

He did not ask Dr Foster to go further.

At 13:20, Meridian's current records director joined the investigation call.

Her name was Rachel Dunn.

She had inherited the receiving-unit archive after Meridian's neonatal department was reorganised.

Edward explained the findings.

“Can you establish what happened to M-204 after day 11?”

“I can establish what the records show.”

“Please do.”

Rachel opened the historical log.

On day 11, the band had been received for preservation.

On day 12, a restricted access request had been submitted.

On day 13, the request had been returned for additional authority.

On day 14, the band was recorded as transferred to a separate secure storage location.

“What was the location?” Edward asked.

“Meridian's restricted clinical archive.”

“Why wasn't that recorded in the preservation summary?”

“It was recorded in the internal storage system, but the summary was prepared from the paper log.”

“Who had access to the archive?”

“Authorised records staff and the medical records supervisor.”

“Could the band have been accessed during the three-day interval?”

Rachel examined the system.

“The access log shows one entry on day 12.”

“Who?”

“The account is identified as MR-ADMIN-02.”

“An individual account?”

“No. A shared administrative account.”

Edward looked at Sophie.

Another shared account.

Another record that established access without establishing who had used it.

At 14:10, Rachel located the original access request.

It had been submitted by the Medical Records Administration office.

The stated purpose was:

**Review of original identification material before receiving-reference reconciliation.**

The request had been returned because the authorisation did not identify the person who would conduct the examination.

The return note was clear.

**Access to the archive is not approval to verify or alter the identity of the item. A named authorised examiner is required.**

Edward read it.

“Was a named examiner later appointed?”

“Yes. The day-14 record includes an appointment.”

“Who?”

Rachel checked.

“Dr Samuel Reed.”

Edward recognised the name.

Dr Reed had examined the mechanically reproduced Alexander Aurelius signature in the Northbridge schedule.

He had also reviewed other historical documents.

“Did he examine the band?”

Rachel opened the appointment record.

“No. The appointment concerned the receiving-reference reconciliation file. It did not include physical examination of the band.”

Edward frowned.

“Then why was his name associated with the band archive?”

“Because the access appointment referred to the same restricted storage collection.”

The distinction was important.

Dr Reed had been authorised to inspect a file.

That did not establish that he had examined every item in the archive.

Edward requested the actual examination report.

At 15:00, the report arrived.

It concerned the M-204 receiving-reference file.

Dr Reed had reviewed the administrative documentation on day 15.

His conclusion was limited.

**The available records do not establish a sufficient basis to confirm that M-204 corresponds to the proposed receiving reference STC-A.**

He had recommended that the original maternity source and the neonatal identification material be reviewed together by an authorised independent examiner.

That recommendation had not been completed.

Edward read the date.

Day 15.

The hospital had requested preservation.

Meridian had retained the band.

Dr Reed had reviewed the administrative documents.

And the original physical comparison had still not taken place.

“Why?” Sophie asked.

Edward scrolled to the final paragraph.

**Further examination requires approval from both the originating medical records authority and the restricted identity authority.**

He checked the approval history.

St. Catherine's had approved preservation.

It had not approved the identity comparison.

The Restricted Personal Records Authority had not responded.

At 16:05, Eleanor Vale joined the review.

Edward showed her the report.

“Can RPRA authorise the comparison now?”

“Not without establishing which records it has authority to release.”

“Can it confirm whether it received the day-15 request?”

“I can ask.”

Edward looked at the custody record.

“Who controlled the band after day 14?”

“Meridian retained physical custody.”

“And who controlled the identity comparison?”

“RPRA had authority over the protected identity materials, but not over the hospital's original maternity register.”

“So neither institution could complete the comparison alone.”

“Correct.”

Edward leaned back.

The records had been preserved in different places under different authorities.

No one had been given sufficient authority to complete the comparison.

That explained the procedural delay.

It did not explain why later summaries described the receiving reference as established.

At 16:50, Elena received a message from Isabelle.

The Valmont Foundation meeting had been confirmed for the following week.

Isabelle had sent the proposed agenda.

It included:

- Overview of the grant-management platform.
- Foundation application requirements.
- Reviewer access and conflict-of-interest controls.
- Evidence retention and decision records.
- Possible supervised use in one small funding programme.

Elena read the final item carefully.

**Possible supervised use.**

She replied that she was willing to demonstrate the system, but the foundation would need to define its own approval authority.

Isabelle responded almost immediately.

*I agree. I don't want anyone treating your platform as a substitute for the foundation's actual decision process.*

Elena smiled.

That was a better answer than she had expected.

She returned to the pilot dashboard.

One of the six organisations had submitted its corrected financial statement.

The application now showed:

Evidence received and independently reviewed

The final decision remained pending.

Elena left it that way.

At 17:35, the Aurelius family received the day's findings.

Alexander listened as Edward explained the physical examination.

“The band contains M-204,” Adrian said.

“Yes.”

“Meridian's receiving unit.”

“Yes.”

“Does that connect it to the St. Catherine's 2:17 record?”

“Not yet.”

Victoria asked, “What about the day-6 amendment?”

“It entered STC-A as the proposed receiving reference. The receiving desk did not confirm the identity.”

“And the band?”

“It was preserved at Meridian five days later. Its chain of custody has an unexplained interval.”

Damien considered the sequence.

“Then the records may be connected, but the connection remains unproven.”

“Exactly.”

Alexander nodded.

“Keep the originals protected.”

Julian looked at Edward.

“Will the missing person be in those records?”

Edward answered honestly.

“Possibly. We haven't established that yet.”

Julian looked down at his notebook.

He did not ask another question.

At 18:20, Rachel Dunn sent one final document.

It was a day-14 storage transfer certificate.

The band had been moved from the receiving unit to Meridian's restricted clinical archive.

The transfer was signed by the medical records supervisor.

But the destination field contained an additional notation.

**Storage group: R-17.**

Edward stared at the screen.

R-17 had appeared in earlier records as a restricted external personal-custody representative identifier.

It was not an identity.

It was not a family appointment.

It was not proof that the person associated with the band had any connection to the protected participant.

But the same code had now appeared in Meridian's storage documentation.

He checked the certificate again.

The field was not labelled *recipient*.

It was labelled *storage group*.

Edward added a warning to the investigation index.

**R-17 appears in a separate storage context. Do not infer that it identifies the same person, office or authority as the later external representative reference without documentary confirmation.**

Then he called Rachel.

“Do you know why the band was assigned to storage group R-17?”

“No,” she replied. “The group was created before I inherited the archive.”

“Can you locate its creation record?”

“I can try.”

“Please do.”

Rachel paused.

“There is something else.”

“What?”

“The storage group was not created by Meridian.”

Edward looked at Sophie.

“Who created it?”

Rachel opened the original system history.

“The originating institution's records authority.”

Edward waited.

Rachel read the entry.

**R-17 storage designation established under restricted preservation instruction, day 14.**

The authorising reference was:

**RPRA/14/02.**

Edward slowly closed the document.

The band had passed through St. Catherine's.

It had been preserved at Meridian.

And its restricted storage designation had been created by the same authority that had controlled the unresolved identity comparison.

The next investigation would have to establish why RPRA had requested a separate storage group—and whether that designation had been intended to protect an original identity item, or to keep it outside the ordinary receiving records system.
