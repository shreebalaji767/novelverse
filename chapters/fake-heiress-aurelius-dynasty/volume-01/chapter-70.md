# **Chapter 70 — The Woman Who Refused the Aurelius Name**

Isabella Vale arrived at the Valmont legal office shortly after ten.

She had received Edward's request the previous evening, along with a brief explanation that he had found another document connected to her maternity records.

She had not asked for details over the telephone.

By now, she had learned that every answer seemed to produce three more questions.

Edward was waiting in the smaller conference room.

A glass of water stood beside a closed folder.

"Thank you for coming," he said.

Isabella took the chair opposite him.

"You said you found another record from Meridian."

"Yes."

"Does it concern my daughter?"

Edward considered his answer carefully.

"It concerns a child whose identity was never confirmed. Your name appears in the visitor register."

Isabella's expression changed.

"What date?"

"One day after a residential review was submitted for a child identified as 28-B."

She looked at him.

"Child 28-B?"

"Yes."

"Is that the child Margaret Voss was responsible for?"

"According to the administrative records, yes."

Isabella was silent.

Edward opened the folder and placed the visitor-register copy in front of her.

She read the entry.

Her eyes moved slowly across the page.

Then she stopped at the handwritten note.

**Visitor requested that the child not be released under the Aurelius name.**

For several seconds, she did not move.

Finally, she looked up.

"Who wrote this?"

"We don't know. The hospital is trying to identify the handwriting."

"Is the entry genuine?"

"The visitor register appears authentic. The signature is consistent with your other documents."

Isabella looked down again.

"I remember going there."

Edward waited.

"I remember the hospital."

She touched the edge of the page.

"But I don't remember asking them not to use the Aurelius name."

"Could you have said it?"

"Possibly."

"Do you know why?"

Isabella closed the folder.

"I need to explain something first."

Edward nodded.

"Please."

Eighteen years earlier, Isabella Vale had been twenty-four.

She had grown up in a family that valued reputation, obedience, and appearances.

Her father, Charles Vale, was a powerful man within the private legal and administrative circles in which he worked. He was not the chairman of a global corporation, but he had enough influence to make people hesitate before refusing him.

Isabella had learned early that her father's approval was difficult to obtain.

She had also learned that his decisions were rarely discussed openly.

When she became pregnant, Charles had initially appeared pleased.

He arranged private medical care, selected the clinic, and insisted that everything be handled discreetly.

Isabella had accepted his involvement.

She had believed he was protecting her.

Then her daughter was born.

At 2:31 in the morning, a nurse placed a newborn girl beside her.

The baby was alive.

Isabella remembered the tiny movement of her fingers, the warmth of her cheek, and the small cry that had sounded impossibly loud in the quiet room.

She had been given a maternity bracelet marked IV-204.

She had seen the identification number on the baby's accompanying record.

Then, within hours, the child had been taken for what staff described as a temporary medical assessment.

Isabella was told she would see her daughter again.

She never did.

Two days later, a representative informed her that the baby had died.

There was no death certificate.

No medical report.

No explanation from the doctor who had attended the delivery.

Only a letter and an instruction to stop asking questions.

Isabella had refused.

She had visited the clinic repeatedly.

She had contacted the administration.

She had demanded the original medical records.

Eventually, Eleanor Vale had shown her a photograph of a newborn labelled Infant A.

Eleanor had said the child might be hers.

But the photograph did not match Isabella's maternity bracelet.

The comparison report had later confirmed that the two records belonged to different infants.

By then, Isabella had already begun to suspect that the story of her daughter's death was false.

She had also begun to suspect that her father knew more than he admitted.

Back in the conference room, Isabella looked at Edward.

"At the time, I had been told my daughter was dead. I didn't believe it, but I had no way to prove she was alive."

Edward listened.

"Then I was given that photograph."

"The photograph labelled Infant A?"

"Yes."

"Did Eleanor tell you the child might be connected to your maternity record?"

"She said it was possible. She also said the photograph was not proof."

"Did you believe her?"

"I wanted to."

Isabella looked toward the window.

"I was desperate for something to hold on to. I think I convinced myself that if I kept asking questions, someone would eventually tell me the truth."

Edward placed another document on the table.

"This is the residential review submitted for Child 28-B. It proposed the name Elena."

Isabella read it.

"Elena."

"Do you recognise the name?"

"No."

"Did your father ever mention it?"

"Not that I remember."

Edward showed her the visitor-register entry again.

"Why would you have asked the hospital not to release the child under the Aurelius name?"

Isabella studied the handwriting.

Then she looked up.

"Because I had been told that the Aurelius family had claimed the child."

Edward became very still.

"What?"

Isabella seemed surprised by his reaction.

"You didn't know?"

"Who told you?"

"My father."

"When?"

"Before I visited Meridian."

"How long before?"

"Perhaps a week."

Edward opened his notebook.

"What exactly did he say?"

Isabella closed her eyes, trying to remember.

"He said there was a family involved in the arrangements. A powerful family. He said they had a claim over one of the children."

"Did he identify the family?"

"Yes."

"The Aurelius family?"

"Yes."

Edward did not interrupt.

"He said the child had been placed under temporary protection and that the Aurelius office would eventually confirm the identity."

"Did he say which child?"

"No."

"Did he say why the Aurelius family had a claim?"

"No."

"Did he tell you that the child might be yours?"

Isabella's expression changed.

"No."

"Did you ask?"

"Of course I did."

"What did he say?"

"He told me I was confused. He said the photograph Eleanor had shown me was unrelated to my maternity record, and that I needed to accept the possibility that my daughter had died."

Edward watched her carefully.

"But you didn't accept it."

"No."

Isabella looked down at the register.

"I thought if the Aurelius family had a claim over a child, then perhaps someone had found my daughter and decided to give her to them."

"Why would you think that?"

"Because my father had already told me the baby had died. Then I discovered that no death record existed. Then Eleanor showed me a photograph of another infant. Everything was being kept separate."

She paused.

"I thought the Aurelius name was being used to hide the identity of a child who might actually belong to me."

Edward absorbed the explanation.

It was plausible.

It was not proof.

"Is that why you went to Meridian?"

"Yes."

"What did you ask?"

"I asked whether they had a child whose identity was connected to my maternity record."

"And?"

"They said they could not confirm anything."

"Did they mention the name Elena?"

"No."

"Did they tell you the child was 28-B?"

"I don't remember seeing that reference."

"Did you ask about the Aurelius family?"

"Yes. I asked whether they had received instructions from them."

Edward placed the register in front of her.

"Your visit was recorded as a request to verify whether the child had been assigned an originating family identity."

Isabella nodded slowly.

"That sounds like something I would have asked."

"And the final note says you requested that the child not be released under the Aurelius name."

She stared at the page.

"I remember saying that."

Edward leaned forward.

"Why?"

Isabella's voice became quieter.

"Because I didn't want them to give my daughter to a family that had no right to her."

The room fell silent.

Edward did not immediately respond.

He could see that Isabella believed what she was saying.

She had not visited Meridian to steal a child.

She had visited because she thought her daughter might be alive and that someone was preparing to transfer her under another family's identity.

But her belief had been based on what Charles told her.

And Charles had repeatedly supplied information that could not be independently verified.

"Did your father tell you that Victoria Aurelius had personally requested the child?" Edward asked.

"No."

"Did he say Alexander Aurelius had authorised anything?"

"He said the Aurelius office was involved."

"Did he show you any documents?"

"No."

"Did he tell you the name of the child?"

"No."

"Then how did he know about the Aurelius claim?"

Isabella looked uncomfortable.

"I don't know."

"Did he explain how he obtained the information?"

"No."

Edward made a note.

"Did he accompany you to Meridian?"

"No. I went alone."

"Did you speak to anyone besides the records officer?"

"Possibly a nurse. I can't remember."

"Did you see the child?"

Isabella shook her head.

"No."

"Did you request to see her?"

"I asked whether I could."

"What did they say?"

"They said the child was not available for visitors."

Edward looked at the entry.

The visitor record did not mention a request to see the child.

It recorded only an identity enquiry.

"Did you leave any written instructions?"

"No."

"Did you sign anything?"

"I don't remember."

Edward turned to the final page of the folder.

"There is no record of a custody request or transfer authorisation under your name."

"Then I didn't take her."

"No. There is no evidence that you did."

Isabella seemed relieved.

"Good."

Edward studied her.

"Why?"

"Because I never wanted to take a child whose identity I couldn't establish."

She looked at him directly.

"I wanted my daughter. I didn't want someone else's."

Edward nodded.

It was an important distinction.

The investigation had repeatedly encountered people who treated a child as an administrative problem to be assigned, transferred, renamed, or concealed.

Isabella, at least in this account, had been trying to establish who the child was before making any claim.

That did not make her memories infallible.

But it mattered.

Edward turned to another question.

"Did your father ever tell you that the child had been given the name Elena?"

"No."

"Did he mention Greenfield Children's House?"

"No."

"Margaret Voss?"

"Not by name."

"Did he mention Meridian?"

"Only when I asked about the child."

"Did he tell you what happened after your visit?"

Isabella thought for a moment.

"He said the matter had been settled."

"What did that mean?"

"I don't know. I asked him whether the child had been transferred."

"What did he say?"

"He told me not to concern myself with arrangements that did not involve me."

Edward's pen stopped.

"Did he say your daughter was dead again?"

"Yes."

"When?"

"A few days later."

"Did he provide evidence?"

"No."

Isabella's expression hardened.

"He told me that continuing to investigate would only make things worse."

Edward closed his notebook.

"Did you believe him?"

"I wanted to."

She looked down at her hands.

"I was tired. I had spent months asking questions and getting contradictory answers. I thought perhaps I was holding on to something that wasn't there."

Edward understood.

The absence of proof could wear down even a determined person.

Especially when the people withholding information were members of one's own family.

Before Isabella left, Edward asked one final question.

"Did your father ever mention a woman who claimed to be Mrs. Aurelius?"

Isabella's face changed.

"Yes."

Edward sat forward.

"Tell me."

"It was before I visited Meridian. I was at my father's office. A woman arrived with a man."

"Did you know her?"

"No."

"Did she identify herself?"

"She said she was Mrs. Aurelius."

Edward felt a familiar tension in his shoulders.

"Did she look like Victoria Aurelius?"

"I had never met Victoria at the time."

"Did she show identification?"

"No."

"Did she speak to you?"

"Only briefly."

"What did she say?"

"She asked whether I was the mother of the child who had been born at 2:31."

Edward's pen stopped.

"What did you tell her?"

"I said yes."

"What happened?"

"She asked whether I had seen the baby after the delivery."

"I said no."

Isabella looked unsettled.

"Then she asked whether I had been given a photograph."

"Did you tell her about Infant A?"

"Yes."

"What did she say?"

"She said the photograph was not mine."

Edward remained silent.

Isabella continued.

"Then she said something strange."

"What?"

"She said that if I wanted to find my daughter, I should stop asking which family had claimed her and start asking who had been paid to move her."

Edward slowly lowered his pen.

The statement was strikingly similar to the direction the investigation had taken.

"Did she explain?"

"No. My father interrupted us."

"What did he say?"

"He told her the meeting was over."

"Did she leave?"

"Yes."

"Did your father know her?"

"I don't know. He seemed angry, but not surprised to see her."

"Can you describe her?"

Isabella thought carefully.

"She was perhaps in her forties. Dark hair. Very composed. She wore a silver ring with a dark stone."

Edward's attention sharpened.

"A silver ring?"

"Yes."

"On which hand?"

"Her right hand, I think."

"Did she have any distinguishing marks?"

Isabella frowned.

"She had a small scar on her left thumb."

Edward did not move.

The description matched the woman who had appeared in earlier accounts.

The unidentified woman who had called herself Mrs. Aurelius.

The woman who had been accompanied by a man.

The woman Margaret Voss had remembered from the disputed placement arrangements.

But the description still did not establish who she was.

"Did she give you her name?" Edward asked.

"No."

"Did you ever see her again?"

"No."

Edward thanked Isabella.

She stood, then paused near the door.

"Mr. Valmont?"

"Yes?"

"If that child was my daughter, why would she have been given another name?"

Edward considered the question.

"We don't know whether she was your daughter."

"I know."

Isabella looked at the visitor-register copy.

"But someone was trying to give an unidentified child a name, while telling me my own child was dead."

Edward had no answer.

She left quietly.

That evening, Edward met Jonathan in the office.

He placed three documents on the table.

The visitor-register entry.

The residential review.

And the old maternity record bearing Isabella's identification number.

Jonathan examined them.

"She remembers the visit."

"Yes."

"And she admits she asked that the child not be released under the Aurelius name."

"Yes."

"Because Charles told her the Aureliuses had claimed the child."

"Correct."

Jonathan leaned back.

"So she believed Child 28-B might be her daughter."

"She suspected it. She had no proof."

"Did she see the child?"

"No."

"Did she attempt to take custody?"

"No."

Edward pointed to the visitor-register entry.

"Her visit was an identity enquiry. There is no transfer request."

Jonathan looked at the photograph of the unidentified woman.

"And the woman with the silver ring?"

"Isabella remembers her."

"Same description?"

"Same ring. Same scar. Same claim to be Mrs. Aurelius."

Jonathan frowned.

"That makes it more likely the accounts refer to the same woman."

"Yes."

"Can we identify her?"

"Not yet."

Edward opened the archive index.

The name of the man who had accompanied her was still unknown.

But there was a new detail.

The woman had met Isabella at Charles Vale's office before the Meridian visit.

That meant Charles knew her.

Possibly well enough to arrange a meeting.

And possibly well enough to know why she was interested in the child.

"Get the visitor records from Vale Private Counsel," Edward said.

Jonathan nodded.

"All visitors?"

"All visitors during the relevant period. Include appointment books, security registers, reception logs, and vehicle entries."

"That will take time."

"Then start with the week before Isabella's Meridian visit."

Jonathan gathered the documents.

"Anything else?"

Edward looked at the old maternity record.

"Find out whether Charles ever told Isabella where her daughter had been buried."

Jonathan stopped.

"What?"

"He told her the child had died. Did he ever tell her there had been a burial?"

Jonathan thought for a moment.

"I don't think we've asked."

"Then ask."

At the Aurelius Estate, Victoria received a copy of Isabella's statement.

She read it in silence.

Alexander sat beside her.

Adrian stood near the fireplace, reviewing the investigation notes.

"She believed the child might be hers," Victoria said.

"Yes," Edward replied over the call.

"Because Charles told her the Aureliuses had claimed the child."

"Yes."

Victoria looked down.

"Did anyone from our office ever contact Isabella?"

"Not according to the records we've found."

"Then someone was using our name to influence her."

"It appears so."

Alexander's expression was severe.

"Whoever this woman was, she was not authorised to speak for us."

"Agreed," Edward said.

Victoria looked again at the description.

"Silver ring. Dark stone. Scar on the left thumb."

"Yes."

"I remember a similar description in the earlier testimony."

"Margaret Voss described a woman with the same features."

Adrian turned from the fireplace.

"Could she have been the person who arranged the placements?"

"Possibly," Edward said. "But we need to identify her before assigning her any role."

Alexander nodded.

"Find her."

Victoria remained quiet.

She was thinking about the question Isabella had asked.

If the child was hers, why had someone tried to give her another name?

And if the child was not hers, why had someone repeatedly used Isabella's maternity record in arrangements involving unidentified infants?

The investigation was no longer simply about a missing Aurelius daughter.

It involved a woman who had been told her child was dead.

A family that had been denied the truth.

And several administrative identities that had been manipulated until nobody could reliably say which child belonged to which mother.

Victoria looked toward the closed door.

"Alexander."

He turned.

"If that woman was pretending to be me, I want her found."

Alexander reached for her hand.

"We will find her."

At 11:12 p.m., Edward received a call from Jonathan.

"I found something."

"What?"

"The visitor register from Vale Private Counsel."

Edward sat up.

"Is the woman listed?"

"Yes."

"Name?"

Jonathan hesitated.

"That's the interesting part."

"Tell me."

"The appointment was entered under the name Eleanor Vale."

Edward went still.

"Eleanor?"

"Yes. But the security register records a different person entering the building."

"Who?"

Jonathan read the name.

**Marianne Cross.**

Edward opened his notebook.

The name was unfamiliar.

"Do we have an address?"

"An old one. The company listed her as an external consultant."

"Photograph?"

"Not yet."

Jonathan paused.

"But there is another entry."

"What?"

"She visited Charles Vale's office again three days after the Meridian discharge."

Edward looked at the dates.

Three days after Child 28-B had left the hospital.

"Was she alone?"

"No."

"Who accompanied her?"

Jonathan read the second name.

**Gabriel Vale.**

Edward slowly lowered the phone.

Gabriel had taken Infant A from St Catherine's on the sixth day.

Gabriel had verified Victoria's false signature.

Gabriel had been involved in the original identity-protection arrangements.

And now, according to the visitor register, he had met Marianne Cross three days after Child 28-B was discharged from Meridian.

Edward looked again at the description of the woman.

Silver ring.

Dark stone.

Scar on left thumb.

The woman who had called herself Mrs. Aurelius.

The woman who had told Isabella to investigate the money.

The woman who had visited Charles Vale's office under Eleanor Vale's name.

For the first time, the investigation had a name that could be pursued.

But it had also uncovered another question.

Why had Gabriel Vale met her after the child left Meridian?

And what had they arranged together?
