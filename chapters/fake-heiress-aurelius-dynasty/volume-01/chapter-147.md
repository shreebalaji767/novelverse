# **Chapter 147 — The Appointment That Ended Without Ending**

The original day-132 nomination schedule was not in the ordinary archive.

Edward had expected that.

What he had not expected was the number of records referring to it without reproducing its contents.

The Family Allocation Review Office had a withdrawal notice.

Meridian had a custody reference.

Cross Administrative Services had a receipt.

Vale Private Counsel had a restricted routing entry.

None contained the nominee's full identity.

Together, however, they established that a proposed personal representation custodian had been selected, that the appointment was withdrawn on day 132, and that the withdrawal had been distributed to several offices.

The identity remained sealed.

Edward placed the four records across his desk.

"Someone managed to make a withdrawal more widely available than the original appointment," Jonathan observed.

"That is not necessarily deliberate."

"But it is convenient."

Edward glanced at him.

"Convenience isn't evidence."

Jonathan accepted the correction.

They had learned enough from the previous months to distrust explanations that were too neat.

"Where is the original?" Edward asked.

"Meridian's restricted custody division. The index says it was transferred from V-17 on day 133."

"Along with the personal acknowledgement?"

"Possibly. The transfer inventory lists one sealed package, but it doesn't name the contents."

"Then we need the package history."

Jonathan opened another folder.

"There's a complication. The withdrawal schedule and the acknowledgement record were placed under separate preservation references. The package inventory does not identify whether they were together."

Edward read the references.

**PA-132 — PERSONAL ACKNOWLEDGEMENT RECORD**

**AV-18 — REPRESENTATION DESIGNATION**

The same day, the same custody route, different records.

"Let's start with the person who accepted the day-150 transfer," Edward said.

"Anna Mercer-Smith?"

"We don't know that yet."

Anna Mercer-Smith arrived at Meridian shortly after lunch.

She had agreed to review the original day-150 receipt, but only after Edward explained that they were not asking her to identify the protected principal.

She read the document carefully.

Then she looked at the initials.

"A.M."

"Do they resemble yours?" Edward asked.

"They are my initials."

"Does that establish you received the document?"

"No."

She returned the receipt.

"I used those initials on some administrative records, but so did other people in the correspondence division. A.M. is not a unique identifier."

"Did you ever accept custody of a personal acknowledgement record?"

"I accepted sealed correspondence for preservation. I did not accept authority over the person who sent it."

"Could this have been one of those transfers?"

"It could."

Edward showed her the second handwritten line.

**Authority withdrawn. Custody receipt remains valid for preservation only.**

Anna read it twice.

"That wording is important."

"Why?"

"Because it distinguishes two things that should never have been combined."

She placed the receipt on the table.

"A person can lose authority to represent someone while still being permitted to preserve documents already entrusted to them. The withdrawal doesn't automatically erase the custody obligation."

"Did you have such an obligation?"

"Not personally. My administrative role ended before day 132."

"Then you could not have received this as an appointed representative."

"Correct."

"But you might have received it as a courier or preservation intermediary."

"Yes. If the transfer was properly authorised."

Edward studied her.

"Would you remember accepting a sealed package of this kind?"

Anna thought for a moment.

"I remember one instruction concerning the personal acknowledgement. I was told that no copy should be attached to Gabriel Vale's conditional response."

"Who told you?"

"I don't remember the speaker. It came through the correspondence division."

"Was the instruction written?"

"I believe so."

"Do you remember the date?"

"After the day-132 withdrawal. Before the day-150 transfer."

Edward exchanged a glance with Jonathan.

"Did you accept the package?"

Anna shook her head.

"I don't know. I remember the instruction, not the delivery. I would be misleading you if I turned one memory into the other."

Edward nodded.

"Did you ever see a completed principal acknowledgement?"

"No."

"Did anyone tell you that the principal had signed?"

"No."

"Did anyone tell you the principal had refused?"

Anna looked at him.

"No. And I would have questioned either statement without the original document."

"Why?"

"Because I had already seen how easily an administrative receipt could be described as something more."

She stood.

"Mr Valmont, I want to make one thing clear. If the initials belong to me, that still does not mean I was acting as the principal's representative. I was not."

"I understand."

"Please make sure the record says so."

Edward promised that it would.

After she left, Jonathan wrote a note beside the initials.

**A.M. — POSSIBLE ANNA MERCER-SMITH. NOT ATTRIBUTED.**

Edward underlined the last two words.

The next lead came from the Family Allocation Review Office.

Its former records supervisor, Helen Ward, had retained a copy of the day-132 withdrawal distribution list.

She met Edward in a small conference room at the office's successor institution.

"The withdrawal was treated as a formal administrative event," she explained. "Once entered, the previous designation could no longer be used for new decisions."

"Was the nominee informed?"

"According to the distribution list, yes."

"Was the nominee identified?"

"Not on this copy."

"Who was informed?"

Helen turned the page.

"The proposed representative's correspondence address was notified. The personal custody division was notified. The preservation office was notified."

"Did the proposed representative acknowledge receipt?"

"The field is blank."

"Was the withdrawal signed?"

"The original had a signature block. The copy we retained does not show a signature."

Edward placed the day-150 transfer receipt beside it.

"Could the person who received this transfer have been the withdrawn nominee?"

Helen studied the initials.

"Possibly. But the withdrawal did not necessarily prevent the person from acting as a preservation intermediary."

"Who could authorise that?"

"The originating office could request preservation. The custodian could accept documents. Neither action would restore the person's representative authority."

"Was a new appointment made?"

"No."

"Was the original appointment replaced by another person?"

Helen shook her head.

"Not according to our register."

Edward looked at the blank field.

"Then who was responsible for the principal's personal representation after day 132?"

Helen did not answer immediately.

"That was the problem," she said finally.

"Explain."

"The appointment was withdrawn because the proposed representative's authority had not been independently established. But no replacement was confirmed."

"Was the principal informed?"

"I cannot establish that."

"Was the principal's right to respond preserved?"

"The records say it was."

"Was anyone appointed to receive that response?"

Helen looked at the papers.

"Not formally."

Edward leaned back.

The arrangement had created a peculiar situation.

The principal's right to respond remained intact.

The representative's authority had been withdrawn.

The original procedure had not been delivered.

And the records continued to refer to a personal acknowledgement that could not be located or verified.

"Did you question this at the time?" he asked.

"I did."

"What happened?"

"I received a written instruction that the withdrawal affected representation authority only. It did not affect document preservation or the pending communication process."

"Who sent it?"

Helen checked the reference.

"V-17. Personal Custody."

"Signed?"

"No."

Edward asked for a copy.

Helen made a request to the archive.

It would take time.

At the Aurelius Estate, Victoria was reading the latest report from Edward.

Alexander stood beside the window, reviewing a separate set of documents.

The report contained no new evidence of biological identity.

It did, however, describe the withdrawn personal representation appointment and the continued preservation of an acknowledgement copy.

Victoria set it down.

"Someone was allowed to keep the documents after losing the authority to represent the person."

"That isn't automatically improper," Alexander said.

"No. But it creates a responsibility. Someone must have been able to distinguish between preservation and representation."

"Or they deliberately kept those responsibilities separate because they knew the appointment was not valid."

Victoria considered that.

"Then why wasn't the principal given the original procedure?"

Alexander did not answer.

They had asked the same question repeatedly.

The records showed that the procedure had been prepared. They showed that it had been retained. They showed that multiple people knew it had not been delivered.

They did not show that the protected principal had ever received it.

Victoria turned to the report's final page.

"Edward is still being careful."

"He should be."

"He hasn't assumed the principal is connected to our family."

"Nor should he."

Victoria looked toward the garden.

"I keep thinking about the person who asked whether they could withdraw without giving a representative response."

Alexander placed his papers down.

"That question could have come from the principal."

"Or someone acting for them."

"Yes."

"Either way, they are asking about a right that should never have depended on the representative."

Alexander nodded.

"Then we should find out whether the original procedure was ever delivered before we consider anything else."

Victoria folded the report.

For eighteen years, they had lived with the possibility that someone had made a decision about their missing daughter without their knowledge.

Now they were investigating another person whose identity had been protected so thoroughly that even the right to answer a question appeared to have become trapped inside administrative procedures.

She did not know whether the two histories were connected.

She only knew that neither should be decided by assumption.

At the Valmont Estate, Dr Mira Sen had completed a preliminary comparison of the day-150 transfer receipt with the original day-132 withdrawal record.

Edward read her findings.

"The handwriting on the second line is consistent with the anonymous writer from the day-48 and day-118 instructions," she said.

"Consistent, not attributed?"

"Exactly. There are shared characteristics in the formation of several letters and the spacing. But the documents are not sufficient to identify the writer."

"Could the same person have written the withdrawal?"

"Possibly. The withdrawal itself contains two separate entries. The main text and the handwritten instruction at the bottom appear to be different hands."

Edward studied the page.

The handwritten instruction read:

*The proposed representative's authority must not be treated as continuing beyond this date.*

Beneath it was a second sentence.

*Preservation of personal material may continue only under a separate custody record.*

"That second sentence," Edward said. "Is it the same hand?"

"Yes. It is consistent with the anonymous writer's other notes."

"Then the person who insisted on separating representation from preservation may also have written the instructions preventing the original procedure from being released."

"That is a reasonable investigative lead."

"Could it be Eleanor?"

Dr Sen shook her head.

"I compared it with Eleanor Vale's verified correspondence. The writing is different."

"Charles?"

"Also different."

"Gabriel?"

"Different."

"Marianne?"

"Different."

Edward looked at the list.

The anonymous writer had now appeared in several places:

- The day-48 instruction preventing release of the original procedure.
- The day-118 instruction repeating the restriction.
- The day-132 withdrawal note.
- The day-133 custody restriction.
- The day-150 instruction separating preservation from representation.

The writer had not simply been trying to prevent documents from being lost.

They had repeatedly insisted that a person could not be treated as a representative merely because they held documents.

But the same person had also helped keep the original procedure out of the principal's hands.

The contradiction was becoming difficult to ignore.

Jonathan returned the next morning with the day-150 transfer history.

The package had not been delivered directly to V-17.

It had first passed through Meridian's restricted intake.

The transfer sequence was:

- Day 149: acknowledgement reproduction reviewed.
- Day 150: supporting material transferred under personal representation custody.
- Day 151: receipt entered at V-17.
- Day 152: custody classification amended.
- Day 153: document separated from the general PR-06 file.

The last entry was especially interesting.

**Custody classification amended: PERSONAL MATERIAL — NOT REPRESENTATIVE RESPONSE.**

The operator field contained a name.

Not initials.

Not a shared account.

A named individual.

Edward read it.

**Margaret Voss.**

He looked up.

"Did she approve the amendment?"

"Her account recorded it. The approval field is blank."

"Have you asked her?"

"Not yet."

"Do that."

Jonathan hesitated.

"There's something else."

He placed a second document on the table.

It was a handwritten instruction dated day 152.

*The copy shall remain separate from the representative's response. It shall not be treated as evidence of principal acknowledgement unless the original is examined under the required authority.*

The handwriting was not Margaret's.

It was the same anonymous hand.

Edward read the sentence again.

The copy was not to be treated as evidence of acknowledgement.

That was consistent with everything Dr Sen had found.

But it raised a new question.

If the copy was not evidence of acknowledgement, why had it been transferred at all?

And why had Margaret Voss changed its custody classification?

Margaret Voss received Edward at her solicitor's office that afternoon.

She read the day-153 amendment without showing surprise.

"I remember this."

"Did you enter it?"

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because the reproduction had been placed in the wrong file."

"Which file?"

"The representative's communication record."

"Gabriel Vale's conditional response?"

"Yes."

"Why was that a problem?"

"Because the copy concerned the principal's personal acknowledgement. It was not Gabriel's response."

"Did you know whether the original was signed?"

"No."

"Did you know who had made the copy?"

"No."

"Did you know who had delivered it?"

Margaret shook her head.

"I received an instruction to preserve it separately. I followed the instruction because it was correct."

"Who gave you the instruction?"

She placed the document down.

"I don't know."

"Was it written?"

"Yes."

"Do you still have it?"

Margaret opened a drawer and removed a narrow envelope.

It had been kept with her original correspondence.

The front read:

**PERSONAL MATERIAL — CUSTODY SEPARATION**

The reverse contained the reference:

**PA-132 / V-17 / DAY 152**

Edward looked at her.

"You kept this?"

"I keep instructions that affect the classification of original records."

"Why?"

"Because people tend to remember the action and forget the authority."

She opened the envelope and removed a single sheet.

The instruction was brief.

*The reproduced acknowledgement is not to be merged with the representative's conditional response. Its existence does not establish that the principal received the original procedure, understood the proposed arrangement, or personally accepted any appointment.*

The final line was handwritten.

*If the principal has not received the original procedure, no conclusion may be drawn from the absence of a personal response.*

Edward looked at the sentence.

It was one of the clearest statements they had found.

"Did you know who wrote it?"

"No."

"Did you ask?"

"I asked the records office to identify the originating authority. They returned the request because the instruction had been delivered under restricted personal custody."

"Who delivered it?"

"An unnamed courier."

"Did you see them?"

"No."

"Was there a receipt?"

"Yes."

Edward waited.

Margaret opened another folder.

"The receipt was not attached to the instruction. It was retained separately because the sender requested that the courier's identity remain restricted."

She placed it on the table.

The receipt contained a date, a time, and a delivery reference.

At the bottom was a short designation:

**DELIVERED BY: P-04 PERSONAL CONTACT — VERIFIED THROUGH ORIGINAL CHANNEL.**

Edward read it twice.

For the first time, the records did not merely refer to a protected principal as an administrative code.

They indicated that a person connected to P-04 had personally delivered an instruction.

But the wording did not establish whether the principal had written it, carried it, or authorised someone else to deliver it.

"Do you know what original channel means?" Edward asked.

Margaret shook her head.

"No. But I remember the accompanying instruction."

"What did it say?"

She looked at the receipt.

"That the courier's identity was not to be entered into the ordinary distribution register."

Edward studied the designation.

A person connected to P-04 had reached the records system.

Someone had delivered an instruction insisting that the principal's absence could not be treated as consent.

And that instruction had been preserved.

Yet the original procedure remained undelivered.

Edward carefully placed the receipt back in its sleeve.

"Find the original-channel verification register," he told Jonathan later. "If this delivery was verified, there must be a record of how."

Jonathan nodded.

"And if there isn't?"

Edward looked at the designation one last time.

"Then someone used the language of verified personal contact without establishing that contact."

He closed the folder.

Either possibility would change the investigation.

Neither yet told them who P-04 was.
