# **Chapter 80 — Eleanor's Instruction**

Edward placed the transfer receipt beside the other documents and read the handwritten instruction again.

**The original card must remain separate from the material held by Victoria Aurelius.**

**E.V.**

Eleanor Vale.

The instruction was dated the fifteenth day after the births.

Two days before Victoria had formally received the envelope containing the reference to Isabella's maternity identification band.

That timing bothered him.

Eleanor had known that Victoria possessed, or was about to possess, material connected to IV-204.

She had also known that the original St Catherine's identification card was being transferred to Adrian Holt.

And she had specifically instructed that the two items remain separate.

Jonathan stood beside the desk.

"Perhaps she was trying to prevent the two pieces from being combined without authorisation."

"That is possible."

"Isn't that exactly what we've been trying to establish? Whether the comparison was conducted properly?"

Edward picked up the receipt.

"Yes. But there is a difference between preventing an unauthorised comparison and preventing an authorised one."

Jonathan nodded.

"Then we need to ask Eleanor."

Edward looked at the clock.

"Not yet."

"Why?"

"Because I want to know whether this instruction was written before or after the transfer was arranged."

He turned the receipt towards the light.

The initials appeared beneath a handwritten date.

But the paper itself had a faint impression from another document.

Edward took a magnifying lens from his drawer.

There were several pressure marks beneath the visible writing.

A line of text could be partially distinguished.

**Pending receipt of original maternal identification material.**

He lowered the lens.

"Jonathan, this receipt may not have been written on the fifteenth day."

"What do you mean?"

"The transfer date could be genuine. The instruction may have been added later."

Jonathan examined the paper.

"Can we prove that?"

"Not from this copy. We'll need the original."

Edward placed the receipt inside a protective sleeve.

"Ask Gabriel's solicitor to produce it."

Eleanor Vale received Edward the following afternoon.

She had agreed to meet at her home, provided that the discussion remained limited to the historical records and that her solicitor could be present.

Edward and Jonathan arrived shortly after two.

Eleanor was waiting in a modest sitting room.

She looked older than she had during their earlier meetings, but her manner remained composed.

"Mr. Valmont."

"Ms. Vale."

She gestured towards the chairs.

"I understand you've found another document."

Edward placed a copy of the transfer receipt on the table.

Eleanor looked at it.

Her expression changed.

Not dramatically.

But enough.

"You recognise it," Edward said.

"Yes."

"Is the instruction yours?"

Eleanor read the handwritten line.

"Yes."

"Did you write it on the fifteenth day?"

"No."

Edward waited.

She placed the document down.

"I wrote it later."

"How much later?"

"The eighteenth day."

Jonathan glanced at Edward.

"Then why does the receipt carry the fifteenth-day date?"

"Because the transfer was arranged on the fifteenth day. The instruction was added when I learned that the original maternity material had been sent to the Aurelius Family Office."

Edward leaned forward.

"Who told you?"

"Marianne Cross."

"What did she tell you?"

"That Victoria Aurelius had received an envelope referring to Isabella Vale's maternity identification band."

"Did she tell you the band itself was inside?"

"No. She said the material was being held separately."

"Did you know it was an empty pouch?"

Eleanor nodded.

"Yes."

"How?"

"Marianne told me."

Edward studied her.

"Why did you instruct Adrian Holt to keep the original St Catherine's card separate?"

"Because the two documents were not yet ready to be compared."

"Why not?"

"Because the card and the band had not been verified as belonging to the infants described in their respective records."

Jonathan frowned.

"But the card was an original hospital identification card."

"That doesn't mean it proves which infant wore it at every stage."

Edward recognised the argument.

It was the same distinction that had repeatedly appeared in the transfer paperwork.

Eleanor continued.

"An identification card can establish that a hospital created a record under a particular reference. It cannot, by itself, prove that the same physical card remained with the same infant throughout several transfers."

"Then what was needed?"

"An uninterrupted chain of identification, supported by the original medical records and an independent examination."

"Did you believe the records were unreliable?"

"I believed they were incomplete."

"Did you believe someone had altered them?"

Eleanor hesitated.

"Yes."

"Who?"

"I suspected several people."

"Charles?"

"Yes."

"Gabriel?"

"At times."

"Marianne?"

"Yes."

"Adrian Holt?"

Eleanor looked at him.

"No. I believed he was trying to preserve the evidence."

Edward took out his notebook.

"Did you believe the Aurelius family was being misled?"

Eleanor's expression softened.

"I believed they were being given documents that looked more conclusive than they were."

"By whom?"

"Charles."

"Did you tell Alexander or Victoria?"

"No."

"Why?"

Eleanor looked down.

"Because I had been instructed not to communicate directly with them."

"By whom?"

"Charles."

Edward's expression hardened.

"You were willing to follow Charles's instructions, but not his instructions concerning the identity records."

"I followed some of them."

"Why?"

"Because I thought the records needed protection."

"From whom?"

Eleanor looked at him.

"From everyone who wanted an answer before the evidence could support one."

Edward placed the clinic's consent form on the table.

"Isabella Vale gave written consent for the comparison on the sixteenth day."

"Yes."

"You knew that."

"Yes."

"Then why did you submit an instruction to keep the original card separate?"

"Because the comparison request was not limited to the card and the band."

"What else was required?"

Eleanor folded her hands.

"The original maternity admission record, the neonatal identification record, the transfer register and the receiving institution's acknowledgement."

"All of which existed?"

"Not all in their original form."

"Which were missing?"

"The receiving acknowledgement."

Edward looked at her.

"Which receiving acknowledgement?"

"The one that should have confirmed the infant associated with STC-A had been received by the institution named in the transfer instruction."

Jonathan turned towards him.

"The sixth-day transfer."

Eleanor nodded.

"Yes."

Edward looked back at her.

"Gabriel signed the transfer instruction."

"He signed the administrative document."

"Was the child physically transferred?"

"I was told that the transfer had proceeded."

"Did you witness it?"

"No."

"Did you see a receiving acknowledgement?"

"No."

"Then you didn't know whether the child on the transfer record was actually Infant A."

"Correct."

Edward closed his notebook.

That was the first direct explanation that connected Eleanor's insistence on separate records with the missing sixth-day receiving confirmation.

But it created another problem.

"Why did you allow the transfer to proceed if the receiving acknowledgement was missing?"

Eleanor looked troubled.

"Because the child required continued medical care."

"Was the destination a medical facility?"

"It was supposed to be."

"Was that verified?"

"No."

"Then what did you know?"

"Only that the transfer had been arranged and that Gabriel was coordinating the paperwork."

Edward remained silent.

Eleanor looked at the documents.

"I made a mistake."

Jonathan waited.

"I believed that keeping the records separate would prevent anyone from assigning the wrong identity to a child. I did not understand how far the administrative arrangement had already gone."

"Did you ever see the child?"

"Yes."

"When?"

"At Meridian."

"Was it the infant associated with IV-204?"

Eleanor shook her head.

"I cannot establish that."

"Did you see a second infant?"

"Yes."

"At St Catherine's?"

"No. At Meridian."

Edward looked at Jonathan.

Two infants had been present.

One was associated with the St Catherine's reference.

The other with Isabella's maternity record.

But the surviving paperwork did not establish whether either infant had been correctly identified at the time Eleanor saw them.

He returned his attention to her.

"Did you know where the St Catherine's card went after Adrian Holt received it?"

"No."

"Did you know Gabriel had taken it back?"

Eleanor frowned.

"Taken it back?"

Edward explained the new custody record.

Eleanor listened without interrupting.

When he finished, she shook her head.

"I did not know that."

"Did you ever speak to Gabriel about the card?"

"Yes."

"When?"

"On the twelfth day."

Edward immediately looked up.

"What did he say?"

"He told me Northbridge had refused to accept the card."

"Why?"

"He said the receiving office did not have authority to accept it without the complete originating materials."

"Did he tell you he had taken possession of it?"

"He said he would keep it protected."

"Did you know he had physically collected it from Northbridge?"

"No."

Edward wrote that down.

Eleanor had known the card was being retained.

She had not known the exact chain of custody.

Or she was choosing her words carefully.

He had no proof either way.

"There's another matter," Edward said.

Eleanor looked up.

"On the seventeenth day, you instructed Adrian Holt to keep the St Catherine's card separate from the material held by Victoria Aurelius."

"Yes."

"Did you know Victoria had not received the actual band?"

"Yes."

"Then what did you believe she was holding?"

"A reference to the band and a written description of its origin."

"Did you know the band itself was at Meridian?"

"Yes."

"Who had custody?"

"Initially, the medical records department. Later, I collected the sealed envelope."

"On the twenty-eighth day."

"Yes."

Edward placed the Meridian receipt before her.

"Why did you collect it?"

"Because the clinic and Meridian had requested that the original identification material remain under a single medical records custodian until the identity review could proceed."

"Why didn't you send it to Northbridge?"

"Because the review had been suspended."

"By Dr. Lang."

"Yes."

"Did you attempt to restart it?"

Eleanor was silent.

Edward waited.

"Yes," she eventually said.

"When?"

"On the thirty-first day."

"To whom?"

"Charles Vale."

"Not the hospital?"

"No."

"Not the Aurelius Family Office?"

"No."

"Why Charles?"

"Because he was the person who had arranged the external review."

Edward looked at her.

"And what did he say?"

"He told me the comparison was no longer necessary."

"Why?"

"He said the families had already been informed of the provisional arrangements."

Edward's expression changed.

"Which families?"

Eleanor looked down.

"The Valmonts and the Aureliuses."

"Had they?"

"I don't know."

"Did you ask?"

"No."

"Did you believe him?"

Eleanor took a long breath.

"I wanted to believe that the children had been placed safely and that the identity question would be resolved later."

Edward's voice became firmer.

"You had an original identification band, an unresolved transfer, an incomplete comparison and no receiving confirmation. Yet you accepted a statement that the matter was no longer necessary."

"I did."

"Why?"

"Because I was tired."

The answer was quiet.

For a moment, Edward said nothing.

Eleanor looked at the table.

"I had spent weeks trying to keep documents from being altered, trying to stop people from making claims that could not be supported, and trying to ensure that the infants were not moved without proper medical arrangements. Every time I raised a concern, someone told me the arrangements had already been made."

She looked up.

"I believed I was preventing something worse."

Edward studied her.

"Did you succeed?"

Eleanor did not answer.

When Edward and Jonathan returned to the car, neither spoke immediately.

Jonathan finally broke the silence.

"She admits the instruction was hers."

"Yes."

"And she knew the original band was being kept at Meridian."

"Yes."

"She also says she didn't know Gabriel had taken the St Catherine's card back from Northbridge."

"Correct."

Jonathan looked at the house.

"Do you believe her?"

Edward considered it.

"I believe she has admitted facts that make her look negligent. That doesn't prove she's telling us everything."

"She seemed genuinely upset."

"She may be."

Jonathan opened the car door.

"What's next?"

"Find the missing receiving acknowledgement."

"From the sixth-day transfer?"

"Yes."

Edward looked at the folder.

"Everything still comes back to that."

The next morning, Edward contacted Meridian Children's Medical Centre.

The hospital had already supplied several records, but the new discovery required a different search.

He requested the original transfer receipt for the infant associated with IV-204.

The records officer, Mr. Kunal Shah, asked for the relevant references.

Edward supplied them.

IV-204.

STC-A.

A-17.

M-204.

Shah searched the surviving records.

After a few minutes, he stopped.

"There is a receipt."

Edward sat forward.

"Where?"

"Under the original Meridian transfer archive."

"Was it issued on the sixth day?"

"Yes."

"Who received the infant?"

Shah read the entry.

**Receiving institution: Northbridge Clinical Review Unit.**

**Infant reference: IV-204.**

**Transport representative: Gabriel Vale.**

**Receiving medical officer: Not entered.**

Edward frowned.

"Was there a signature?"

"There's a receiving acknowledgement attached."

"Can you read it?"

Shah enlarged the scanned document.

The first section contained the usual information.

Infant reference.

Transfer date.

Medical condition.

Transport details.

Then Edward saw the receiving signature.

It was not Gabriel's.

It was not Dr. Peter Lang's.

It was not Dr. Samuel Hale's.

It belonged to a woman.

**M. Cross.**

Edward stared at the screen.

Marianne had signed for the infant.

Not the identification card.

Not the records.

The infant.

Jonathan, standing beside him, read the name.

"She told us she had only delivered documents."

Edward said nothing.

He examined the rest of the receipt.

A small handwritten notation appeared beside the signature.

**Receiving party accepts temporary medical responsibility pending originating identity confirmation.**

Below it was another line.

**Infant identification band replaced on arrival.**

Edward looked at the date.

The sixth day.

The same day Isabella's IV-204 infant had been transferred from the maternity clinic.

The same day the St Catherine's Infant A transfer had been arranged.

But the receipt did not state that both infants had arrived.

It identified only one.

IV-204.

Edward read the receiving address.

**Northbridge Clinical Review Unit — Medical Receiving Ward.**

Then he looked at the bottom of the page.

A second reference had been written there.

**STC-A — Comparison pending.**

He leaned back.

The transfer that had been treated as a possible connection between two infant records had a receiving acknowledgement.

But the acknowledgement belonged specifically to IV-204.

It did not say that Infant A had been received.

And Marianne Cross had signed for the child.

Edward contacted Marianne's solicitor immediately.

He requested an explanation for the sixth-day receiving signature.

The solicitor replied within an hour.

Marianne would meet him again.

This time, she did not request that the meeting be delayed.

She arrived at Edward's office shortly after four.

Her solicitor accompanied her.

Edward placed the scanned receipt on the table.

Marianne looked at it.

Then she looked at him.

"You told us you had only delivered the records," Edward said.

"I did."

"You signed for an infant."

Marianne's expression tightened.

"Yes."

"Why didn't you mention that?"

"Because I was not asked whether I had signed a receiving acknowledgement."

"You were asked whether you had physically received the child."

Marianne looked down.

"I did not physically transport the infant."

"That is not what the document says."

She examined the receipt.

"I was the receiving administrative representative."

"On the sixth day."

"Yes."

"At Northbridge."

"Yes."

"Who brought the infant?"

"Gabriel."

Edward remained silent.

Marianne continued.

"He arrived with a medical transport team. I signed the receiving acknowledgement because the receiving physician was not present at that moment."

"Was the infant associated with IV-204?"

"The document says so."

"Did you verify the band?"

"I checked the temporary reference."

"Was the original band attached?"

"No."

Edward leaned forward.

"Then how did you know the infant was IV-204?"

Marianne looked at him.

"The transport paperwork identified the child."

"Was there an independent medical confirmation?"

"Not at that moment."

"Did you see the original maternity identification band?"

"No."

"Did you see the original St Catherine's identification card?"

"No."

Edward placed another document beside the receipt.

It was the original transfer instruction signed by Gabriel.

**Originating record: IV-204.**

**Cross-reference: STC-A.**

**Do not treat cross-reference as proof of infant identity.**

Marianne read it.

"That's the instruction we received."

Edward studied her.

"Why was STC-A written on the receiving receipt if it was not proof of identity?"

"Because the comparison was pending."

"Who told you to write it?"

"Gabriel."

"Did he explain what it meant?"

"He said the infant's originating reference was IV-204, and that STC-A was an associated comparison reference."

"Associated how?"

"He didn't explain."

Edward waited.

Marianne looked at the receipt again.

"Mr. Valmont, I was not responsible for determining the child's identity. I was responsible for receiving the infant into temporary administrative custody."

"Did you see the child again?"

"Yes."

"When?"

"On the ninth day."

"Where?"

"At Northbridge."

"Was the infant still identified as IV-204?"

Marianne nodded.

"Yes."

"Was the original band present?"

"No. A temporary receiving band had been issued."

Edward looked at Jonathan.

The original IV-204 band had been removed at Meridian.

The infant had arrived at Northbridge under a replacement reference.

The St Catherine's cross-reference had been carried forward in the paperwork.

But no one had established that the infant was the child recorded as Infant A.

He turned back to Marianne.

"Did you receive a second infant on the sixth day?"

Marianne's face changed.

"Not at the same time."

"Did you receive another infant later?"

"Yes."

"Which one?"

"An infant identified as STC-A."

Edward went very still.

"Where?"

"At a different receiving point."

"When?"

"The ninth day."

"Who delivered the infant?"

"Gabriel."

"Did you sign a receiving acknowledgement?"

Marianne hesitated.

"I believe so."

"Where is it?"

"I don't know."

Edward placed his pen down.

"Then we need to find it."

Marianne looked at him.

"I understand."

Edward collected the documents.

Two infants.

Two receiving events.

Two different dates.

And the same administrative representative.

One infant had arrived under IV-204, with a cross-reference to STC-A.

The other had arrived under STC-A.

The records had been kept separate because nobody had verified their identities.

But now the investigation had established something important.

The two infants had not simply been confused in a single hospital register.

They had been handled as separate receiving cases by the same network of people.

And Marianne Cross had personally accepted both.

That night, Edward returned to his office alone.

He spread the new documents across the desk.

The sixth-day receipt.

The original IV-204 transfer instruction.

The ninth-day reference to STC-A.

The Northbridge custody register.

The original St Catherine's identification card.

He drew two lines on a sheet of paper.

**IV-204 — Isabella Vale's maternity record.**

**STC-A — St Catherine's Infant A.**

The sixth-day transfer had carried IV-204.

The ninth-day receiving record appeared to carry STC-A.

The two references had crossed paths, but they had not been merged.

He wrote beneath them:

**No confirmed biological identity.**

Then he added another question.

**Were the children transferred separately because their identities were known to be different, or because someone wanted them treated as different?**

He looked at the clock.

It was nearly midnight.

His telephone vibrated.

A message had arrived from Jonathan.

It contained a photograph of a newly recovered document.

The heading read:

**NORTHBRIDGE CLINICAL RECEIVING REGISTER — DAY NINE**

Edward enlarged the image.

The entry for STC-A was visible.

Receiving representative: Marianne Cross.

Transport representative: Gabriel Vale.

Temporary medical custodian: Dr. Peter Lang.

But the final column contained something unexpected.

**Previous receiving reference: IV-204.**

Edward stared at the words.

The STC-A infant had been recorded as having a previous receiving reference of IV-204.

That meant the same reference appeared in two separate receiving records.

Or someone had deliberately carried the number from one infant's file into another.

Edward read the entry again.

Then he called Jonathan.

"Where did you find this?"

"In the Northbridge successor archive."

"Is it an original?"

"Apparently. The records officer is checking the paper register."

Edward looked at the two infant references on his desk.

For eighteen years, the investigation had treated the cross-reference between IV-204 and STC-A as an administrative link that had never been verified.

Now the receiving register suggested something much more complicated.

The reference IV-204 had not merely been compared with STC-A.

It had been carried forward as a previous receiving identity.

And someone had written it into the record of the second infant.
