# **Chapter 141 — The Request That Had No Collector**

The first problem with the day-133 request was that nobody had signed it.

The second was that somebody had nevertheless expected it to be obeyed.

Edward placed the photocopy between himself and Margaret Voss.

The document was short.

**V-17 — PERSONAL CUSTODY**

**REQUEST FOR COLLECTION**

Item: PR-06 / Original Communication Procedure — Control Copy\
Current custodian: Margaret Voss\
Requested action: Transfer to authorised personal custody representative\
Purpose: Completion of pending principal communication record

At the bottom, a line had been left blank.

**Collector's name and verification: Pending.**

A handwritten instruction followed.

*Do not release until the principal's identity is independently confirmed.*

Edward read it twice.

“Was that written before or after the request was refused?”

Margaret adjusted her glasses.

“After. The request arrived without a verified collector. I added the instruction to the refusal record because the original request described the transfer as a routine custody action.”

“Did you know who had sent it?”

“No.”

“Did you recognise the handwriting?”

Margaret considered the question.

“I recognised the language. Not necessarily the person.”

Edward waited.

She turned the paper towards herself.

“Someone has been very careful to distinguish between preserving a document and delivering it. They understand that the two things are not interchangeable.”

“That distinction appears repeatedly in the records.”

“Yes. And every time someone tries to remove it, the same problem follows.”

She tapped the blank collector field.

“An instruction without an authorised person. A notification without a verified recipient. A response without proof that the principal received the information.”

Edward looked at her.

“Do you believe the person who submitted this request knew the control copy contained the original procedure?”

“I believe they knew enough to ask for it.”

“Could they have been trying to complete the communication process?”

Margaret's expression tightened.

“Or trying to obtain the document that would show why the process had never been completed.”

For a moment, neither spoke.

Edward gathered the papers.

“I want the original V-17 access register.”

“You won't get much from the ordinary register.”

“Why?”

“Because Personal Custody was not treated as a visitor category. It was a restricted administrative designation. People entering under that designation did not always have to identify themselves in the public access log.”

“Who maintained the restricted register?”

“Arthur Bell.”

Edward stood.

“Then I'll ask him.”

Margaret stopped him before he reached the door.

“Edward.”

He turned.

“If the request was made by someone who already knew the principal's identity, that does not mean they had authority to act for that person.”

“I understand.”

“No,” she said quietly. “I want you to remember it when you find a name.”

Edward nodded.

He left with the photocopy.

And with a question that had become increasingly difficult to ignore.

Who had been trying to obtain a document that explained why the principal's own response was still missing?

Arthur Bell had retained more records than Edward expected.

The former V-17 caretaker had kept his old administrative notebooks in numbered storage boxes, each accompanied by a basic inventory. He had explained previously that he did not trust the official clearance process after the confusion surrounding the west-corridor room.

Now he brought out three additional folders.

“Personal Custody,” he said, placing them on the table. “These weren't part of the ordinary visitor records.”

Jonathan opened the first folder.

It contained entry authorisations, courier acknowledgements and temporary access permits.

The second contained collection requests.

The third was labelled **RESTRICTED HANDOVER — DAY 133**.

Edward looked at it.

“Is this the original?”

Arthur nodded.

“I kept it because the request was refused, but the person who delivered it asked for a written explanation.”

“Who delivered it?”

“A document runner.”

“Name?”

“Not recorded.”

“Was it the same woman with the brown leather case?”

Arthur shook his head.

“No. The runner was a man. Younger than me, certainly. He carried an ordinary document pouch.”

“Did he say who had sent him?”

“He said the request came from Personal Custody.”

“Did he identify the person who authorised it?”

“No.”

Jonathan turned a page.

There was a small entry in the margin.

**Delivery accepted. Authority reference absent. Collection not permitted.**

Below it appeared the same handwritten note found on Margaret's refusal record.

*Do not release until the principal's identity is independently confirmed.*

Edward leaned closer.

“Was this instruction already on the request when it arrived?”

Arthur checked the folder.

“No. It was added after the refusal.”

“By whom?”

Arthur hesitated.

“I don't know.”

“You maintained the restricted records.”

“I maintained the entries. I didn't authorise every instruction placed in them.”

“Could someone have entered the room after the request was refused?”

Arthur opened a separate access sheet.

“Two people had permission to enter the administrative corridor that afternoon. I was one. The other was a records supervisor.”

“Name?”

Arthur turned the sheet around.

**R. HART — TEMPORARY RECORDS SUPERVISOR.**

Edward recognised neither the name nor the designation.

“Was Hart employed by Vale Private Counsel?”

“No. Temporary appointment through a preservation contractor.”

“Do you remember this person?”

“I remember the appointment. Not the individual.”

Jonathan examined the authorisation.

“Was the supervisor allowed to amend collection requests?”

Arthur shook his head.

“Only to record physical custody and access. They could not approve release of protected documents.”

“Could they add a restriction?”

“Not without a separate instruction from the responsible authority.”

Edward pointed to the handwritten note.

“Then somebody added this without an authorisation reference.”

Arthur nodded.

“That is what the record suggests.”

Jonathan turned to the next page.

A small notation had been made beneath the refusal entry.

**Related item: PI-07 / sealed personal instruction.**

Edward's attention sharpened.

“Why was PI-07 included?”

Arthur checked the entry.

“Because the request was not limited to the control copy. The runner also asked whether the sealed instruction could be transferred at the same time.”

“The instruction Eleanor preserved?”

“Yes.”

“Was that request refused too?”

“Immediately. It had a separate access restriction.”

“Who refused it?”

Arthur pointed to the initials.

**A.B.**

“My initials,” he said. “I refused the physical transfer. I had no authority to release the box.”

Edward looked at Jonathan.

The day-133 request was now connected to two separate records.

Margaret's control copy of the original communication procedure.

And Eleanor's sealed personal instruction.

Both were protected.

Both were being sought by an unidentified representative.

Neither request contained a verified collector.

“Did the runner leave any acknowledgement?” Edward asked.

Arthur opened the final page.

A receipt had been signed by the runner, confirming delivery of the request.

The signature was illegible.

But the courier's reference was clear.

**CA-17 / RESTRICTED INTAKE.**

Edward recognised it immediately.

The same code had appeared on the conditional response that might have originated with Susan Bell.

“Cross Administrative Services,” he said.

Arthur nodded.

“That was the delivery channel.”

“Was the runner employed by them?”

“I cannot say. The pouch carried their reference, but there was no company identification.”

Jonathan copied the receipt.

The connection was not proof that the same person had arranged the earlier correspondence.

But it meant the request had entered V-17 through a channel already used for protected communication.

And someone had deliberately avoided identifying the person who would collect the documents.

At Meridian Records and Preservation Services, Dr Mira Sen examined the new material that afternoon.

She placed the day-133 request beneath a transparent document sheet and compared its handwriting with the anonymous instructions from days 48 and 118.

Edward watched her work.

“Same writer?”

“Possibly.”

“More than possibly?”

She moved the papers closer together.

“The capital letters are consistent. So is the unusual way the writer forms the lower loop in the letter *g*. The spacing is also similar.”

“Could it be the same person?”

“Yes. But I would not yet attribute all three documents to one individual.”

She pointed to the final sentence.

“The wording is particularly interesting.”

“Because of the principal's identity?”

“Because the writer distinguishes between two things that other documents repeatedly combine.”

She marked the relevant phrases.

**Principal's identity independently confirmed.**

**Receiving authority independently appointed.**

“These are not the same requirement,” she said. “One concerns identifying the protected principal. The other concerns establishing who is authorised to receive information or act in a defined capacity.”

Edward studied the request.

“Could someone know the principal's identity but still lack authority to collect the procedure?”

“Certainly.”

“And could someone have receiving authority without knowing the principal's identity?”

“That would depend on the scope of the appointment. But the documents we've examined suggest the process required both questions to be handled separately.”

She returned to the handwriting comparison.

“There is another detail.”

“What?”

“The anonymous writer did not simply repeat the language from the day-48 and day-118 instructions. The day-133 note appears to respond to a particular risk.”

“What risk?”

“Someone collecting the control copy and using it without the underlying identity verification.”

Edward looked at the sentence again.

*Do not release until the principal's identity is independently confirmed.*

“Then the writer may have been trying to prevent misuse.”

“Possibly.”

“Even though the request itself was made without a verified collector?”

Dr Sen paused.

“That is precisely why I would not assume the requester and the person who added the restriction were the same.”

Edward looked up.

Two people.

One request.

One refusal.

And a handwritten safeguard added afterwards.

The records might have been created by different people with entirely different intentions.

“Can you compare the handwriting with the original PR-06 procedure?” he asked.

“I can compare it with the surviving control copy, but remember that the control copy may contain annotations made at different times.”

“Do that.”

Dr Sen nodded.

“And Edward?”

“Yes?”

“If the writer is the same person who prepared the earlier restrictions, we may be looking at someone who understood the entire communication procedure better than the administrative offices that were processing it.”

Edward took the copied request.

“That would explain why the instructions keep appearing outside the ordinary system.”

“It might.”

He left the examination room with no name.

But for the first time, the anonymous writer seemed less like someone trying to hide a decision and more like someone repeatedly trying to prevent an invalid one.

That did not make the writer trustworthy.

It made the writer important.

The Aurelius residence was unusually quiet that evening.

Alexander had postponed a scheduled meeting, while Victoria had asked Helena Brooks to attend a private records discussion in the library.

Edward presented the new findings.

He explained the day-133 request, the missing collector, the two restricted items and the connection to Cross Administrative Services.

Adrian listened without interrupting.

Damien studied the access record.

Lucien, unusually, did not make a joke.

When Edward finished, Alexander looked at the copied sentence.

“Do not release until the principal's identity is independently confirmed.”

“Yes.”

“Whoever wrote this knew the principal had not been independently confirmed.”

“Or knew the records did not contain sufficient proof of confirmation,” Edward replied.

Victoria looked at the request.

“Could the principal have been aware of the process?”

“We still have no evidence that the original procedure reached them.”

“Then we cannot assume they knew they were being asked to make a decision.”

“No.”

“And we cannot assume their silence meant agreement.”

Edward met her eyes.

“No.”

Victoria lowered her gaze.

For eighteen years, the family had searched for a missing daughter. Every new document brought them closer to understanding the administrative history surrounding the children.

But the documents had also taught them how little they actually knew.

Alexander turned to Helena.

“Could the control copy be used to identify the principal?”

“Not by itself,” Helena said. “It establishes what the procedure required. It does not establish who was entitled to receive it.”

“Could the original personal instruction contain that information?”

“It might contain the intended process, but the instruction itself was sealed because the receiving authority had not been appointed.”

Adrian leaned back.

“So the same missing appointment prevents us from opening one document and completing the other.”

“Correct,” Helena said.

Damien tapped the access record.

“And somebody tried to collect both.”

“Without identifying the collector,” Edward said.

Alexander's expression hardened.

“Find out who authorised the request.”

“We're following the delivery channel.”

“Do not let the investigation become another administrative exercise where everyone can claim they received something but nobody accepts responsibility for what happened.”

Edward nodded.

Victoria looked at him again.

“There's something else.”

He waited.

“If the anonymous writer was protecting the principal's right to make a personal decision, I don't want that instruction treated as evidence that the principal belongs to any particular family.”

“No one is doing that.”

“Good.”

She folded her hands.

“We have spent too long allowing records to suggest conclusions they cannot support.”

The room fell silent.

Then Alexander closed the folder.

“Continue.”

In Singapore, Elena was having a very different kind of problem.

Her prototype had rejected a grant application that should have passed its initial review.

She sat in front of her laptop, staring at the status panel.

**APPLICATION STATUS: INCOMPLETE**

**REVIEW BASIS: DOCUMENT VALIDATION**

**REVIEW AUTHORITY: NOT ASSIGNED**

Elena frowned.

“That is not what I programmed.”

Clara's face appeared in the corner of the video call.

“What happened?”

“The system has decided that an application cannot move forward because the reviewer field is blank.”

“Isn't that reasonable?”

“Not if the application is supposed to be waiting for reviewer assignment. It's treating the absence of a reviewer as a failed review.”

Clara considered this.

“So it is confusing ‘nobody has reviewed it’ with ‘someone reviewed it and rejected it’?”

“Exactly.”

Elena opened the workflow configuration.

She had built separate fields for application status, reviewer assignment, decision status and audit history. But one transition rule still assumed that every pending application already had an assigned reviewer.

She changed the rule.

**Pending assignment** would remain pending.

**Review in progress** would require an authorised reviewer.

**Decision issued** would require a completed review and a recorded basis.

And a missing decision would remain missing.

Clara watched the updated screen.

“You really hate systems that pretend something happened when it didn't.”

Elena smiled faintly.

“I hate having to guess what happened because a system used the wrong word.”

“That's fair.”

Elena saved the change and ran the test again.

Test passed.

The application remained pending assignment instead of being incorrectly rejected.

She leaned back.

“Much better.”

Clara grinned.

“Congratulations. You have successfully taught a computer not to lie.”

“I taught it to distinguish between different kinds of incomplete information.”

“Less catchy, but probably more useful.”

Elena laughed.

After the call ended, she returned to her project notes.

She added one more principle:

*An unresolved process must remain visibly unresolved. It should never be converted into a completed decision merely to make the system look tidy.*

She read the sentence once, then saved the document.

Outside her window, the city lights were beginning to appear.

There was still a great deal to learn before her project could become something useful beyond the university.

But for the first time, she felt that she was building something with a purpose she could clearly explain.

And that was enough for tonight.

Back at Meridian, Edward received a message from Jonathan shortly before midnight.

It contained a photograph of a newly recovered page from the day-133 custody packet.

The page had been filed separately from the collection request.

It was an internal routing slip.

At the top:

**V-17 — PERSONAL CUSTODY**

**RELATED MATTER: PR-06 / CONTROL COPY**

**RELATED MATTER: PI-07 / SEALED INSTRUCTION**

Below that, someone had written:

*The requested materials are not to be transferred to the proposed collector. A separate identity record must be obtained first.*

Edward read the line again.

The phrase **proposed collector** was new.

It suggested that someone had been considered for the collection before the request arrived.

But the name had been removed.

Jonathan had attached a second photograph showing the reverse side.

Most of the page was blank.

Near the bottom, a faint pressure impression remained from a document that had once rested on top of it.

Dr Sen's preliminary enhancement had recovered three lines.

**PROPOSED COLLECTOR: [REDACTED]**

**AUTHORITY BASIS: AV-18 / PRINCIPAL REPRESENTATION**

**STATUS: WITHDRAWN — DAY 132**

Edward stared at the final line.

The collection request had arrived on day 133.

The proposed collector's authority had been withdrawn one day earlier.

Someone had requested the control copy after the authority supporting the proposed collection had already ceased to exist.

And the anonymous writer had insisted that the principal's identity be independently confirmed before anything was released.

Edward called Jonathan.

“Did you find the withdrawal record?”

“Not yet.”

“Look for every AV-18 designation withdrawn on day 132. Not just collection authority. Any appointment, representation or custody arrangement.”

“Understood.”

Edward ended the call.

Then he looked once more at the page.

The identity of the proposed collector was still hidden.

But the timing was no longer ambiguous.

**The person who had been expected to collect the original procedure had lost their authority before they came to collect it.**
