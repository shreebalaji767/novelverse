# **Chapter 145 — The Initials That Could Not Identify a Person**

Anna Mercer-Smith arrived at Meridian Records and Preservation Services carrying a folder of her own.

She placed it on the consultation table but did not open it.

“I received your message,” she said. “You mentioned a courier reference.”

Edward nodded.

“Route 06. Day 135.”

Anna looked at the reference Jonathan had printed.

Her expression changed slightly.

“I remember that designation.”

“Did you handle the communication?”

“I need to see the original instruction.”

Edward passed it across the table.

Anna examined the paper, paying particular attention to the dispatch conditions.

**The communication must be delivered to the person who requested the original procedure. No substitute recipient is permitted.**

She read the line twice.

Then she looked at the initials.

“A.M.”

“Are those yours?”

“They could be.”

“Could you explain?”

Anna opened her folder.

Inside were several old employment records, correspondence acknowledgements and copies of her authorised administrative assignments.

She selected a small sheet.

“This is my signature from the period. I used my full surname on formal records, but initials on internal delivery acknowledgements.”

Edward placed the two documents beside each other.

The initials looked similar.

Dr Mira Sen, who had been invited to examine the records, studied them without touching the originals.

“Similar formation,” she said. “But these are too short for a reliable attribution.”

Anna nodded.

“I wouldn't certify them either.”

Edward looked at her.

“Did you deliver documents under Route 06?”

“Yes.”

“What kind?”

“Protected correspondence. Usually between Cross Administrative Services, V-17 and Meridian.”

“Did you deliver the day-136 communication?”

Anna looked at the reference.

“I don't remember that specific delivery.”

“Could you have?”

“Yes.”

“Did you know the person who requested the original procedure?”

“No.”

“Did you meet them?”

Anna paused.

“I met someone at C-19 once. I don't know whether it was the same person.”

Edward leaned forward.

“When?”

“Day 43.”

The room became quiet.

Jonathan opened his notebook.

“Was that the person who declined to identify themselves?”

“I believe so.”

“What do you remember?”

Anna considered her answer carefully.

“I had been asked to wait at C-19 because a courier delivery might require a second attempt. While I was there, an adult arrived. I didn't know their name. They asked whether the correspondence explained who was requesting their information.”

“And what did you say?”

“I said I was not authorised to discuss the contents.”

“Did they ask about the original procedure?”

“Not by that name.”

“What did they ask?”

Anna looked at the day-136 instruction again.

“They asked whether the person contacting them had explained what they were expected to do.”

Edward wrote it down.

“What happened next?”

“The courier could not provide an answer. The visitor declined to identify themselves and left.”

“Did you speak to them again?”

“No.”

“Did you see them receive any documents?”

“No.”

Edward waited.

Anna closed her folder.

“There is something else I remember.”

“What?”

“Before I left St Catherine's, I was given an instruction about protected correspondence. It said that if a person asked for the basis of a request, I was not to substitute a verbal explanation for the original document.”

Edward looked at her.

“Who gave you that instruction?”

“I don't know. It was included in a routing note.”

“Do you still have it?”

“I kept an authorised copy.”

She opened her folder again.

The document was dated day 19.

Its reference was **AV-18 / COMMUNICATION HANDLING**.

The instruction was brief:

*Where a protected principal requests the original procedural document, no intermediary shall provide a summary as a substitute. The original must be delivered through a verified channel.*

Below it was another sentence.

*An intermediary's explanation shall not be recorded as receipt by the principal.*

Edward felt the significance immediately.

This was not merely a later attempt to correct a bad administrative practice.

The instruction had existed before the original procedure was prepared.

“Who issued this?”

Anna pointed to the bottom.

“Eleanor Vale's office.”

“Did Eleanor write it?”

“I don't know. It came through her office.”

Edward looked at the date.

Day 19.

The original PR-06 procedure had been prepared on day 33.

Someone had already anticipated that an intermediary might be asked to explain a document that the principal had never received.

And they had prohibited that substitution.

“Why did you keep this?” Jonathan asked.

Anna answered without hesitation.

“Because I was told not to treat a verbal explanation as a completed delivery. I thought it was an important distinction.”

Edward looked at her.

“Did you ever receive an instruction to disregard it?”

“No.”

“Did you ever sign a record stating that the principal had received the original procedure?”

“No.”

“Did you ever certify that P-04 had been personally informed?”

“Absolutely not.”

Anna returned the document to its folder.

“Whatever happened after I left St Catherine's, I did not verify the principal's identity or confirm that they received the procedure.”

Edward nodded.

That answer was clear.

But the initials remained unresolved.

Later that afternoon, Edward and Jonathan visited Eleanor Vale.

She received them in her private office, where several old correspondence boxes remained stacked along one wall.

Edward placed the day-19 instruction before her.

Eleanor read it carefully.

“I recognise this.”

“Did you issue it?”

“I approved its circulation.”

“Who drafted it?”

“I cannot remember with certainty.”

“Was it intended for Anna Mercer?”

“It was distributed to the correspondence staff, including temporary liaisons.”

“Why was it necessary?”

Eleanor removed her glasses.

“Because people were beginning to treat an intermediary's explanation as though it were the principal's acknowledgement.”

“Was that happening with P-04?”

“I cannot say that every instance involved P-04. But the protected correspondence system was being used for several matters, and the same mistake appeared repeatedly.”

She looked at the document.

“Someone would deliver an envelope, explain what it supposedly contained, and then record the communication as completed. But the recipient might never have seen the original document.”

Edward nodded.

“Did you know about the day-43 visit to C-19?”

“I knew a delivery had failed.”

“Did you know the visitor returned or asked for the original procedure?”

“No.”

“Did you know about the day-136 request?”

Eleanor shook her head.

“Not at the time.”

Edward placed the day-136 register beside the day-19 instruction.

“Someone asked for the original procedure. They were told it could not be delivered because PR-06 remained active.”

Eleanor's expression hardened.

“That is precisely what the day-19 instruction was designed to prevent.”

“Can you explain?”

“PR-06 was supposed to protect the communication process. It was not supposed to become a reason to withhold the procedure indefinitely.”

“Who had authority to release it?”

“The receiving authority and the originating representative, acting together.”

“But the receiving authority was never appointed.”

“Correct.”

“Then the procedure could never be delivered.”

Eleanor looked at him.

“An administrative impasse does not authorise someone to invent an alternative.”

Edward studied her.

“Did you ever ask for the procedure to be released?”

“Several times.”

“Were you refused?”

“Yes.”

“Did you know who made the decision?”

“No. The responses came through administrative channels.”

Eleanor opened a drawer and removed a small envelope.

“I kept this because I did not want the refusal to disappear into the general correspondence record.”

The envelope was marked:

**PR-06 / RELEASE REVIEW — DAY 118**

Inside was a short letter.

*The original procedure remains under preservation. No further release action is permitted until the personal representation process has been completed.*

There was no signature.

Only a routing code.

**PC-04 / PERSONAL CUSTODY REVIEW.**

Edward looked at the code.

“Is PC-04 connected to P-04?”

“I don't know. The reference was used by the Personal Custody division.”

“Who managed that division?”

“Not a single person. It was a restricted administrative function.”

“Could Charles Vale have issued the instruction?”

“He could have submitted a request. He could not independently authorise release or permanent withholding.”

Edward placed the letter beside the day-136 record.

“Someone was using Personal Custody to prevent the procedure from being delivered.”

Eleanor nodded slowly.

“And someone else was using it to request the original documents.”

“Could they have been the same person?”

“I cannot tell you.”

She looked at the day-19 instruction again.

“But I can tell you something else. The person who requested the procedure was entitled to understand what they were being asked to acknowledge.”

Edward waited.

Eleanor continued.

“Without that document, they could not know the limits of the representative's authority, the conditions of the proposed arrangement, or the consequences of responding.”

“Then the request for the procedure was reasonable.”

“It was necessary.”

At the Aurelius residence, Victoria received a copy of Eleanor's day-19 instruction.

She read it in the library.

Alexander stood beside the window.

“Does it establish that the principal was contacted?”

“No,” Victoria replied.

“Does it establish that someone attempted to contact them?”

“It establishes that a procedure existed for handling such a request.”

She turned the page.

“Someone had already anticipated that an intermediary might be used in place of direct communication.”

Alexander looked at her.

“Do you think the principal was trying to protect themselves?”

“I don't know.”

Victoria folded the paper.

“But I think we should stop describing every unanswered request as a refusal.”

Alexander nodded.

“We have done that before.”

“Not deliberately.”

“No. But the result was the same.”

Victoria looked at the family records.

The names of two women appeared repeatedly in the investigation.

Her own.

And Isabella Vale's.

Neither had been given a complete account of what had happened to their newborn children.

Neither had received the promised identity reconciliation.

And neither had been able to establish which infant, if any, had ultimately been connected to their maternity record.

Victoria returned her attention to the correspondence.

“Find out whether the day-136 request was ever answered properly.”

Alexander nodded.

“And if it wasn't?”

“Then we need to know whether the person is still waiting.”

That evening, Jonathan called Edward with a new discovery.

The day-136 communication had not been the last request.

There were two additional entries.

Day 149.

Day 181.

Both had the same classification:

**REQUEST FOR ORIGINAL PROCEDURE — PR-06.**

The first had been marked:

**RECEIVED — NO ACTION.**

The second:

**RECEIVED — REFERRED TO PERSONAL CUSTODY.**

Edward sat down.

“Who submitted them?”

“Sender identity restricted.”

“Delivery channel?”

“Both came through C-19.”

“Same courier?”

“The records don't say.”

“Any response?”

“No.”

Edward looked at the day-181 entry.

“Who received the referral?”

Jonathan checked the index.

“Personal Custody review desk.”

“Name?”

“None.”

“Approval?”

“None.”

Edward was silent for a moment.

Then he asked, “What was the date of Eleanor's final objection?”

“Day 181.”

Edward looked at the entry again.

The same day.

Eleanor had objected to the continued withholding of the original procedure.

And a separate request for that procedure had arrived through C-19.

He called Eleanor.

She answered after the second ring.

“Edward?”

“Did you know a request for the original procedure was received on day 181?”

“No.”

“Did you submit your objection that day?”

“Yes.”

“Could your objection have been connected to the request?”

“I don't know. I sent mine through the official records channel.”

Edward looked at Jonathan.

“Were both records received on the same date?”

Jonathan checked.

“Yes. The C-19 request was received at 10:14. Eleanor's objection was registered at 15:40.”

Edward thanked her and ended the call.

The chronology was becoming more important than the documents themselves.

Someone had requested the procedure repeatedly.

Someone had refused to release it.

Eleanor had objected.

And on the same day, another request had entered the system.

Edward opened the day-181 record.

At the bottom was a note that had not appeared in the previous requests.

*If the original procedure cannot be delivered, please confirm whether the principal may withdraw from the proposed arrangement without providing a representative response.*

Edward read it slowly.

This was no longer simply a request for information.

It was a question about the principal's right to decline the arrangement altogether.

And the system had recorded no answer.

The final status was:

**REFERRED — NO AUTHORITY TO RESPOND.**

Edward looked at Jonathan.

“Find the original day-181 communication.”

Jonathan nodded.

“And Edward?”

“Yes?”

“The sender included an attachment.”

“What attachment?”

Jonathan enlarged the scan.

The attachment field contained a single description.

**COPY OF ORIGINAL ACKNOWLEDGEMENT — SIGNATURE NOT VISIBLE.**

Edward stared at the screen.

The sender had apparently retained a copy of the acknowledgement.

But the original remained sealed at Meridian.

And the signature was not visible.

For the first time, the investigation had evidence that the protected principal—or someone with access to their personal correspondence—might possess a copy of a document the archives had never been allowed to examine.

Edward looked at the reference.

**PA-132 / PERSONAL ACKNOWLEDGEMENT.**

Then he looked at the sender field.

**IDENTITY RESTRICTED.**

The next step was clear.

They had to find out where the copy had come from.

Because if it was genuine, someone had managed to obtain part of the principal's original acknowledgement despite every restriction designed to keep it sealed.
