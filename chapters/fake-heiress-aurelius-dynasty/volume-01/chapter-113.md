# **Chapter 113 — The Claim That Arrived Before the Confirmation**

The box had not been opened.

That was the first thing Edward Valmont confirmed when he returned to Meridian Records and Preservation Services the following morning.

The second was that nobody had officially requested permission to open it.

The third was considerably more troubling.

Someone had recorded that the proposed recipient had already been notified.

Edward stood in the preservation room, examining the sealed container through the transparent inspection panel.

PRIVATE INSTRUCTION 07.

The label was old, the edges slightly discoloured, and the original preservation seal remained intact. Beneath the label, the transfer reference had been written in black ink.

The box looked unremarkable.

Its contents, however, had been kept separate from an investigation involving several hospitals, two maternal identities, multiple temporary placements, and a receiving authority that had never been appointed.

Jonathan placed the previous day's photographs on the inspection table.

“Should we begin with the handwriting?”

“No,” Edward replied.

Jonathan looked at him.

“Chain of custody first.”

Edward pointed to the transfer receipt.

“If we cannot establish when the second note appeared, we cannot establish whether it was written before or after the box arrived here. And if we cannot establish that, we cannot assume it was intended as an instruction.”

Jonathan nodded.

The archivist, Mr. D'Souza, approached with a thin folder.

“I retrieved the original custody records,” he said. “There are three documents associated with this box.”

He placed them on the table.

“The first is the initial preservation receipt. The second is the transfer record from Unit Six. The third is an internal condition report prepared when the box arrived here.”

Edward opened the folder.

The initial receipt contained the familiar instruction.

**THE RECEIVING AUTHORITY REMAINS UNAPPOINTED. IF THE PROPOSED RECIPIENT IS NOT VERIFIED, THIS INSTRUCTION MUST REMAIN UNOPENED.**

Eleanor Vale's initials appeared beneath it.

The next line was the one that concerned him.

**THE PROPOSED RECIPIENT HAS ALREADY BEEN NOTIFIED.**

No initials.

No date.

No reference number.

No indication of who had written it.

Edward examined the lower margin.

“Is this part of the original document?”

Mr. D'Souza adjusted his glasses.

“It appears to be. The paper is continuous. There is no indication that a separate sheet was attached.”

“Was it present when the box arrived?”

“The condition report may answer that.”

He opened the third document.

It contained a list of observations made on the day of arrival.

**Container received with original seal intact.**

**External label undamaged.**

**Transfer instruction present.**

**Additional handwritten notation observed beneath the receiving-authority restriction.**

Edward stopped.

“Additional notation observed?”

“Yes.”

“Was its wording recorded?”

Mr. D'Souza turned the page.

The archivist's handwritten description was brief.

**Statement concerning prior notification. Origin and date not established. No action taken pending clarification of authority.**

Edward read it twice.

“So the note was already there when the box arrived.”

“That is what our records indicate.”

“Did anyone ask you to verify the notification?”

“No. We were instructed to preserve the container, not investigate the statement.”

“Who gave the instruction?”

Mr. D'Souza checked the accompanying receipt.

“An individual from Vale Private Counsel. The name on the delivery record is Thomas Greer.”

Edward exchanged a glance with Jonathan.

Thomas Greer had provided them with the copy of Private Instruction 07.

“Did Mr. Greer deliver the box personally?”

“No. The delivery was made by a contracted courier. Mr. Greer signed the transfer authorisation.”

“Was there an accompanying notification receipt?”

The archivist checked the file again.

“No.”

“An acknowledgement from the proposed recipient?”

“No.”

“Any evidence that the recipient had accepted the proposed arrangement?”

“No.”

Edward closed the folder.

“Then the statement is unsupported.”

Mr. D'Souza gave a cautious nod.

“We treated it as an unverified annotation.”

Edward looked once more at the box.

Someone had written that the proposed recipient had already been notified.

But the archive had received no proof.

And the instruction beneath that statement still prohibited opening the box without joint authority.

“Please preserve the original receipt separately,” Edward said. “We will need an independent examination of the ink and handwriting.”

“Certainly.”

“And nobody is to add another notation to the original.”

Mr. D'Souza nodded.

Jonathan waited until they had left the preservation room.

“Do you think the note was written to make the instruction appear ready for opening?”

“Possibly.”

“But the person who wrote it didn't actually open the box.”

“Or couldn't.”

Jonathan considered that.

“The instruction required the receiving authority and originating representative together.”

“Yes.”

“And there was no receiving authority.”

“Exactly.”

Edward walked toward the archive's administrative office.

“The note may have been intended to create the impression that one of the conditions had already been satisfied. That doesn't mean the person who wrote it could satisfy the other condition.”

Jonathan glanced back toward the sealed room.

“So someone wanted the instruction opened, but couldn't legally arrange it.”

“Or someone wanted the record to show that the proposed recipient had been informed, whether or not that was true.”

Neither possibility was reassuring.

The handwriting examination took place that afternoon.

Dr Mira Sen received high-resolution photographs of the original transfer receipt, along with known writing samples from Eleanor Vale, Charles Vale, Marianne Cross, Adrian Holt, Margaret Voss, and Thomas Greer.

Edward made one condition clear.

“Do not identify the writer by process of elimination.”

Dr Sen looked up from the documents.

“I wasn't planning to.”

“I mean it. If the writing doesn't match the available samples, I don't want a speculative name.”

“Understood.”

She examined the enlarged images.

“The two entries were made at different times,” she said after several minutes.

“Can you establish the order?”

“Yes. The first instruction was written before the second notation. There is a difference in ink composition and writing pressure.”

“Can you date the second?”

“Not precisely. Ink examination can sometimes establish relative differences, but it cannot give you a reliable calendar date simply because one ink appears older than another.”

Edward nodded.

“Does it match any of the samples?”

“Not sufficiently for attribution.”

“What about Eleanor Vale?”

“The second writer's letter formation is different from hers.”

“Charles Vale?”

“Not enough to support a match.”

“Marianne Cross?”

“Some superficial similarities, but nothing conclusive.”

Dr Sen moved another photograph across the table.

“There is something else.”

Edward leaned forward.

“The second notation was written with a different pen pressure and a slightly different angle. Whoever added it did not write the original instruction. However, the writer deliberately placed the sentence beneath the restriction rather than beside it.”

“Why does that matter?”

“Because it creates a visual relationship between the two statements.”

She pointed to the first line.

**THE RECEIVING AUTHORITY REMAINS UNAPPOINTED.**

Then to the second.

**THE PROPOSED RECIPIENT HAS ALREADY BEEN NOTIFIED.**

“The second statement appears to answer the practical concern raised by the first,” she said. “But it does not actually resolve it.”

Edward studied the page.

“The first statement concerns authority. The second concerns notification.”

“Correct. They are separate administrative matters.”

“And notification cannot appoint the receiving authority.”

“Not by itself.”

Edward leaned back.

For months, the investigation had repeatedly encountered the same problem.

A receipt had been treated as approval.

A preservation transfer had been presented as an appointment.

A proposed allocation had been described as a completed arrangement.

Now a notification claim appeared beside an instruction that specifically required an independently appointed authority.

The documents were not merely incomplete.

Someone had repeatedly tried to make one administrative step look like another.

The next person Edward contacted was Marianne Cross.

She agreed to meet him at her office, but her expression changed when he placed the photograph of the transfer receipt in front of her.

“I have never seen this document,” she said.

“Have you seen the wording?”

Marianne read the second notation.

“The proposed recipient has already been notified.”

“Yes.”

She put the photograph down.

“That statement is incorrect.”

Edward watched her carefully.

“How can you be certain?”

“Because I was responsible for delivering the notifications.”

“Every notification?”

“Every notification assigned to me under the arrangement. That does not mean I knew the identities of all proposed recipients.”

“Did you ever receive an instruction concerning Private Instruction 07?”

Marianne shook her head.

“No.”

“Did you receive a notification assignment connected to the proposed recipient in that instruction?”

“I received several requests concerning Schedule 4 and the recipient schedule. None gave me a verified receiving authority.”

“Did any of them identify a family?”

Marianne hesitated.

“Some identified an administrative recipient.”

“Which is not the same thing.”

“No. It isn't.”

Edward placed another document before her.

It was the original R-04 acknowledgement register.

He pointed to the day-118 entry.

**RECEIVING REPRESENTATIVE DECLINED ACKNOWLEDGEMENT.**

**ORIGINATING IDENTITY NOT CONFIRMED.**

**RECEIVING AUTHORITY NOT APPOINTED.**

Marianne read it silently.

“That was Susan Bell.”

“Correct.”

“She refused the proposed allocation.”

“She refused to acknowledge responsibility. She also requested confirmation of the originating family.”

Marianne nodded.

“I remember.”

“Was any later notification successfully delivered to Susan?”

“No.”

“Was another recipient appointed after her refusal?”

“No.”

“Then who was notified?”

Marianne did not answer immediately.

Edward continued.

“I'm not asking whether someone received a letter. I'm asking whether the proposed recipient identified in Private Instruction 07 was notified through a valid procedure.”

“I cannot confirm that.”

“Can you identify a record showing it?”

“No.”

“Can you identify an authorised person who certified it?”

“No.”

Marianne looked down at the photograph again.

“Then whoever wrote that statement was claiming something that had not been established.”

“Could it have been a misunderstanding?”

“It could have been.”

“Could someone have mistaken an acknowledgement of receipt for notification?”

“Certainly. That happened more than once.”

Edward waited.

Marianne finally added, “But there is a difference between receiving a document and being told what it means.”

Edward looked at her.

“Explain.”

“Suppose someone sends a proposed allocation to an administrative office. The office signs for the envelope. That proves the envelope arrived. It does not prove that the person inside the envelope was informed, that the person receiving it had authority to represent the family, or that the proposed allocation was accepted.”

“And yet the records could be made to look as if all three had happened.”

“Yes.”

Edward gathered the documents.

“Did Charles Vale ever ask you to certify that a family had been notified?”

Marianne's face tightened.

“He asked me to certify that a notification had been processed.”

“Was that the same thing?”

“No.”

“Did you tell him?”

“I did.”

“What did he say?”

Marianne looked toward the window.

“He said the families could not remain in uncertainty indefinitely.”

“That isn't an answer.”

“No,” she said quietly. “It wasn't.”

She turned back.

“I asked him which family he wanted informed. He said the receiving arrangement had already been established administratively.”

“But the receiving authority had not been appointed.”

“I knew that.”

“Did he?”

“I believe he did.”

Edward studied her expression.

“Why do you say believe?”

“Because he never directly told me the authority had been appointed. He spoke as though the remaining problem was paperwork.”

“And you disagreed.”

“I told him that paperwork was the problem.”

For a moment, neither spoke.

Then Marianne opened a drawer and removed a thin envelope.

“I kept something else.”

Edward looked at it.

“What is it?”

“A copy of a dispatch instruction. It was sent to R-04 after the day-118 refusal.”

“Why wasn't it included in the register?”

“Because it was never accepted as a valid notification assignment.”

She handed it over.

The instruction was dated day 120.

Its subject line read:

**REPEAT NOTIFICATION — PROPOSED RECEIVING ARRANGEMENT**

The recipient field had been partially obscured.

The instruction requested that Marianne arrange a second delivery of the proposed allocation.

But below the request, someone had written:

**DELIVERY MAY PROCEED WITHOUT FINAL RECEIVING AUTHORITY. ACKNOWLEDGEMENT TO BE OBTAINED FOR ADMINISTRATIVE CLOSURE.**

Marianne's initials appeared beside a short refusal.

**NOT AUTHORISED. ORIGINAL RECEIVING REPRESENTATIVE HAS NOT ACCEPTED. ORIGINATING FAMILY REMAINS UNCONFIRMED.**

Edward looked at her.

“You refused.”

“Yes.”

“Did you send the refusal back?”

“Through the restricted correspondence channel.”

“Do you have the receipt?”

Marianne searched the envelope.

“I have the dispatch reference.”

She wrote it on a separate sheet.

**R-04/NT-120/06.**

Edward copied it into his notebook.

“Who sent the original instruction?”

Marianne turned the page over.

The sender's field was blank.

There was only a routing code.

**VPC-PI/07.**

Edward's eyes narrowed.

“Private Instruction 07.”

Marianne nodded.

“The same reference.”

“Was the instruction signed?”

“No.”

“Was there an authorised sender code?”

“No.”

“Then it was not a valid instruction.”

“That was my conclusion.”

Edward carefully returned the paper to its envelope.

The new document did not prove who had written the claim on the preservation receipt.

But it established something important.

Two months after the receiving arrangement had failed, someone had attempted to send another notification without appointing a receiving authority.

Marianne had refused.

And the attempt had been connected to the same private instruction that remained sealed in Meridian's custody.

That evening, Edward and Jonathan returned to the Valmont residence.

Richard was in his study when they arrived. Sebastian had joined him, and Victoria Aurelius was present through a secure video conference.

Edward placed the dispatch instruction on the table.

“I found a second notification attempt.”

Victoria leaned closer to the screen.

“Was it successful?”

“No.”

“Who refused it?”

“Marianne Cross.”

Alexander, seated beside Victoria, spoke for the first time.

“Was the refusal recorded?”

“Yes. She retained the original copy and the dispatch reference.”

Edward explained the day-120 request.

He described the instruction to proceed without a final receiving authority and the request to obtain an acknowledgement for administrative closure.

Victoria listened without interrupting.

When he finished, she asked, “Did the instruction identify the proposed recipient?”

“Not in the surviving copy.”

“Then we still don't know whether it concerned an Aurelius placement.”

“No.”

Alexander's expression remained controlled.

“Does the reference Private Instruction 07 establish that the proposed recipient was connected to the Aurelius family?”

“No. It establishes only that the dispatch was linked to the same restricted instruction.”

“Then we must not treat it as evidence of our daughter's placement.”

Edward nodded.

“I agree.”

Victoria looked down at the document on her desk.

For eighteen years, she had lived with the knowledge that a daughter had been born to her and disappeared from every reliable record.

Every new document offered a possibility.

Every possibility had to be examined.

She had learned not to turn possibilities into memories.

“Who was supposed to receive the instruction?” she asked.

“That remains unclear.”

“Could it have been the family that refused Child 28-B?”

“Possibly. But the dispatch does not identify Susan Bell, and we cannot assume the proposed recipient in Private Instruction 07 was the same person named in Schedule 4.”

Alexander nodded slowly.

“Then the next step is to identify the recipient field from the original dispatch record.”

Edward agreed.

“I have requested the full routing history.”

Richard looked at him.

“And Charles Vale?”

“We will ask him directly about the instruction.”

Sebastian frowned.

“Will he answer?”

“He may.”

“And if he doesn't?”

“Then we examine the records that passed through his office.”

Richard leaned back.

“Be careful. We have already seen genuine signatures attached to documents that were not what they appeared to be. A name on a routing record does not establish that the person authorised its contents.”

Edward glanced at his father.

“I know.”

Richard's voice softened slightly.

“Then keep the investigation narrow. Find out who sent the instruction, who was supposed to receive it, and why the authority was missing.”

Edward gathered the papers.

“That is exactly what I'm trying to establish.”

Across the city, Elena was having a different kind of difficult day.

Her bedroom floor was covered with an assortment of objects.

Two suitcases stood open beside the wardrobe. A smaller bag contained documents, chargers, medicines, and the items she had decided not to trust to checked luggage.

On her desk lay three folders.

One contained her Singapore programme documents.

Another held her academic project proposal.

The third contained a budget she had revised four times.

Elena stared at the figures.

Then she stared at the suitcase.

Then back at the figures.

“I am beginning to suspect that travelling internationally is a financial punishment,” she muttered.

Nicholas, sitting on the window seat, looked up from his phone.

“You're going to an international academic programme. It isn't supposed to be free.”

“Tuition and accommodation are covered.”

“Exactly.”

“Flights aren't.”

“Correct.”

“Daily expenses aren't.”

“Also correct.”

“And apparently every document requires another document.”

Nicholas smiled.

“Welcome to adulthood.”

Elena gave him a flat look.

“I would like to return it.”

“Unfortunately, the receipt expired.”

She threw a folded pair of socks at him.

Nicholas caught them.

“Excellent. Your packing skills are improving.”

“They were never bad.”

“Then why have you packed six pairs of socks for a trip that requires four?”

“Because I don't trust laundry facilities.”

“That is a reasonable answer.”

Elena returned to her budget.

She had calculated her estimated monthly expenses, including transport, meals, academic supplies, communication, and a small emergency reserve.

She had also created a separate section for expenses she could reduce if necessary.

Nicholas leaned forward.

“Why are you planning to spend less than the amount you have available?”

“Because having a budget doesn't mean spending all of it.”

“You're allowed to enjoy yourself.”

“I know.”

“Buy something occasionally.”

“I know.”

“Eat somewhere nice.”

“I know.”

Nicholas narrowed his eyes.

“You keep saying that.”

“Because you're repeating yourself.”

He laughed.

Then his expression became more thoughtful.

“Are you nervous?”

Elena paused.

A moment later, she closed the budget folder.

“A little.”

“About the programme?”

“About everything.”

She looked at the suitcases.

“I've never lived away from home. I know the accommodation is arranged, and I know the programme has an orientation team. But I'll be responsible for everything else.”

Nicholas nodded.

“You'll manage.”

“I hope so.”

“You've managed much worse.”

Elena glanced at him.

“That is not particularly comforting.”

“It's true.”

He got up and approached the desk.

“What is this?”

“My project proposal.”

He picked up the folder.

The first page contained the working title:

**A Transparent Digital Grant Management Platform for Small Educational and Community Organisations.**

Nicholas read the summary.

“You're still working on this?”

“Yes.”

“You've changed the scope.”

“Several times.”

“Why?”

“The original version was too broad. I was trying to solve every problem at once.”

“And now?”

“Now I want to focus on the application process, eligibility checks, document tracking, and reporting. If it works for small organisations, it might be useful for larger institutions later.”

Nicholas turned another page.

“You've included audit trails.”

“That's important. Organisations need to know who changed what and when.”

“Approval histories?”

“Yes.”

“Role-based permissions?”

“Yes.”

He looked at her.

“You've thought about this quite seriously.”

Elena shrugged.

“I don't want to arrive there with nothing except a presentation.”

“You don't need to prove yourself every minute.”

“I know.”

She took the folder back.

“But I want to do something useful.”

Nicholas studied her for a moment.

Then he nodded.

“Then do it.”

There was no lecture.

No promise to finance the project.

No suggestion that she should use the Valmont name to secure introductions.

Elena appreciated that more than she said.

Her phone rang.

The screen showed Clara's name.

Elena answered the video call.

Clara appeared with a notebook in one hand and a cup of tea in the other.

“Finally!”

“I spoke to you yesterday.”

“That was yesterday. Today is today.”

Elena smiled.

“Very philosophical.”

“Thank you. I've been practising.”

Clara noticed the suitcases.

“Are you packing already?”

“Trying to.”

“Trying?”

“I've reached the stage where I keep moving the same objects between bags.”

Clara laughed.

“That's progress. At least they're all in one place.”

Nicholas leaned into the camera.

“Tell her to stop packing unnecessary things.”

Clara looked at him.

“Who are you?”

“Elena's second brother.”

“Then you should know better than to interfere with a packing process.”

Nicholas looked offended.

Elena grinned.

“Thank you, Clara.”

Clara returned her attention to Elena.

“Have you checked your arrival instructions?”

“Twice.”

“Accommodation confirmation?”

“Three times.”

“Emergency contacts?”

“Twice.”

“Passport?”

Elena picked it up from the desk and held it toward the camera.

Clara nodded solemnly.

“Good. You may now leave the country.”

“I'm glad I have your permission.”

They spoke for several minutes about the programme, the first-week schedule, and the possibility of visiting the campus library together once Clara arrived.

Then Clara noticed the project folder.

“Is that the grant platform?”

“Yes.”

“Are you bringing it?”

“Of course.”

“Good. I want to see the revised version when you arrive.”

Elena smiled.

“I'll send it to you before then.”

“Don't send me a forty-page document at midnight.”

“I make no promises.”

The call ended a little later.

Elena placed the phone down.

Nicholas was watching her.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

“That expression means something.”

He shook his head.

“You look excited.”

Elena looked toward the suitcases.

“I am.”

For the first time in a long while, the future seemed to contain something she had chosen entirely for herself.

It was not a family decision.

It was not a consequence of a DNA report.

It was not an attempt to prove she deserved a place in someone else's home.

It was simply her next step.

And she intended to take it.

Later that night, Edward received a message from Jonathan.

The archive had located the original dispatch routing sheet associated with reference R-04/NT-120/06.

It had not been stored with Marianne Cross's correspondence.

It had been retained in a separate administrative collection.

Edward opened the scanned image.

The sender field was still blank.

The recipient field was restricted.

But the routing table contained three entries.

**ORIGINATING REPRESENTATIVE — NOTIFIED: NO**

**MEDICAL RECORDS AUTHORITY — NOTIFIED: NO**

**PROPOSED RECEIVING REPRESENTATIVE — DELIVERY ATTEMPT: PENDING**

Below them was a fourth line.

**JOINT INSTRUCTION CUSTODY — ACCESS CONDITION: UNFULFILLED**

Edward read the final notation.

It was faint, but legible.

**IF RECIPIENT ACKNOWLEDGES, RELEASE PRIVATE INSTRUCTION FOR REVIEW.**

He stopped.

The dispatch had not been intended merely to inform the proposed recipient.

It had been designed to obtain an acknowledgement that could trigger the next stage of the process.

The sealed instruction was supposed to be released for review after the proposed recipient acknowledged the notification.

But the joint authority condition had remained unfulfilled.

Edward enlarged the lower-right corner.

There was a handwritten reference beside the instruction.

**RC-18/FINAL — S. BELL**

Susan Bell.

The same woman who had refused the proposed allocation.

Edward sat back.

Susan had not accepted responsibility for Child 28-B.

She had not confirmed the originating family.

She had not accepted the allocation.

And someone had nevertheless attempted to obtain an acknowledgement from her.

The next line was even more troubling.

**ALTERNATE RECIPIENT TO BE CONTACTED IF INITIAL REPRESENTATIVE DECLINES.**

No name followed.

Only a reference.

**AV-18.**

Edward stared at it.

The same reference appeared on the sealed envelope held for the receiving identity custodian.

The same reference had been found in the correspondence surrounding Private Instruction 07.

He called Jonathan.

“Did you see the alternate recipient reference?”

“Yes.”

“Do we have the associated schedule?”

“Not yet. The archive says the schedule was filed separately.”

“Where?”

Jonathan paused.

“That's the problem.”

“What?”

“It wasn't sent to R-04.”

Edward looked at the document.

“Where was it sent?”

“Vale Private Counsel. Personal correspondence division.”

Edward's expression hardened.

“Under whose name?”

Jonathan read from the record.

“Eleanor Vale.”

Edward looked at the sealed instruction reference again.

Someone had attempted to notify Susan Bell.

When she refused, an alternate recipient had been identified.

But the alternate's identity had been kept outside the ordinary recipient register.

And the schedule that could reveal that identity had been sent to Eleanor Vale's personal correspondence division.

Edward ended the call and looked at the time.

The archive would not open until morning.

For the first time since the investigation began, the question was no longer simply who had been proposed to receive the child.

It was why the original recipient had been allowed to refuse—

and who had been waiting to take that person's place.
