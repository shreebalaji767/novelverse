# **Chapter 188 — The Adviser Who Was Not Asked to Decide**

Jonathan Mercer had been appointed for thirty days.

Edward discovered that before he discovered why.

The appointment instrument was dated day 157 and expired on day 187. Its stated purpose was to provide temporary advisory support during the programme's administrative closure.

The scope was narrow.

Jonathan could review correspondence, identify unresolved dependencies, prepare status reports, and recommend administrative action.

He could not approve restricted access, appoint control officers, determine receiving identities, or issue instructions on behalf of the Programme Authority.

The final page contained Charles Vale's recommendation.

It was brief.

*Mr Mercer has prior experience in complex programme closures and may assist in consolidating outstanding correspondence.*

There was no explanation of why Charles had selected him.

There was no reference to the vacant control liaison position.

There was no mention of the temporary credentials that remained active.

Edward placed the appointment beside the day-160 closure query.

“His appointment began on the same day the expired credential was used,” Sophie said.

“Three days earlier, actually.”

“But the first recorded access was day 157.”

“Yes.”

“Could he have been appointed because someone anticipated the access problem?”

“Possibly.”

“Or could the timing be coincidental?”

“Also possible.”

Edward closed the appointment instrument.

“We need his actual work, not the date.”

Jonathan agreed to an interview the following morning.

He arrived at 9:40, carrying a thin folder and a printed copy of his appointment.

He was in his early forties, neatly dressed, and visibly irritated by the fact that his appointment was being examined.

“I was an adviser,” he said after the introductions. “Not an operational officer.”

Edward nodded.

“We understand the limits of your appointment.”

“Then I hope we can avoid spending an hour discussing permissions I never possessed.”

“That depends on your answers.”

Jonathan glanced at the documents.

Edward began with the day-160 closure query.

“Did you prepare this request?”

“No.”

“Did you instruct anyone to prepare it?”

“No.”

“Did you see the handwritten note attached to it?”

“Yes.”

“Who wrote it?”

“I don't know.”

“Who gave it to you?”

“It was in the closure working file.”

“When did you first see it?”

“Day 160.”

Edward made a note.

“Did you consider the wording important?”

“I considered it a statement of the administrative difficulty.”

“Which difficulty?”

“That the control liaison position remained vacant, no successor had been nominated, and the programme was approaching closure.”

“Did you believe the shared desk could replace the control liaison?”

“No.”

“Did you believe routine administrative work could continue?”

“Yes.”

“Did you believe restricted control functions could continue?”

Jonathan paused.

“No.”

Edward placed Marianne Vale's day-160 clarification on the table.

“She said the same thing.”

“I remember.”

“Did you read it?”

“Yes.”

“Did you agree with it?”

“Of course.”

“Then why did the closure task continue without a successor?”

“Because the task was about administrative closure, not appointment.”

Edward looked at him.

“The task specifically asked whether temporary access arrangements could be treated as resolved.”

“I did not create that task.”

“Did you review it?”

“I reviewed the status.”

“Did you recommend completion?”

“I recommended that the routine closure process continue, provided restricted matters remained outside the shared desk's authority.”

Edward glanced at Sophie.

That qualification was new.

“Where did you record it?”

“In my review notes.”

“May we see them?”

Jonathan opened his folder.

He produced a two-page report dated day 161.

The first page contained a summary of outstanding closure matters.

The second page contained a recommendation.

**Routine correspondence reconciliation may continue within existing administrative permissions.**

**Unresolved control functions must remain excluded from the closure desk's operational scope.**

**Temporary credentials must be handled according to their individual appointment and revocation requirements.**

Edward read the last line twice.

“Did you send this to Peter Langford?”

“Yes.”

“When?”

“Day 161.”

“Did he acknowledge it?”

“Not directly.”

“Did you receive any response?”

“No.”

“Did you follow up?”

Jonathan looked uncomfortable.

“I discussed the report with him.”

“When?”

“Day 162.”

“What did he say?”

“That the closure team was aware of the distinction.”

Edward placed Peter's continuity statement beside Jonathan's report.

Peter's document had been approved on the same day.

It did not mention the exclusion of restricted matters.

“Did you see this?”

Jonathan examined it.

“Yes.”

“Did you object?”

“I wasn't asked to approve it.”

“That wasn't my question.”

Jonathan's expression tightened.

“I told Peter that the wording should not be used to imply that the control function had been restored.”

“Where?”

“In a conversation.”

“Was that conversation recorded?”

“No.”

“Did you send a written correction?”

“No.”

Edward made another note.

Jonathan had recognised the problem.

He had documented the distinction.

But he had not ensured that his qualification appeared in the final closure record.

That was not proof of deliberate wrongdoing.

It was, however, a failure worth examining.

At 11:15, Edward asked about Charles Vale.

“Why did Charles recommend you?”

“He knew my previous work.”

“What work?”

“Administrative closure reviews, records reconciliation, transition planning.”

“Had you worked with him before?”

“Yes.”

“On this programme?”

“No.”

“Did he explain the unresolved control appointment when he recommended you?”

“He told me there were outstanding governance issues.”

“Did he identify the vacant control liaison position?”

“He mentioned that some responsibilities had not transferred cleanly.”

“Did he tell you the shared desk was performing restricted functions?”

“No.”

“Did you discover that yourself?”

“I saw the operational chart.”

“When?”

“Day 159.”

Edward placed the chart before him.

It described the shared desk as maintaining administrative continuity until the programme's closure.

Jonathan pointed to the wording.

“This is why I prepared the day-161 qualification.”

“Did you ask who had approved the chart?”

“No.”

“Why not?”

“I assumed it was an internal administrative arrangement.”

“But your appointment specifically required you to identify unresolved dependencies.”

Jonathan looked at him.

“Yes.”

“Was the absence of a successor an unresolved dependency?”

“Yes.”

“Did you report it?”

“I included it.”

“Did you recommend that the Programme Authority formally suspend the control function?”

“No.”

“Why not?”

“Because I was not authorised to recommend a substantive governance decision.”

Edward paused.

“You were authorised to recommend administrative action.”

“Yes.”

“Then why didn't you recommend that the responsible authority make the decision?”

Jonathan looked at his folder.

“I should have.”

It was the first direct admission of the interview.

Edward wrote it down.

At 12:30, Sophie received the appointment recommendation file.

It contained Charles Vale's original request to the Programme Authority.

The request was dated day 155.

It had been submitted two days before Jonathan's appointment began.

The subject line read:

**Temporary Closure Advisory Support — Immediate Appointment**

Charles had written that the programme required an adviser to consolidate correspondence and prevent unresolved administrative matters from delaying closure.

There was an attachment.

A list of five outstanding issues.

Edward read them in order.

1. Incomplete correspondence reconciliation.
2. Outstanding temporary credential revocations.
3. Vacant control liaison position.
4. Unresolved restricted appointment records.
5. Pending closure confirmation.

He looked at Sophie.

“Charles knew all five were outstanding.”

“Yes.”

“Did he recommend a separate person for each issue?”

“No. Jonathan was proposed as a single adviser.”

Edward turned the page.

Charles's recommendation contained an additional sentence.

*The adviser should coordinate with the shared Administrative Review Desk to maintain continuity and avoid unnecessary duplication of work.*

Edward looked at the date.

Day 155.

The same day Helen Sloane had warned Daniel Mercer that the shared desk could not replace the vacant control liaison.

The same day Peter received the clarification requiring separate written authorisation for continued access.

And the same day the Governance Secretariat confirmed that the outstanding correspondence schedule did not grant authority for continued access.

Charles's recommendation had been prepared after those warnings.

Edward read the sentence again.

*Coordinate with the shared Administrative Review Desk.*

It did not explicitly authorise restricted work.

But it connected the new adviser to the same desk whose authority had been questioned.

“Did Charles discuss this recommendation with you?” Edward asked Jonathan.

“He told me the desk would be my administrative contact.”

“Did he tell you the desk had a vacant control function?”

“No.”

“Did he tell you its temporary credentials were under review?”

“No.”

“Did you ask?”

“I wasn't aware of the issue at the time.”

Edward placed the document aside.

That was consistent with Jonathan's account.

It did not make Charles's recommendation harmless.

Across the estate, Richard Valmont was reading a different report.

Catherine had left it on his desk after a foundation meeting.

It concerned Isabelle's proposed scholarship administration model.

Isabelle had introduced a new requirement: every application marked administratively complete had to retain a visible record of any unresolved eligibility or decision authority.

Richard read the proposed procedure.

“You've added another review stage,” he said.

Catherine stood by the window.

“Yes.”

“Will that slow the process?”

“Sometimes.”

“Is that necessary?”

“According to Isabelle, it is.”

Richard closed the file.

“Has she explained why?”

“She has.”

“And you agree?”

“I do.”

Richard glanced at the document again.

A small note had been added in the margin.

*No pending decision should disappear merely because the administrative task has been closed.*

Richard was silent.

“Isabelle wrote that?”

“Yes.”

He looked at Catherine.

“She has become very particular about procedures.”

“She has become particular about consequences.”

Richard returned to the report.

“Is Elena involved?”

“Not in drafting it. She reviewed one of the test workflows.”

“Did Isabelle ask her?”

“She requested technical feedback.”

Richard nodded.

The two girls were not suddenly close.

But they were beginning to work around the same problem without turning every exchange into a contest.

For Catherine, that was progress.

For Richard, it was something he was still learning not to interfere with.

At 3:20, Jonathan's interview resumed.

Edward placed the five-item list before him.

“Which of these issues did you personally handle?”

“Correspondence reconciliation and the closure report.”

“What about temporary credential revocations?”

“I monitored the status.”

“Did you instruct anyone to revoke the credentials?”

“No.”

“Did you instruct anyone to retain them?”

“No.”

“Did you know Marianne Cross's credential remained active?”

“I learned about the discrepancy during the closure review.”

“When?”

“Day 160.”

“Before or after the restricted correspondence access notification?”

Jonathan hesitated.

“After.”

“Did you know Lydia Shaw's credential also remained active?”

“Yes.”

“Did you report that?”

“I included both in the outstanding credential list.”

“Where is that list?”

“In the day-161 report.”

Edward checked it.

The report contained two entries.

**MC-EXT-02 — Appointment expired. Revocation status unresolved.**

**TEMP-MON-02 — Appointment expired. Revocation status unresolved.**

Neither had a named operator.

Neither had an authorisation to remain active.

Jonathan had recorded both.

Edward turned the page.

A handwritten note appeared beneath the list.

*Pending confirmation from Programme Administration.*

“Who wrote this?”

“I did.”

“Why was confirmation required?”

“Because the system showed the credentials as active, but I had no authority to disable them.”

“Did you ask Peter to confirm their status?”

“Yes.”

“Did he respond?”

“Not in writing.”

“What did he say?”

“He said the closure team was dealing with the outstanding access matters.”

“Did you ask whether the credentials had been used?”

“No.”

“Why not?”

Jonathan looked at Edward.

“I did not have access to the underlying correspondence logs.”

“You could have requested them.”

“I could have requested them. I could not authorise access.”

“Did you request them?”

“No.”

Edward closed the report.

“Then you recorded the problem without establishing what had happened.”

“Yes.”

“And you allowed the matter to remain unresolved.”

Jonathan did not answer.

At 5:10, Edward received a new message from the records office.

It concerned the handwritten note found in Closure Working File 12.

The original had been located.

It was not written on ordinary office paper.

It had been torn from a printed briefing sheet.

The reverse side contained a partial heading.

**NORTHBRIDGE CONTINUITY TRUST — ADMINISTRATIVE EXPENDITURE REVIEW**

Edward stared at it.

Northbridge.

The trust established by Dr Samuel Hale for temporary care, medical, and administrative expenses.

The same trust whose expenditure records had been used to create misleading summaries of child-related payments.

He requested a scan of the complete sheet.

It arrived minutes later.

The front contained the handwritten note:

*Please confirm whether the existing desk arrangement is sufficient for the remaining period. The vacancy has become difficult to resolve, and no further nomination is expected before closure.*

The reverse contained a printed table.

It listed three administrative expenditure categories.

- Temporary records preservation.
- Correspondence and verification support.
- Programme closure administration.

At the bottom was a reference.

**NB-17/04 — Consolidated Expenditure Ceiling**

Edward recognised it.

The original Northbridge financial instruction had been signed by Alexander Aurelius.

It authorised a consolidated expenditure ceiling for service providers.

It did not approve individual child transfers.

It did not appoint receiving representatives.

It did not establish identity.

But the sheet now connected the closure working file to the Northbridge expenditure process.

Edward called Sophie.

“Find out how this paper entered Programme Administration.”

She examined the scan.

“The reverse table has a document reference.”

“Find the source file.”

“Northbridge expenditure review?”

“Yes. And the distribution list.”

Sophie began searching.

Edward looked at the handwritten sentence.

Someone had written that no further nomination was expected.

Someone had placed the note in a folder containing a Northbridge expenditure sheet.

And someone had asked whether the shared desk arrangement was sufficient despite the vacant control function.

The adviser had not made the decision.

The coordinator had not made the appointment.

The supervising office had not nominated a successor.

The Programme Authority had not suspended the function.

Yet the closure process had continued.

At 5:47, Sophie's search returned a result.

The expenditure sheet had been distributed to four offices on day 143.

One was Northbridge Continuity Trust.

One was Vale Private Counsel.

One was Programme Administration.

The fourth was a restricted liaison office.

**Recipient: Office of the Protected Principal's Communication Representative.**

Edward read the recipient line again.

The document had not been sent to P-04 directly.

It had been sent to an office associated with the limited communication authority held by Gabriel Vale.

That did not establish that Gabriel had seen it.

It did not establish that P-04 had received it.

But it meant the Northbridge expenditure sheet had travelled through a channel connected to the protected principal's correspondence.

Edward opened the distribution record.

The delivery field was blank.

The receipt field was blank.

The document had been marked:

**Dispatched — confirmation pending.**

He looked at the handwritten note once more.

The mystery had begun with an unresolved appointment.

Now it was touching the same financial and correspondence channels that had surrounded the protected principal.

Edward placed the documents in separate folders.

He would not merge them simply because they shared a reference.

But he could no longer treat the Northbridge paper as an accidental piece of stationery.

Someone had carried it into the closure process.

And the next question was no longer only who had written the note.

It was why a document intended for a consolidated expenditure review had been used to record a problem with the missing control authority.
