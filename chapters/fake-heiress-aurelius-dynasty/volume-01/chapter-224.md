# **Chapter 224 — The Request That Could Not Name Its Subject**

The Day-53 request remained sealed for another twenty-four hours.

Edward had asked the Restricted Personal Records Authority to disclose the appointment's issuing authority, the reason for deferral, and the identity of the proposed examiner.

The response arrived with several restrictions.

The proposed examiner's identity could be disclosed to the investigation team after an independence review.

The originating family field would remain sealed.

The protected subject's identity would remain sealed.

The original consent procedure could be examined, but no contact with a potential subject was authorised.

Edward read the response twice.

“They're willing to show us who was proposed to conduct the review,” Eleanor said.

“Yes.”

“But not who the review concerned.”

“Not until the authority for disclosure is established.”

“Could that person be Elena?”

“We have no basis to say that.”

Eleanor nodded.

The distinction had become central to their work.

A document could concern a protected individual without identifying that individual.

A proposed examiner could have been selected for a particular process without knowing the subject's identity.

And a family could request a review without having authority to choose the person who would undergo it.

Edward wrote a new request.

**Disclose the proposed examiner's appointment history and independence assessment without releasing protected subject identifiers.**

The response arrived that afternoon.

The proposed examiner was Dr. Naomi Bennett, an independent forensic genetics specialist.

She had not been appointed.

Her proposed role was limited to overseeing a future biological comparison after the relevant individuals had been independently identified and had given informed consent.

Her appointment had been deferred because the originating family confirmation was incomplete.

The record contained no DNA result.

No biological samples had been collected under the proposed procedure.

No comparison had been performed.

Dr. Bennett had never received the identity of the protected subject.

Edward placed the appointment request on the table.

“So the process never began.”

“Correct,” Eleanor said.

“Why was it deferred?”

“Because the family authority could request the process, but could not identify the subject through an administrative reference.”

Edward examined the original application.

The request had been submitted by a recognised family authority.

Its scope was limited to requesting preservation and proposing an independent review.

It did not permit the family to select a protected individual, compel testing, or decide the outcome.

The final section contained a handwritten note:

**Any proposed biological comparison must be voluntary, independently authorised, and based on verified identification of the participants.**

Edward looked at Eleanor.

“This is the first proper foundation for the next stage.”

“Only a foundation.”

“Yes.”

At St Catherine's, Dr. Hale reviewed the proposed biological comparison procedure.

He confirmed that the historical source records could be used as supporting evidence only if their custody and limitations were disclosed.

The procedure required a separate source review.

It also required the proposed living participants to be identified independently.

“Could the old maternity register be used to establish a likely maternal candidate?” Edward asked.

“It could establish what the hospital recorded about a delivery. It cannot establish who the living child became.”

“What if the timing and other records align?”

“That may support a hypothesis. It does not replace biological comparison.”

Eleanor asked, “Would a DNA result alone be enough?”

“For biological relationship, a properly conducted and interpreted comparison can provide strong evidence. But the result must be tied to verified samples from correctly identified participants. A laboratory cannot correct an identity error that occurred before collection.”

Edward wrote that down.

The chain of identification had to be as reliable as the laboratory analysis.

A correct scientific result from incorrectly labelled samples would still be useless.

Dr. Hale continued.

“The participants must know what is being tested, why it is being tested, who will receive the results, and what will happen if the findings are unexpected. Consent cannot be treated as a signature collected to complete an administrative field.”

Edward closed his notebook.

That sentence would matter later.

The Valmont Foundation meeting began on Tuesday at 10:00.

Elena arrived with her laptop and a printed process map.

Isabelle met her outside the conference room.

“The director has asked everyone to bring their current procedures.”

“Everyone?”

“Finance, programme administration, and two trustees.”

Elena looked at the room.

“That is promising.”

The meeting began with the foundation director, Mrs. Caroline Webb, explaining the programme's objectives.

The foundation funded educational grants for students from several regions.

Its existing system relied on email submissions, shared spreadsheets, and separate payment records.

Elena listened.

Then she asked to see an example application from receipt to final payment.

The administrator opened a sample file.

The application had been received.

The supporting documents had been uploaded.

A reviewer had entered an assessment.

A trustee had approved the award.

The finance office had initiated payment.

But there was no clear record showing who had authorised an exception to the eligibility rules.

There was also no independent confirmation that the recipient had received the payment.

Elena pointed to the relevant fields.

“This says payment initiated.”

“Yes,” the finance officer replied.

“Does it say the recipient received the money?”

“It should have reached their account.”

“That's not quite the same thing.”

The finance officer frowned.

Elena continued carefully.

“I am not saying the payment failed. I'm saying the record should distinguish between an instruction being sent, the bank processing it, and the recipient confirming receipt.”

The foundation director nodded.

“That is fair.”

Elena moved to the approval field.

“Who has authority to approve an exception?”

The trustee answered, “The committee.”

The programme director said, “The director can approve certain exceptions.”

The finance officer added, “We usually receive the decision after it has been made.”

Elena stopped.

“Then we need to identify the actual authority before building that permission into the system.”

Isabelle watched the trustees exchange glances.

No one appeared offended.

They appeared uncomfortable.

That was different.

By the end of the meeting, the foundation had agreed to a limited process review.

Elena would not be responsible for administering grants.

She would not approve awards.

She would not decide eligibility.

Her role would be to document the existing workflow, identify conflicting responsibilities, and recommend how the platform could record the process without replacing institutional authority.

The agreement would be written.

The trustees also agreed to appoint one internal process owner before implementation discussions continued.

Elena closed her laptop.

Mrs. Webb smiled.

“You've given us more questions than answers.”

Elena returned the smile.

“I think that's a good result for a first meeting.”

Isabelle walked her out.

“You handled that well.”

“They were willing to listen.”

“Not everyone is.”

Elena adjusted the strap of her bag.

“Then I suppose the important thing is finding out who actually wants to solve the problem.”

Isabelle nodded.

For once, she did not try to turn the moment into a family discussion.

At the Valmont estate, Catherine read the meeting summary that evening.

Richard sat opposite her.

“She's agreed to review the process?”

“Only the process. She has not agreed to implement anything.”

“Isabelle was clear about that.”

Catherine looked at the summary.

“She has a sensible approach.”

Richard nodded.

“She also seems determined not to be absorbed into the foundation.”

“Then we should respect that.”

He closed the document.

“Do you think she will ever want to be part of the family again?”

Catherine took a moment before answering.

“I think asking that question too often will make it harder for her to answer honestly.”

Richard said nothing.

Catherine returned to the report.

For the first time, the foundation's work was being discussed with Elena as a professional whose decisions belonged to her.

It was a small change.

But it mattered.

Edward's next task was to establish the originating family's authority.

The Day-53 request had been submitted by a recognised family authority.

But the family identity remained sealed.

The request could not be used to identify a living subject.

Nor could the investigation assume that the family was Aurelius merely because the historical records included a 2:17 a.m. maternity entry associated elsewhere with Victoria Aurelius.

The source records had not been linked.

Edward needed a lawful way to determine whether the family authority could be independently confirmed without exposing the protected subject.

Helen Ward proposed a restricted authority review.

It would examine:

- The original family authority instrument.
- The issuing authority's appointment register.
- The witness's limited role.
- The request's scope.
- Any later amendments or withdrawals.

The review would not disclose the protected subject's identity.

Edward approved the request.

Two days later, the review began.

The original family authority instrument was produced under controlled access.

The signatory had been recognised as a family authority for the limited purpose of initiating personal communication and requesting preservation.

The instrument had not authorised the signatory to determine the protected individual's identity.

It had not authorised the signatory to appoint a receiving family.

It had not authorised the signatory to require a biological test.

The instrument remained valid for its original purpose.

No withdrawal had been recorded.

The witness appointment was also examined.

The witness had confirmed execution of the declaration.

The witness had not certified the signatory's biological relationship to the protected individual.

Edward read the conclusion.

**The originating family authority was valid for submission of the preservation and review request. Its scope did not extend to identification of the protected subject or compulsory biological examination.**

Eleanor looked at him.

“So the family had the right to ask.”

“Yes.”

“But not the right to decide who would be tested.”

“Correct.”

“Then who can identify the subject?”

“The next stage has to establish that independently.”

Edward returned to the Day-53 request.

A separate field had been marked:

**Potential subject identification route.**

The field did not contain a name.

It referred to an original clinical source and a separate personal identity record.

The two sources had been deposited under different custodial arrangements.

They had never been compared.

The request required them to be examined together before any living individual could be approached.

Edward checked the references.

One was a St Catherine's historical record.

The other was a protected personal identity record held by Meridian.

They had never been compared.

The next morning, Meridian confirmed that the protected personal identity record remained available.

It was not a DNA record.

It was a sealed historical identity document containing information about a living person.

The record's custodian could not disclose the person's name without a valid authority.

But the custodian could confirm its existence and preservation status.

Edward requested a controlled examination of the record's relationship to the St Catherine's clinical source.

The proposed examination would not reveal the living person's identity to the investigation team unless the independent examiner found that disclosure was authorised and necessary.

The request was sent to the relevant authorities.

A response came late that afternoon.

The examination could proceed.

But the examiner had to be different from the person who prepared the original identity comparison document.

The examiner also had to be independent of the family authority and the receiving administration.

Edward read the conditions.

Eleanor leaned over his shoulder.

“Another examiner?”

“Yes.”

“Why not Dr. Bennett?”

“She was proposed for the biological comparison. This is a historical source-identity review. The appointment must remain separate.”

“Could Dr. Cole do it?”

“She examined the original maternity slip and band. Her previous work would need to be disclosed, and the authority wants a separate reviewer for the personal identity record.”

Eleanor nodded.

“Then we find someone else.”

At 8:45 p.m., Edward received the proposed appointment list.

There were three candidates.

One had previously worked for Meridian.

One had advised the family authority.

The third had no recorded involvement in the original receiving routes or the earlier administrative reviews.

Her name was Dr. Priya Nair.

Her speciality was forensic document and identity-record comparison.

Edward requested her independence declaration.

The declaration arrived the following morning.

Dr. Nair disclosed no prior role in the historical processes.

She had not advised any of the families.

She had not handled the original records.

She accepted the proposed examination, subject to access approval.

Her scope was narrow:

**Determine whether the protected personal identity record and the original St Catherine's clinical source can be reliably associated, and identify any limitations that prevent a conclusion.**

She would not determine biological parentage.

She would not identify the living subject to the investigation team without separate authorisation.

And she would not recommend that any person be approached for testing unless the independent source review established a lawful identification route.

Edward signed the request.

For the first time, the investigation had two separate examination paths.

One concerned whether the historical maternity and neonatal materials could be connected.

The other concerned whether the protected personal identity record could be connected to an original clinical source.

Neither had yet established a living identity.

Neither had established a biological relationship.

But both were now proceeding under separate, properly defined authority.

Edward placed the two appointment requests side by side.

The old system had treated references as if they were answers.

This time, the investigators would wait for the evidence.

And if the evidence eventually pointed toward Elena, she would be asked—not instructed—to decide whether she wished to participate.
