# **Chapter 109 — The Warning That Arrived Before the Decision**

The routing cover had been written on day 34.

That date mattered. 

It was the day the original OR-28 independent recipient review had been formally requested. It was also the day Charles Vale had begun circulating instructions concerning the proposed family allocation.

Edward had spent years examining records created after the arrangements had already started.

Now, for the first time, he was looking at a document that appeared to anticipate the consequences.

He placed the photograph of the routing cover beside the revised OR-28 request.

The two documents were different in tone.

The routing cover warned against treating temporary care as permanent allocation.

The revised request allowed the administrative process to close while the independent identity review remained unresolved.

Between those two documents lay twenty-two days.

Twenty-two days in which someone had changed the direction of the process.

“Have we received the original?” Edward asked.

Jonathan checked his messages.

“The archive supervisor has confirmed it is being transferred under the existing preservation authority. We should have it this afternoon.”

“Who held it before Unit Six?”

“That's what I'm trying to establish.”

Edward pointed at the initials.

“C.V. could be Charles's initials, but we need to verify the signature and the chain of custody.”

“Understood.”

“And don't assume the handwriting is his just because the initials match.”

Jonathan nodded.

Edward closed the folder.

“Find the first person who received it.”

The original routing cover arrived shortly after three.

It was stored inside a transparent preservation sleeve, with its original fold lines intact. The paper had yellowed slightly, but the ink remained legible.

The archive supervisor watched as Edward signed the inspection register.

“It's a single sheet,” she explained. “It was separated from the day-56 request during an earlier inventory. The reference number was entered incorrectly, which is why it wasn't included in the first search.”

“Was it opened?”

“The sleeve was opened for preservation assessment. The document itself has not been altered.”

Edward examined the front.

**OR-28 — RESTRICTED CORRESPONDENCE**

**ROUTING AUTHORITY: VALE PRIVATE COUNSEL**

**DATE: DAY 34**

The destination field was partially obscured by an old adhesive mark.

Only two words remained visible.

**RECEIVING REVIEW.**

On the reverse, Charles Vale's initials appeared beneath the warning.

Dr Mira Sen had already been asked to examine the handwriting.

Her preliminary assessment arrived later that afternoon.

The initials were consistent with Charles's known handwriting, but the signature area contained no full signature. The warning itself appeared to have been written by a different person.

Edward read the report.

“So Charles approved the routing, but may not have written the warning.”

“That's correct,” Dr Sen said over the telephone. “The initials are consistent with his handwriting. The main text is not. I would not attribute the warning to him.”

“Can you identify its author?”

“Not from this document alone.”

Edward thanked her.

He returned to the routing cover.

Someone had written a warning.

Charles had authorised the document's circulation.

But the author and the person who approved its movement were not necessarily the same.

“Jonathan,” he said, “we need the original dispatch register.”

The dispatch register was found in the Vale Private Counsel successor archive.

It was not a list of the documents themselves.

It was a record of sealed correspondence moving between offices.

The day-34 entry for OR-28 contained three lines.

**Prepared by: Family Allocation Administration.**

**Authorised for restricted circulation: C. Vale.**

**Receiving destination: R-04 / Recipient Review Custody.**

Edward read the entry.

“R-04 received it.”

“Yes,” Jonathan replied.

“Then why was it later found among the Unit Six records?”

“The R-04 archive transferred its preserved correspondence to Unit Six after the trust closed. The routing cover was included in that transfer.”

“Who signed the original receipt?”

Jonathan turned the page.

There was a signature.

**MARIANNE CROSS.**

Edward sat back.

Marianne had been the administrative liaison responsible for delivering notifications once a receiving identity had been verified.

She had also preserved the incomplete recipient-status entries and refused to treat the day-47 suspension as approval.

But this document had arrived at R-04 on day 34.

Before the independent review was suspended.

Before the later notification disputes.

Before the false appointment of Adrian Holt.

Edward looked at the signature.

“Ask Marianne to come in again.”

Marianne Cross arrived the following morning.

She looked older than she had during their first meeting, though her expression remained composed.

Edward placed the routing cover in front of her.

“Do you remember this?”

Marianne examined the paper.

Then she turned it over.

“Yes.”

“You received it?”

“I did.”

“On day 34?”

She checked the dispatch reference.

“Yes.”

“Who delivered it?”

“A courier from Vale Private Counsel.”

“Did you read it?”

Marianne's eyes remained on the handwritten warning.

“I was required to register the instruction. I read the document because it concerned the recipient review.”

“Do you recognise the handwriting?”

She studied it more carefully.

“I think so.”

“Whose?”

Marianne hesitated.

“Eleanor Vale.”

Edward did not react immediately.

“Are you certain?”

“No. I recognise the style of her administrative notes, but I cannot certify it.”

“Did Eleanor have authority to issue instructions to R-04?”

“Not to appoint a receiving family. She could issue preservation and separation instructions concerning the records under her custody.”

“Then why was her warning on a document authorised by Charles Vale?”

Marianne looked at him.

“Because they were not necessarily disagreeing.”

Edward frowned.

“Explain.”

“Charles wanted the proposed arrangements recorded. Eleanor wanted the proposed arrangements kept separate from verified identity. Those positions could coexist if everyone respected the restrictions.”

“And did they?”

“No.”

Marianne placed the paper down.

“R-04 received several instructions during that period. Some came from Vale Private Counsel. Some came from the medical records authority. Some came from Eleanor. The purpose of the trust was to preserve the status of the records until the required authorities could complete the process.”

“Who was responsible for ensuring that happened?”

“No one person.”

“That's convenient.”

“It's also true,” Marianne replied. “The system required separate approvals. I was not permitted to replace a missing approval with my own judgment.”

Edward pointed to the warning.

“Why did you keep this?”

“Because it was relevant to the recipient review.”

“Did you believe someone was trying to turn temporary care into permanent allocation?”

Marianne looked down.

“I believed there was a risk.”

“Did you tell Charles?”

“Yes.”

“What did he say?”

“He said the children could not remain in an administrative limbo indefinitely.”

Edward waited.

Marianne continued.

“He argued that temporary care arrangements needed to be recognised. He was right about that. Children required medical care, stable accommodation, and responsible adults. But I told him that recording current care was not the same as confirming permanent family identity.”

“What was his response?”

“He said the distinction would be maintained.”

Edward looked at the handwritten warning again.

“Then why did the records change?”

“I don't know.”

“Did Eleanor know about the proposed allocation?”

“Yes.”

“Did she object?”

Marianne nodded.

“She repeatedly said that the records could not be merged until the receiving authority was independently appointed.”

“Did she ever identify the correct receiving family?”

“No.”

“Did she know which infant belonged to which mother?”

Marianne shook her head.

“Not from the documents I handled.”

Edward let the silence settle.

Then he asked the question that had brought him back to Marianne.

“Did you ever see a receiving family acknowledge the proposed allocation?”

Marianne's answer was immediate.

“No.”

“Not even through an intermediary?”

“No valid acknowledgement.”

“Then what was the purpose of the notification marked complete?”

“I have told you. The notification was not completed.”

“And the altered record?”

“I did not authorise it.”

Edward studied her face.

She appeared tired, but not surprised.

“Did you know about the day-34 warning when the day-56 status request was processed?”

“Yes.”

“Did you attach it to the revised request?”

“No.”

“Why not?”

“Because it was already part of the restricted R-04 correspondence record. The revised request was not supposed to replace it.”

Edward leaned forward.

“Did you understand that the revised request would be distributed without the warning?”

Marianne hesitated.

“I understood that the administrative summary would not include the full recipient-status material.”

“That isn't the same thing.”

“No,” she admitted. “It isn't.”

She looked at the document.

“I thought the original warning would be preserved and consulted whenever a permanent allocation was considered.”

“But it wasn't.”

“No.”

For the first time, her composure faltered.

“I should have insisted on a written acknowledgement that the restriction remained active.”

Edward made a note.

“Did anyone ask you to withdraw the warning?”

“No.”

“Did anyone ask you to destroy it?”

“No.”

“Did anyone ask you to remove it from the day-56 distribution?”

Marianne considered the question.

“The instruction was that R-04 recipient-level material should not be duplicated. I understood that to include the warning.”

“Who gave that instruction?”

“Vale Private Counsel.”

“Charles?”

“I cannot say.”

Edward closed his notebook.

Marianne had confirmed the warning was received, registered, and preserved.

She had also confirmed that it was not attached to the revised status request.

But the question remained.

Why had the restriction been allowed to survive in one archive while the administrative system moved forward without it?

At the Aurelius residence, Victoria was reviewing the latest investigation report.

Alexander sat across from her.

The document contained no new biological evidence.

No DNA comparison had been completed.

No infant identity had been established.

Yet the day-34 warning had changed the character of the investigation.

Victoria read the translated summary twice.

“The person who wrote this understood the danger,” she said.

Alexander nodded.

“They knew a temporary arrangement could be mistaken for a permanent family allocation.”

“And they tried to prevent it.”

“Apparently.”

Victoria placed the report down.

“Then why did nobody stop the process?”

“Because a warning is not an appointment authority. It cannot substitute for the approvals that were missing.”

She looked at him.

“I'm not asking why the document was legally insufficient. I'm asking why the people responsible did not act on it.”

Alexander did not answer immediately.

He had spent eighteen years looking for a daughter whose identity had been obscured by a sequence of administrative decisions.

He had learned that every apparent answer created more questions.

“Some of them acted,” he said finally. “Eleanor preserved the restrictions. Marianne retained the acknowledgements. Margaret refused to certify the records. Dr Hale refused to complete the medical reconciliation.”

“And yet the system still moved.”

“Yes.”

Victoria looked toward the windows.

“Perhaps everyone believed someone else would stop it.”

Alexander folded the report.

“Or perhaps someone understood that as long as each person handled only one part, no one would see the whole.”

Victoria was quiet.

After a moment, she asked, “Have we learned anything about the child?”

“No.”

“About Elena?”

“No.”

Victoria lowered her eyes.

She had grown accustomed to hearing that answer.

It did not make it easier.

“I hope she is happy with her programme,” she said.

Alexander looked at her.

“So do I.”

They did not discuss contacting Elena.

They had no verified basis to do so, and Victoria was determined not to turn an unfinished investigation into another intrusion in the life of someone who might have nothing to do with them.

For now, they would wait for evidence.

At the Valmont Estate, Elena was checking her travel itinerary when a message arrived from Clara.

**Clara:** Have you finished packing?

**Elena:** Almost.

**Clara:** That means no.

**Elena:** It means I am reviewing the packing list.

**Clara:** You're going to bring three books and then complain about the weight.

Elena glanced at the books stacked beside her suitcase.

**Elena:** Four.

**Clara:** That's worse.

Elena laughed.

A video call followed.

Clara appeared on screen with a notebook in hand.

“Show me the suitcase.”

“No.”

“Why?”

“Because you're going to judge me.”

“I already am.”

Elena turned the camera slightly.

Clara stared at the neatly arranged clothes and documents.

“You've packed enough stationery to open a shop.”

“Stationery is useful.”

“So is empty space.”

Elena considered that.

“That's debatable.”

Clara laughed.

They discussed the programme schedule, accommodation, local transport, and the academic project Elena wanted to develop.

Elena had been thinking about a digital system for managing small educational grants.

She wanted to create something practical: a platform that could help smaller institutions track applications, budgets, and scholarship distribution without requiring expensive administrative software.

It was still an early idea.

She had no team, no funding, and no certainty that it would work.

But she had begun outlining the requirements.

Clara listened carefully.

“You could make a small prototype first.”

“That is what I was thinking.”

“Then test it with one organisation.”

“Exactly.”

“And don't try to build everything at once.”

Elena smiled.

“Are you giving me advice?”

“Yes.”

“That's unusual.”

“I can be useful occasionally.”

Elena laughed again.

When the call ended, she looked at her notebook.

The project was small compared with the businesses run by the Valmonts or the Aureliuses.

That was precisely why she liked it.

It was something she could build from the beginning.

Something she could understand, improve, and take responsibility for herself.

She closed the notebook and returned to her packing.

Edward received the formal handwriting assessment that evening.

Dr Mira Sen had compared the day-34 warning with Eleanor Vale's known correspondence.

The result was cautious.

The main text showed several consistent features with Eleanor's writing, particularly the formation of the letters *r*, *g*, and *t*. However, the sample was too short to establish authorship conclusively.

The initials C.V. were consistent with Charles Vale.

The routing cover had therefore likely been prepared by one person, annotated by another, and authorised for circulation by Charles.

Edward read the report.

Then he looked at the distribution register.

“Jonathan.”

“Yes?”

“Who was responsible for preparing the day-34 OR-28 review request?”

Jonathan opened his laptop.

“According to the register, Family Allocation Administration.”

“Not an individual?”

“The original request has a preparer's code. We haven't identified the user yet.”

“Get the personnel records.”

Jonathan searched the archive index.

A result appeared.

He opened it.

Then he stopped.

“What is it?” Edward asked.

“The preparer's code.”

“Who?”

Jonathan turned the screen.

The record showed an employee who had worked at Vale Private Counsel during the relevant period.

**ELEANOR VALE.**

Edward looked at the name.

“Eleanor prepared the original review request?”

“According to the personnel register.”

“And the warning appears to be in her handwriting.”

“Yes.”

Edward returned to the routing cover.

That meant Eleanor had not merely reviewed the restrictions after the system began failing.

She had helped initiate the independent review before it failed.

She had prepared the request.

She had written a warning against converting temporary care into permanent allocation.

She had later reviewed the sealed documents at Unit Six and insisted that Schedule 4 remain separate.

Her actions formed a consistent pattern.

But one detail remained unexplained.

If Eleanor had prepared the original review request on day 34, why had she not been able to prevent its suspension on day 47?

Edward examined the original routing cover once more.

A small line appeared beneath the destination field.

It had been almost entirely covered by the old adhesive mark.

He tilted the paper under the examination light.

The faint text became clearer.

**COPY TO ORIGINATING REPRESENTATIVE: WITHHELD PENDING CONSENT.**

Edward read it twice.

“Jonathan, find out whose consent was required.”

Jonathan looked at the document.

“Eleanor's?”

“Perhaps. Or someone else's.”

Edward placed the routing cover carefully back into its sleeve.

The review had been initiated.

The warning had been preserved.

But the person who wrote it had not possessed enough authority to make the warning binding on every department.

And the missing consent might explain why.

He looked at the final line again.

**COPY TO ORIGINATING REPRESENTATIVE: WITHHELD PENDING CONSENT.**

For the first time, Edward wondered whether the person who had been kept from seeing the review was not a receiving family representative at all.

It might have been one of the biological mothers.

Or someone acting on her behalf.

And if that was true, the investigation had not yet discovered the most important person who had been excluded from the process.
