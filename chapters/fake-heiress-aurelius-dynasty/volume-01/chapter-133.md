# **Chapter 133 — The Response That Never Reached Its Destination**

Meridian Records and Preservation Services had received the request before Edward arrived.

That did not mean the archive had agreed to release the document.

The records manager, Rachel Morton, made that distinction clear as she led Edward and Jonathan into a restricted examination room.

“PO-04 is not part of the ordinary identity archive,” she explained. “It was transferred under a separate custody instruction. We can confirm its existence and inspect the transfer history, but access to the original correspondence remains subject to the release conditions.”

“Who has authority to release it?” Edward asked.

“The appointed personal correspondence custodian, together with independent confirmation of the principal's identity.”

“Was a custodian ever appointed?”

Rachel consulted her register.

“No formal appointment appears in our records.”

“Then who authorised the transfer?”

“Charles Vale authorised collection from V-17. That did not make him the appointed custodian.”

Edward nodded.

“Can we inspect the delivery-attempt record?”

Rachel considered the request.

“We can provide the preservation copy. The original remains sealed.”

“That is sufficient for now.”

She placed a document envelope on the table.

Its reference was:

**PO-04 / ORIGINAL DELIVERY ATTEMPT — DAY 43.**

Edward did not open it immediately.

He examined the seal, photographed the exterior, and asked Rachel to confirm the archive's condition report.

Only after the procedure had been recorded did he remove the preservation copy.

The document was a delivery report.

Not a letter.

Not an acknowledgement.

A report prepared by someone responsible for moving protected correspondence.

The first section contained ordinary details.

**Origin:** Personal Representation File, PR-06.

**Destination:** Protected Principal — P-04.

**Delivery method:** Restricted personal delivery.

**Delivery date:** Day 43.

**Result:** Delivery not completed.

Edward read the last entry again.

“Why?”

Rachel turned to the second page.

The explanation was recorded in three lines.

*Recipient identity confirmation unavailable.*

*Receiving authority not appointed.*

*Original communication returned to sealed custody.*

Jonathan looked up.

“So the letter was never delivered.”

“That is what the report says,” Edward replied.

He continued reading.

The delivery officer had been instructed to verify three things before handing over the correspondence:

1. The person receiving it was P-04.
2. The person had been informed of the purpose of the communication.
3. The delivery officer had no authority to interpret or answer on the principal's behalf.

The final instruction was underlined.

*If identity cannot be independently confirmed, do not leave the communication with an intermediary.*

Edward looked at Rachel.

“Who was the delivery officer?”

“The name is restricted in this copy.”

“Is it recorded in the original?”

“Yes.”

“Can you confirm whether the officer was Gabriel Vale?”

Rachel checked the index.

“No. Gabriel's name appears as the communication representative, not the delivery officer.”

“Was Gabriel present?”

“There is no indication of that.”

Jonathan examined the return section.

“Who received the letter back?”

The report identified the receiving office only as:

**VPC — PERSONAL REPRESENTATION CUSTODY.**

There was no individual signature.

Edward looked at the date.

Day 43.

The same day Gabriel's conditional response had been returned to Charles's office.

The same period Eleanor had warned that a representative's response could not substitute for the principal's personal acknowledgement.

Two documents had moved through the system.

One was a conditional response from a representative.

The other was an original communication intended for P-04.

Neither had completed the process.

“Is there a record of what the original communication contained?” Edward asked.

Rachel shook her head.

“Not in the delivery report.”

“Was it described on the envelope?”

“Yes. The inventory contains a brief description.”

She opened the storage index.

**Contents declared by originating office:**

- Notice of proposed receiving arrangement.
- Explanation of proposed representation.
- Request for personal response.
- Copy of relevant procedural rights.

Edward studied the list.

“Who prepared it?”

“The Personal Instructions Division.”

“Was the principal being asked to accept a child?”

Rachel checked the original inventory description.

“No. The stated purpose was to explain a proposed receiving arrangement and request the principal's position.”

“Was the child's identity included?”

“The inventory doesn't say.”

“Was the originating family identified?”

“Not in the description.”

Edward looked at Jonathan.

The document might have been a genuine attempt to contact P-04.

But the principal had not received it.

And the report did not explain whether anyone had tried to resolve the identity problem.

“Can we see the return correspondence?” he asked.

Rachel hesitated.

“There is a separate entry.”

She opened another register.

**Day 44 — Delivery Failure Notice**

**Instruction:** Return to originating office.

**Additional action:** No repeat delivery without revised identity verification.

The receiving officer had added a handwritten note.

*The person presented at the destination declined to provide identifying confirmation. No independent verification was available.*

Edward read it twice.

“The person presented at the destination?”

“Yes.”

“Was that person P-04?”

“The report does not establish that.”

“Did the person refuse the communication?”

“No. It says the person declined to provide identifying confirmation.”

“Those are not the same thing.”

Rachel nodded.

“No.”

Edward looked at the report again.

Someone had arrived at the destination.

Someone had been asked to establish their identity.

They had declined.

The delivery had been stopped.

But the record did not establish why.

At the Aurelius Estate, Victoria received the update that afternoon.

She listened as Edward explained the day-43 delivery attempt.

“So someone may have gone to meet P-04,” she said.

“Yes.”

“But they could not confirm that the person they met was the principal.”

“Correct.”

“Did the person refuse to receive the communication?”

“No. We have no evidence of that.”

Victoria looked down.

“Then we still don't know whether P-04 ever knew someone was trying to reach them.”

“That's right.”

Alexander folded his arms.

“Whoever organised the delivery knew the identity was unconfirmed. Why send someone at all?”

“To attempt verification,” Edward replied. “The delivery instructions required the officer to establish identity before handing over the material.”

“Then the procedure worked.”

“Partly. It prevented an unverified person from receiving protected correspondence.”

Victoria looked at him.

“But it did not create another opportunity for the principal to be contacted.”

“No.”

“Was a second attempt made?”

Edward paused.

“We know Gabriel requested one on day 44. It was denied.”

Victoria's expression tightened.

“So the first attempt failed, and the second was stopped.”

“On day 45, Eleanor objected to the suspension. She said the principal should receive the information before anyone treated the absence of a response as a decision.”

Alexander looked towards the window.

“And someone maintained the suspension.”

“Yes.”

Victoria returned to the day-43 report.

“Then I want to know who was waiting at the destination.”

In Singapore, Elena's project team had received its evaluation feedback.

The supervisor's comments were encouraging.

The platform was practical, the permission structure was clear, and the audit trail was well designed.

But one recommendation stood out.

**Include a procedure for unsuccessful verification. A failed identity check should not automatically become a permanent rejection.**

Elena read it twice.

Daniel leaned over her shoulder.

“That sounds like a reasonable improvement.”

“It is.”

“Can we add it before the final submission?”

“Yes. We need to distinguish between a failed verification attempt and an actual refusal.”

Daniel opened the prototype.

“What should the status say?”

Elena considered it.

“Verification incomplete. Further contact required, where appropriate.”

“And if the person refuses to identify themselves?”

“Record that exact event. Don't convert it into refusal of the application or refusal of the arrangement.”

Daniel smiled.

“You've become very particular about wording.”

Elena shrugged.

“Words decide what people think happened.”

She added a new section to the system.

**Unsuccessful Verification — Follow-up Required**

It included fields for:

- Date and method of attempted verification.
- Person or office conducting the attempt.
- Evidence requested.
- Whether the person declined verification or was unable to provide it.
- Whether the underlying request was received.
- Next authorised action.

Professor Lin reviewed the change later that afternoon.

“This is useful,” he said. “You're making the system distinguish between what happened and what someone later assumes happened.”

Elena looked at the updated screen.

“That was the point.”

Edward returned to the Meridian archive the following morning.

Rachel had prepared a second set of records.

The first was the delivery officer's assignment sheet.

The second was the destination verification form.

The third was a return instruction.

Edward began with the assignment sheet.

The delivery officer had been appointed by a separate administrative service.

The name was visible.

**Nora Whitcomb.**

Jonathan looked up.

“The courier?”

“Yes.”

They had already interviewed Nora about the day-40 envelope and the day-61 transfer case.

Edward read the assignment.

Nora had been asked to deliver the personal correspondence to a protected destination.

She was not authorised to identify the principal independently.

She could only compare the person's identification against a sealed verification reference held by the originating office.

The reference had not been provided.

“Why send her without the reference?” Jonathan asked.

Edward examined the dispatch note.

“Perhaps the reference was supposed to be delivered separately.”

The dispatch note contained a second envelope number.

**PO-04 / VERIFICATION KEY — SEPARATE DELIVERY.**

But the status field was blank.

“Was the verification key ever sent?”

Rachel checked the register.

“No delivery record appears.”

“Was it included in the correspondence package?”

“No. The inventory lists it separately.”

Edward looked at Jonathan.

“Then Nora was sent to deliver a protected letter without the means to verify its recipient.”

Rachel nodded.

“That is consistent with the documents.”

“Who was responsible for sending the verification key?”

She consulted the assignment sheet.

“The originating office.”

“Which office?”

“The designation field is blank.”

Edward leaned back.

The same missing designation again.

Someone had assigned the correspondence to an originating office without identifying which office held responsibility for completing the verification.

Nora had been sent to a destination without the necessary reference.

The attempt had failed.

And the failure had later been used to justify suspending further contact.

Nora agreed to meet Edward that evening.

She remembered the assignment.

“I was given a sealed envelope,” she said. “I was told not to hand it over unless the person could confirm a reference.”

“Did you receive that reference?”

“No.”

“Were you told someone would provide it at the destination?”

“I was told it might be available through the local contact.”

“Was the local contact named?”

“Not on my copy.”

“Did you meet anyone?”

“Yes.”

“Can you describe the person?”

Nora looked uncomfortable.

“I would rather not guess at details after so many years. I remember an adult. I cannot confidently identify their age or appearance now.”

“Did the person claim to be P-04?”

“No.”

“Did they refuse the letter?”

“They asked what it was.”

“What did you say?”

“That it was private correspondence requiring identity confirmation.”

“And?”

“They asked who had sent it.”

“Did you tell them?”

“I said the sender was listed as a protected originating office.”

Nora paused.

“They asked me whether the letter concerned a child.”

Edward remained still.

“What did you say?”

“I told them I wasn't authorised to discuss its contents.”

“Did they provide identification?”

“No.”

“Did they refuse?”

“They said they would not identify themselves without knowing who was requesting their information.”

Edward exchanged a glance with Jonathan.

That was different from refusing the correspondence.

“What happened next?”

“I contacted the dispatch office. They told me not to leave the envelope.”

“Did you ask for the verification key?”

“Yes.”

“What did they say?”

“That it had not been issued.”

Nora looked at the table.

“I was uncomfortable with the situation. The person at the destination was being asked to identify themselves to someone who couldn't explain who had authorised the request.”

Edward understood her concern.

“Did you return the envelope?”

“Yes. Sealed.”

“Did you write the return note?”

“I wrote the delivery report. The administrative office added the final status.”

“Did you ever see the person again?”

“No.”

“Did anyone contact you to arrange another attempt?”

“No.”

Nora hesitated.

“There was one thing.”

Edward waited.

“Before I left the destination, the person asked me to write something down.”

“What?”

Nora took a folded paper from her folder.

“I kept a copy of the note I made at the time. It wasn't part of the official delivery report.”

She opened it.

The handwriting was hers.

One sentence had been written beneath the date.

*Please tell whoever sent you that I cannot answer a question I have not been allowed to read.*

Edward read it again.

The words were simple.

They did not prove who had spoken them.

They did not establish that the person was P-04.

But they changed the meaning of the failed delivery.

The person had not necessarily refused to answer.

They might have been asking to see the question first.

Back at the office, Jonathan placed Nora's note beside the official delivery report.

“The official record says identity confirmation unavailable.”

“And Nora's note says the person wanted to know what the communication concerned.”

“Could the person have been P-04?”

“Possibly.”

“Could it have been someone else?”

“Yes.”

Edward looked at the day-43 delivery record.

The original communication had been returned.

The verification key had never been issued.

The person at the destination had not identified themselves.

And the only recorded statement from that person suggested they wanted to know what they were being asked to answer.

“Find the verification key,” Edward said.

Jonathan searched the archive reference.

A result appeared.

**PO-04 / VERIFICATION KEY — PREPARED DAY 42.**

Edward leaned closer.

“Prepared?”

“Yes.”

“Was it dispatched?”

Jonathan opened the record.

The status was marked:

**WITHHELD — ORIGINATING OFFICE INSTRUCTION.**

“Who issued the instruction?”

Jonathan enlarged the signature field.

There was a name.

**Charles Vale.**

Edward stared at it.

The key had existed before Nora's delivery attempt.

It had been prepared.

And someone had deliberately withheld it.

The instruction contained one further line:

*Verification key is not to be released until the proposed receiving authority confirms its appointment.*

Edward read it slowly.

The person at the destination had been asked to identify themselves before being allowed to read the communication.

The verification key had been withheld because the receiving authority had not been appointed.

But the receiving authority could not be appointed until the identity process was completed.

And the identity process could not be completed without the principal's response.

Edward placed the document down.

The system had become a circle with no legitimate way out.

Someone had built a process in which every missing approval depended on another missing approval.

And on day 43, when a person had finally asked to see the question before answering it, the person had been sent away without ever receiving the letter.
