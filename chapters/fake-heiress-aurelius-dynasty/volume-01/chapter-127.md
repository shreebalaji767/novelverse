# **Chapter 127 — The Instruction Thomas Greer Was Never Supposed to Give**

Thomas Greer did not deny writing the instruction.

He denied that it meant what Edward believed it meant.

They met in a private conference room at Greer's current legal practice. He was older than in the archived photographs, with silver hair, a carefully maintained suit, and the restrained manner of someone accustomed to answering difficult questions without volunteering unnecessary information.

He read the PI-42 instruction twice.

Then he placed it on the table.

“I remember this.”

“You authorised the replacement wording?”

“I authorised an administrative amendment.”

“You removed the requirement for the principal's personal acknowledgement.”

“I permitted the file to proceed without that acknowledgement being treated as a condition for temporary administrative continuity.”

Edward watched him.

“That is a very elaborate way of describing the same thing.”

“It is not the same thing.”

“Explain the difference.”

Greer folded his hands.

“The proposed arrangement had reached an impasse. A possible recipient had been identified. A representative had communicated a conditional response. Temporary care arrangements were already in place. The administration needed to preserve those arrangements without pretending that permanent placement had been authorised.”

“So you removed a requirement.”

“I separated the requirement from the temporary administrative process.”

“Was that your authority to do?”

Greer did not answer immediately.

“I believed it was.”

“Did you have authority to waive the principal's acknowledgement?”

“No.”

“Then what exactly did you believe you were authorised to do?”

“To ensure that the absence of an acknowledgement did not automatically invalidate the existing temporary arrangements.”

Edward glanced at Jonathan.

Greer had chosen his words carefully.

He was not claiming that the principal had agreed.

He was claiming that the administration needed to continue while the question remained unanswered.

“Who asked you to make the amendment?” Edward asked.

“Charles Vale.”

“Did he ask you to remove the acknowledgement requirement?”

“He asked me to review the procedure.”

“Did he explain why?”

“He said the administrative system was treating the proposed recipient's lack of personal acknowledgement as a reason to suspend all continuity arrangements.”

“Was that accurate?”

“Partially.”

“Meaning?”

“Some temporary care payments, medical records, and institutional responsibilities were being delayed because the proposed receiving identity had not been finalised.”

Edward looked at the day-35 Northbridge trust records.

That part was possible.

The trust had been created to fund temporary care, not permanent placement. Delays in administrative continuity could affect payments and services.

But none of that required a principal's consent to be recorded.

“Why didn't you simply authorise the temporary arrangements to continue?” Edward asked.

“I thought that was what I had done.”

“Then why did you replace the wording with ‘Principal acknowledgement not required for administrative continuity’?”

“Because that was the distinction I wanted recorded.”

Edward leaned forward.

“And why did you leave the original acknowledgement form inside the package?”

“Because the principal's response was still required before any permanent appointment.”

Greer produced another document.

It was a memorandum dated day 41.

The heading read:

**TEMPORARY ADMINISTRATIVE CONTINUITY — PR-06**

The memorandum stated that temporary care arrangements could remain active while the proposed recipient's identity and authority were independently verified.

It specifically prohibited:

- Recording the proposed recipient as permanently appointed.
- Treating Gabriel Vale's communication as personal consent.
- Transferring permanent responsibility.
- Using the administrative continuity entry as evidence of biological identity.

At the bottom was Greer's signature.

Edward read the document twice.

“This memorandum was never included in the day-47 summary.”

“I don't know why.”

“Did you send it to Charles?”

“Yes.”

“Did you send it to Daniel Mercer?”

“No.”

“Did you send it to the Family Allocation Records Office?”

“I believe a copy was included in the legal distribution.”

“Can you prove that?”

Greer opened his briefcase.

“I kept the dispatch copy.”

The distribution list contained four entries.

Charles Vale.

Gabriel Vale.

Personal Instructions Division.

Family Allocation Records Office.

The receipt section showed three acknowledgements.

The Family Allocation Records Office had returned its copy.

Its reason was handwritten:

*Temporary administrative continuity is outside the scope of permanent allocation registration. No receiving authority has been appointed.*

Edward placed the memorandum beside the day-47 summary.

The same distinction appeared repeatedly.

Temporary care could continue.

Permanent allocation could not.

Yet the summary had converted an incomplete receiving arrangement into something that looked approved.

“Who prepared the day-47 summary?” Edward asked.

“I don't know.”

“Who requested it?”

“Charles.”

“Did you review it?”

“No.”

“Did you authorise its distribution?”

“No.”

“Then your instruction was used to support a statement you never approved.”

Greer looked at the summary.

“That appears to be the case.”

Edward was not ready to leave.

“Mr Greer, I need to understand something about P-04.”

Greer became noticeably more cautious.

“What about it?”

“You authorised the administrative amendment. Did you know the identity of the principal?”

“Yes.”

“Was P-04 the proposed recipient?”

“I cannot confirm that.”

“Did the person have a legal representative?”

“Not in the ordinary sense.”

“What does that mean?”

“The protected arrangement used a communication intermediary. It did not establish a conventional legal representation relationship.”

“Then why was Gabriel authorised?”

“Because he had been approved as a restricted communication intermediary under AV-18.”

“Who approved him?”

“The protected authority was verified by Eleanor Vale.”

“Who was the principal?”

Greer closed his briefcase.

“I cannot disclose that.”

“Even now?”

“Not without the conditions specified in the original instruction.”

Edward studied him.

“Do you believe those conditions can still be fulfilled?”

“I don't know.”

“Is the principal alive?”

Greer looked at him.

“I cannot answer that.”

The response was not reassuring.

But Edward did not push further.

He had learned that forcing an answer from someone who was legally restricted rarely produced useful information.

Instead, he asked another question.

“Did you ever receive a signed acknowledgement from P-04?”

“No.”

“Did you ever receive a written refusal?”

“No.”

“Then what was the principal's actual position?”

Greer remained silent.

Edward stood.

“That is what we are trying to establish.”

At Meridian, the original PR-06 package remained sealed.

Edward requested a detailed examination of its exterior, including the seal, labels, and any transfer markings.

Rachel Morton arranged the inspection.

The package had been preserved carefully.

The original seal was intact.

The label listed the contents:

**Principal information acknowledgement form.**

**Proposed receiving arrangement summary.**

**Questions and clarification sheet.**

A smaller line appeared underneath.

**Personal response pending.**

Edward examined the label.

“Was this description added before or after the package was returned to Charles?”

Rachel checked the preservation record.

“Before.”

“Then whoever prepared the package knew the personal response was outstanding.”

“Yes.”

“Does the package have any record of being opened after day 43?”

“No. The seal was intact when it arrived here on day 61.”

Edward looked at the transfer record.

The sender was Charles Vale's office.

The receiving officer was Eleanor Vale.

He turned the page.

A second inventory sheet had been attached.

It listed the same three documents, but the last item was described differently.

**Personal response — not received.**

Edward pointed to the discrepancy.

“Why does one inventory say ‘personal response pending’ and the other say ‘not received’?”

Rachel examined the two entries.

“They may refer to different stages of the process.”

“Can you tell which came first?”

“The first label was created on day 40. The second inventory was created on day 43.”

“Then the wording changed after the package was returned.”

“Yes.”

Edward looked at the sealed package again.

The acknowledgement form had been preserved.

The response had not been received.

And the day-47 summary would later say consent was recorded.

He requested copies of both inventory records.

In Singapore, Elena's project had reached a more manageable stage.

The student group had completed the revised prototype.

It now included a simple application dashboard, a reviewer permission system, and a version history showing every administrative change.

The group presented it to their programme supervisor.

He examined the screen.

“Why does the system retain previous versions instead of replacing them?”

Elena answered.

“Because a change to a record shouldn't erase the fact that something different was recorded earlier.”

“Wouldn't that make the system more complicated?”

“A little.”

“Is it worth the additional work?”

“I think so. Otherwise, a user can see the current status but cannot understand how it became the current status.”

The supervisor nodded.

“And what happens if the original entry was incorrect?”

“It can be corrected, but the correction should be recorded separately. The system should show what changed, when it changed, and who was authorised to make it.”

He looked at the prototype again.

“That is a useful principle.”

Elena smiled.

After the presentation, her teammate Daniel approached her.

“You were right about the history feature.”

“I know.”

He laughed.

“You could at least pretend to be modest.”

“I've been working on that.”

Her phone vibrated.

A message from Nicholas.

**How is Singapore? Have you remembered to eat properly, or are you surviving on coffee and determination?**

Elena replied:

**I ate lunch. And dinner is planned. You can stop monitoring my food.**

His answer came quickly.

**I will consider reducing the frequency of inspections.**

She shook her head, smiling.

Then she returned to her project.

There were still several improvements to make.

For once, the work in front of her was entirely her own.

The next development came from Daniel Mercer.

He contacted Edward after reviewing the original day-47 distribution records.

“I found the instruction that accompanied the summary,” he said.

“Where?”

“In the old Family Allocation Review archive. It was filed separately from the summary itself.”

“What does it say?”

Daniel sent a scanned copy.

The document was dated day 46.

Its subject read:

**ADMINISTRATIVE STATUS — PR-06 / AV-18**

The instruction was brief.

*For continuity purposes, the representative's conditional response may be reflected as consent recorded. This entry must not be used to establish permanent receiving identity or final placement.*

Edward read it carefully.

There it was.

The phrase that had appeared in the day-47 summary.

But the instruction contained a restriction.

The conditional response could be reflected as consent recorded for continuity purposes.

It could not establish permanent receiving identity or final placement.

Edward looked at the signature.

It belonged to Charles Vale.

He called Daniel.

“Did you receive this instruction?”

“Yes.”

“Did you interpret ‘consent recorded’ as personal consent?”

“At the time, I understood it as an administrative status.”

“Did you know the principal's acknowledgement was missing?”

“Yes.”

“Did you know Gabriel's response was conditional?”

“Yes.”

“Then why did you enter the wording?”

“Because the instruction specifically limited its use to administrative continuity.”

Edward was quiet.

The explanation was consistent with Daniel's earlier account.

But the wording was still misleading.

“Did you think it was appropriate to use the word consent?”

“No.”

“Did you object?”

“I asked whether the phrase could be changed to ‘conditional response received.’”

“What happened?”

“Charles's office rejected the change.”

“Why?”

“He said the system required a standard status field and that the restriction would be preserved in the supporting file.”

Edward looked at the document again.

“Was the restriction preserved?”

Daniel hesitated.

“Not in the ordinary summary.”

“Was it attached?”

“I don't know.”

Edward ended the call.

The investigation had finally located the instruction behind the day-47 wording.

Charles had authorised the phrase.

Daniel had entered it.

The restriction existed, but it had not travelled with the summary.

The administrative record had therefore become more definitive than the underlying instruction.

Edward briefed Alexander and Victoria that evening.

“Charles authorised the phrase ‘consent recorded’ for administrative continuity,” he explained. “He also prohibited its use as evidence of permanent receiving identity or final placement.”

Victoria looked at the document.

“Then he knew it was not actual consent.”

“He knew the principal's acknowledgement was outstanding.”

“Did he know the proposed recipient had not personally agreed?”

“Yes.”

Alexander read the instruction.

“Why would he insist on using that wording?”

“We don't know.”

“Could it have been an attempt to keep temporary arrangements from being disrupted?”

“Possibly.”

Victoria looked at the missing acknowledgement field.

“But the wording could easily be misunderstood.”

“Yes.”

“Was it misunderstood?”

Edward did not answer immediately.

“The ordinary administrative record treated the response as consent. The restricted records retained the conditions. Different offices therefore held different versions of the same situation.”

Alexander closed the file.

“Then we need to establish who had access to both.”

Edward nodded.

“That is where the investigation is going.”

Later that night, Jonathan found another entry in the Family Allocation Review archive.

It concerned the distribution of the day-47 summary.

The summary had been sent to five destinations.

The first four were familiar.

- R-04.
- Personal Instructions Division.
- Family Allocation Records Office.
- Northbridge Continuity Trust.

The fifth was different.

**V-17 — PRIVATE CUSTODY**

There was no named recipient.

No individual acknowledgement.

Only a receipt showing that a sealed copy had been collected.

The signature belonged to Arthur Bell, the former caretaker at V-17.

Edward stared at the record.

“Why was a copy sent to Charles's private custody property?”

Jonathan checked the attached routing instruction.

“Because the document was marked as restricted continuity material.”

“Who requested the copy?”

“Charles Vale.”

“Who authorised the transfer?”

“The same.”

Edward looked at the receipt again.

A handwritten note appeared beneath Arthur Bell's signature.

*Copy received. Original remains with Personal Instructions Division.*

Edward read it twice.

The private property had received a copy of the administrative summary, not the original acknowledgement.

That meant someone at V-17 had been given the version that said consent was recorded.

But whether the copy was intended for storage, review, or some other purpose remained unknown.

Jonathan placed another page beside it.

“This was attached to the V-17 receipt.”

It was a short instruction dated day 48.

*Do not combine this summary with the principal acknowledgement package. The two records must remain separate until personal response is received.*

Edward looked at the wording.

Charles had authorised the summary.

He had arranged for a copy to be stored at V-17.

But he had also instructed that the summary not be merged with the package containing the missing personal acknowledgement.

The distinction was deliberate.

Edward leaned back.

“Find out who collected the V-17 copy after Arthur Bell received it.”

Jonathan nodded.

“And if it was never collected?”

“Then we need to know who had access to the room where it was kept.”

Edward returned to the day-48 instruction.

The same person who had allowed an administrative summary to say consent was recorded had insisted that the original acknowledgement remain separate.

It was not proof of good intentions.

Nor was it proof of deliberate deception.

But it suggested that the file had been divided for a reason.

And the next answer might be waiting inside the private custody archive at V-17.
