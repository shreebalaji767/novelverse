# **Chapter 64 — The Twelfth-Day Correction**

Edward read the correction for the fourth time.

The document was short.

That made it more disturbing.

There was no lengthy explanation, no medical assessment, and no supporting report. Just a handwritten instruction connecting Isabella Vale's maternity record to the St Catherine's private nursery register.

And beneath it, Eleanor Vale's signature.

**DO NOT MERGE THE MATERNAL IDENTITY WITH THE RECEIVING INFANT RECORD.**

**THE TWO RECORDS MUST REMAIN SEPARATE UNTIL THE ORIGINATING REPRESENTATIVE CONFIRMS THE CHILD.**

The instruction had been issued on the twelfth day after the births.

Edward placed the document beside Isabella's original maternity identification sheet.

The two records had been connected.

Then someone had deliberately prevented them from becoming one record.

He called Jonathan.

"I need you to come in."

"Now?"

"Yes."

"Have you found something?"

"Something Eleanor signed herself."

Jonathan arrived twenty minutes later.

Edward showed him the correction.

Jonathan read it carefully.

"She knew about Isabella."

"Yes."

"She knew Isabella's child had been connected to Infant A."

"Yes."

"And she stopped the records from being merged."

Edward nodded.

Jonathan pulled out a chair.

"That could mean she discovered the connection was false."

"It could."

"Or she discovered it was true but didn't have authority to confirm it."

"Also possible."

Jonathan looked at the document again.

"Why would she need to keep the records separate?"

Edward turned towards the computer.

"Because the person who arranged the transfer had not established which child was which."

"Then why did Eleanor sign the correction?"

"Perhaps she was trying to prevent another mistake."

Jonathan considered that.

"She may have been protecting the children."

"Or protecting the arrangement."

Edward opened the historical register.

"There is one more thing."

He pointed to the correction's reference number.

**CORRECTION FILE: 12-D/EV-04.**

Jonathan frowned.

"EV-04?"

"Eleanor Vale."

"Where is the supporting file?"

"That's what I'm trying to find."

Edward searched the archive index.

The result appeared almost immediately.

**12-D/EV-04 — SUPPORTING MATERIALS**

**STATUS: SEALED**

**ACCESS RESTRICTION: ORIGINATING REPRESENTATIVE ONLY.**

Jonathan leaned forward.

"Who was the originating representative?"

Edward clicked the associated record.

The field was blank.

At the Aurelius Estate, Alexander received Edward's message during a meeting with Adrian.

He read the summary without speaking.

Adrian watched his expression.

"What is it?"

"Eleanor Vale issued a correction on the twelfth day."

"Regarding Isabella?"

"Yes."

"Did she identify Isabella's child as Infant A?"

"No. She explicitly prevented the records from being merged."

Adrian considered the implication.

"Then Eleanor believed the connection was unverified."

"Or she knew the connection was dangerous."

Alexander placed the report on the table.

"Have Edward request the sealed supporting file."

"He already has."

"Good."

Adrian opened another document.

"There is something else. We found a payment made to Eleanor's private legal account on the same day."

Alexander looked up.

"From whom?"

"Vale Private Counsel."

"Amount?"

"Not particularly large. Enough to cover a private legal review and medical records examination."

"Was it part of her regular compensation?"

"No. It was a separate payment."

Alexander read the transaction details.

The description was brief.

**IDENTITY DISCREPANCY REVIEW — TWO MATERNAL RECORDS.**

He looked at Adrian.

"She was investigating the children."

"Apparently."

"Who instructed her?"

"That's the problem. The payment was authorised through a private account. We haven't identified the person who requested it."

Alexander stood.

"Find the original instruction."

Isabella returned to her apartment with a copy of the maternity identification sheet.

She placed it on the dining table.

For years, she had imagined what it would feel like to receive an answer.

She had expected relief.

Perhaps anger.

Perhaps even grief.

Instead, the document had given her another question.

Her daughter had been connected to a child at St Catherine's.

But nobody could tell her whether that child was actually hers.

She took out the hospital bracelet.

The ink had faded, but the identification number remained visible.

**IV-204.**

She placed it beside the photograph.

**INFANT A — 2:17 A.M.**

The numbers did not match.

The times did not match.

And yet someone had deliberately connected them.

Isabella opened a drawer and removed a notebook.

She had begun keeping records years ago, initially because she was afraid of forgetting details.

Over time, it had become a personal archive.

She turned to an entry dated eleven years earlier.

*Received a telephone call from an unknown woman.*

*She said she had information about the child.*

*She refused to give her name.*

*She asked whether I still had the original bracelet.*

Isabella read the next line.

*I said yes.*

*She asked me not to show it to anyone.*

Beneath that was another sentence.

*She said the bracelet belonged to a different child.*

Isabella slowly closed the notebook.

She remembered the telephone call.

She remembered the woman's voice.

But she had never understood why someone would contact her after so many years only to tell her that the one remaining piece of identification might not belong to her daughter.

She picked up her telephone.

There was one person she could still ask.

Edward received the call shortly after seven.

"Mr. Valmont?"

"Yes."

"This is Isabella."

"Good evening."

"I remembered something."

Edward sat down.

"What is it?"

"Eleven years ago, someone called me. A woman."

"What did she say?"

"She asked whether I still had my daughter's hospital bracelet."

Edward reached for his notebook.

"What did you tell her?"

"That I did."

"Did she ask to see it?"

"No. She said it belonged to a different child."

Edward stopped writing.

"Did she identify herself?"

"No."

"Did she mention St Catherine's?"

"No."

"Did she mention Eleanor?"

"No."

"Did she say anything else?"

Isabella was quiet.

Then she answered.

"She said I had been given the wrong photograph."

Edward looked at the picture on his desk.

"Did she explain?"

"No. I asked her which photograph I should have received. She said she couldn't tell me."

"Why not?"

"She said she had already made one mistake and wasn't going to make another."

Edward sat very still.

"Did you recognise her voice?"

"No."

"Could you describe it?"

"Older than mine. Calm. She sounded tired."

"Did she give you a number?"

"No. The call came from a private line."

Edward looked at the twelfth-day correction.

"Isabella, would you be willing to meet again?"

"Yes."

"There's something I need to show you."

"What?"

"A document signed by Eleanor Vale."

Isabella took a breath.

"Then I think I should tell you something else."

Edward waited.

"I don't believe Eleanor was the person who first told me my daughter had died."

Edward's pen stopped.

"Who do you think was?"

"I don't know."

"Then why do you believe that?"

"Because I remember the person who brought me the letter."

She paused.

"It wasn't Charles Vale."

The following morning, Edward and Jonathan met Isabella at the legal office.

She brought the notebook and a written description of the visitor.

The visitor had been a woman in her forties, perhaps older.

She had worn a grey coat and carried a thin leather document case.

She had introduced herself as a representative of the medical facility.

She had delivered the letter informing Isabella that her daughter had died.

"Did she have any identification?" Edward asked.

"She showed me something. I was recovering. I didn't read it properly."

"Did she tell you the name of the medical facility?"

"She said she was working on behalf of the hospital."

"Do you remember her name?"

Isabella closed her eyes.

"Something beginning with M."

Edward looked at Jonathan.

"Margaret?"

"No," Isabella said immediately.

She opened her eyes.

"It was longer. Maybe Marianne. Or Marjorie."

Edward wrote both names down.

"Did she come alone?"

"No. There was a man waiting outside."

"Do you remember him?"

"He was wearing a dark suit. I didn't see his face clearly."

"Did he speak to you?"

"No."

Edward showed her a photograph of Charles Vale.

"Was this the man?"

Isabella studied it.

"No."

He showed her a photograph of Gabriel Vale.

She shook her head again.

"Not him."

Then Edward showed her a photograph of Eleanor Vale.

Isabella hesitated.

"I think I've seen her before."

"At the time of your delivery?"

"No. Later."

"When?"

"At the Vale office. She came to speak with Charles. I was working there then."

"Did you speak to her?"

"Briefly."

"What did she say?"

Isabella looked down at her hands.

"She asked whether I still believed my daughter was alive."

Edward leaned forward.

"And what did you say?"

"I told her I had no reason to believe otherwise."

"What did she say?"

Isabella's voice became quieter.

"She said that was the only answer I should trust."

The sealed supporting file was located that afternoon.

It contained seven pages.

Edward and Jonathan examined the documents under the supervision of the archive administrator.

The first page was a medical comparison request.

It listed three separate records:

- Isabella Vale's maternity admission.
- St Catherine's Infant A record.
- An unidentified private nursery receiving record.

The request asked for a comparison of the dates, identification numbers, and maternal references.

The second page contained the preliminary findings.

**NO CONFIRMED IDENTITY MATCH.**

The third page was more revealing.

It listed two possible explanations for the discrepancy.

**POSSIBILITY ONE:** The receiving infant record was incorrectly connected to Isabella Vale's maternal record.

**POSSIBILITY TWO:** The infant identification materials had been transferred between facilities without a corresponding maternal record amendment.

Neither possibility had been confirmed.

Jonathan looked at Edward.

"So even Eleanor didn't know."

"Not from this report."

The fourth page contained a handwritten note.

*The photograph supplied to the mother must not be treated as proof of identity.*

Edward recognised the handwriting.

It belonged to Eleanor.

The fifth page contained an instruction:

*No further infant movement should take place until the maternal records are independently verified.*

The sixth page was a list of people who had received copies of the preliminary findings.

Charles Vale.

Marcus Morelli.

Margaret Voss.

Gabriel Vale.

And one final name.

**ISABELLA VALE.**

Jonathan frowned.

"She received a copy?"

Edward checked the distribution date.

"Yes."

"Did she know?"

"I don't think so."

The seventh page was missing.

A note at the bottom of the file read:

**FINAL COMPARISON RESULT — DELIVERED TO RECIPIENT IN PERSON.**

There was no recipient name.

Edward returned to the Aurelius Estate that evening.

Victoria was waiting in the library.

She had asked to see the original correction herself.

Edward placed the documents on the table.

Victoria read them carefully.

"Isabella's child was never confirmed as Infant A."

"Correct."

"And Eleanor knew that."

"Yes."

"She also knew the photograph wasn't proof."

"Yes."

Victoria looked at him.

"Then why did she give it to Isabella?"

Edward considered the question.

"Perhaps she wanted Isabella to know that a child connected to her records might still be alive."

"That isn't the same as giving her hope."

"No."

Victoria returned to the first page.

"How many children were involved?"

"We still don't know."

"How many mothers?"

"At least three separate maternity records have now appeared."

Victoria looked up.

"Mine. Catherine's. And Isabella's."

"Yes."

She sat back.

"Then perhaps we've been investigating the wrong arrangement."

Edward waited.

Victoria continued.

"We have been assuming two infants were exchanged between two families. But if Isabella's child was also connected to the records, perhaps the arrangement involved more than two children."

Edward did not dismiss the possibility.

It was precisely the kind of question the investigation needed to consider.

But it could not become a conclusion without evidence.

"That is possible," he said. "We need the original medical records from all three facilities before we can establish how many infants were involved."

Victoria nodded.

"Then find them."

At 10:18 p.m., Jonathan called Edward.

"I found something."

Edward sat up.

"What?"

"The missing seventh page."

"Where?"

"Not in the sealed file. It was attached to the payment record."

Edward opened his computer.

Jonathan sent the scan.

It was a receipt for the final comparison result.

**DELIVERED PERSONALLY.**

**RECIPIENT: MARGARET VOSS.**

**DATE: TWENTY-EIGHTH DAY AFTER BIRTHS.**

Edward read the entry.

Then he noticed a second line.

**ORIGINAL IDENTIFICATION MATERIALS RETURNED TO RECIPIENT.**

There was a handwritten note beneath it.

*The bracelet does not belong to the photograph.*

Edward stared at the sentence.

The bracelet Isabella had kept for eighteen years.

The photograph Eleanor had given her.

The two items had never belonged together.

And Margaret Voss had known that from the twenty-eighth day.

Edward called Jonathan.

"Did you notice the reference number?"

"Yes."

"Which one?"

"IV-204."

Edward opened the original maternity record.

The bracelet's identification number was clearly recorded there.

But the photograph's reference number was different.

**SC-217-A.**

The photograph had come from St Catherine's.

The bracelet had come from Isabella's clinic.

Someone had deliberately placed them together.

And the person who had first done so had known that they belonged to two different records.

Edward looked at the date of the original photograph transfer.

It was the sixth day after the births.

He looked at the date of Eleanor's correction.

The twelfth day.

Then he looked at the date Margaret Voss received the final comparison report.

The twenty-eighth day.

Three separate dates.

Three separate decisions.

And one person who had known the truth long before either family began searching.

**Margaret Voss.**

But the final comparison report contained another handwritten sentence.

Edward enlarged it.

*The child identified by the bracelet has already been moved.*

Beneath it was a second line.

*The child identified by the photograph has not yet been located.*

Edward slowly lowered his hand.

For the first time, the investigation had established that the bracelet and photograph belonged to two different infants.

It had also established that one of those infants had been moved before the twenty-eighth day.

The other remained missing.

But the document did not say which child was which.

And Margaret Voss had known that distinction for eighteen years.
