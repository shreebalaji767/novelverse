# **Chapter 97 — The Family Already Informed**

The correspondence register was not where Edward expected it to be.

Vale Private Counsel's archive occupied three floors of a renovated commercial building, its older records stored in a separate preservation facility outside the city. The firm had changed ownership twice, merged with another legal practice, and reorganised its departments so thoroughly that several former employees described its filing system as a collection of historical accidents.

The reference **VC/NR-118/ACK** had survived the first search.

The document itself had not.

At nine forty-five the following morning, Edward received a call from the archive manager.

"We have a register entry," Rachel Morton said. "But the attachment is missing."

"Missing from the file or never received?"

"That distinction is exactly why I'm calling you."

Edward glanced at Jonathan, who had been sorting through the previous day's notes.

"Explain."

"The register records an acknowledgement received on day one hundred and eighteen. It gives the reference, the date, and the status. The attachment number appears in the index, but the corresponding document isn't in the box."

"Was it removed?"

"The transfer inventory says it was transferred."

"To where?"

"Unit Six."

Edward's hand tightened around the telephone.

Unit Six had already appeared in the investigation: the secure archive where Annex C and Schedule 4 had been stored under separate restrictions.

"Who signed the transfer?"

"Adrian Holt."

"Of course."

"There's another problem," Rachel continued. "The acknowledgement was indexed under correspondence, but the transfer inventory lists it under identity-preservation material."

"Those are different categories."

"Yes."

"Who changed the classification?"

"We don't have a name. The correction was made during a later archive consolidation."

Edward closed his eyes briefly.

"Can you send me the original index?"

"I can send the certified extract. The original register requires a formal request."

"Prepare it."

"I already have."

The call ended.

Jonathan looked up from his notebook.

"Unit Six again?"

"An acknowledgement was moved there. The attachment is missing."

"Could be the notification receipt."

"Could be."

Edward walked to the window.

The city below was beginning its ordinary morning. Cars moved through the intersection. A delivery worker unloaded boxes from a van. Someone crossed the street while reading a message on their phone.

It was difficult to reconcile that ordinary world with the records they were examining.

Somewhere in the past, a child had been moved from one institution to another. Someone had signed a form. Someone had entered a temporary name. Someone had recorded a notification as complete.

And eighteen years later, nobody could say with certainty which family had been told what.

"Get the request ready," Edward said. "We'll go to Unit Six as soon as access is approved."

Jonathan nodded.

Then his phone rang.

He answered, listened for several seconds, and looked toward Edward.

"It's Dr Hale."

Edward turned.

"Put him through."

The call was transferred.

"Edward," Samuel Hale said. "I received your message about the day-118 review."

"Did you maintain the correspondence register?"

"No. I was the medical verification authority. The correspondence register belonged to Vale Private Counsel."

"Did you know an acknowledgement was recorded on day one hundred and eighteen?"

"I knew a notification had been discussed."

"Discussed or delivered?"

"Those are not the same thing."

Edward glanced at Jonathan.

"That's becoming a familiar distinction."

"It should have been familiar to everyone involved at the time."

"Did you see the notification receipt?"

"I saw a summary."

"Who showed it to you?"

"Charles Vale."

"What did it say?"

Hale paused.

"It said that a proposed receiving arrangement had been acknowledged."

"By whom?"

"The summary did not name the person."

"Did it name the child?"

"No."

"Then what exactly had been acknowledged?"

"That was my objection."

Edward leaned against the desk.

"Tell me."

Hale's voice grew more deliberate.

"An acknowledgement can confirm that a document was received. It cannot confirm that the document's contents are correct. It cannot confirm that the recipient has authority to accept a child. And it cannot confirm biological identity."

"Did you tell Charles that?"

"Repeatedly."

"Did he listen?"

"He said the families needed certainty."

"Did they?"

"I don't know."

Edward looked at the note Eleanor had copied.

**If the receiving identity cannot be confirmed, notify the family already informed.**

"Dr Hale, did you ever see that instruction?"

"No."

"Would you have approved it?"

"Not without knowing who had been informed and what they had been told."

"Did you approve any notification on day one hundred and eighteen?"

"No."

The answer came without hesitation.

"Did you authorise the use of Schedule 4 to identify a receiving family?"

"No."

"Did you authorise a family allocation?"

"No."

Edward let the silence continue.

Hale finally spoke again.

"There's something I should tell you."

"What?"

"On day one hundred and eighteen, I was asked to confirm a medical reference."

"Which reference?"

"Child 28-B."

Edward straightened.

"Why?"

"The request said the child had been associated with a proposed receiving arrangement. I was asked whether the medical record could support the allocation."

"Could it?"

"No."

"Why not?"

"Because the medical record did not establish the child's originating identity. It only established that a child had been admitted under that temporary reference."

"Who made the request?"

"Charles."

"Did you refuse?"

"Yes."

"Was the refusal recorded?"

"I believe so."

"Where?"

"In the medical verification file."

Edward wrote it down.

"Did you tell Charles that Child 28-B could not be assigned to a family?"

"I told him the medical record could not establish the assignment. The family allocation was not within my authority."

"Did he tell you a family had already been notified?"

Hale's answer was delayed.

"Yes."

Edward looked at Jonathan.

"Did he identify the family?"

"No."

"Did you ask?"

"I did."

"And?"

"He said the notification was being handled through the appropriate office."

Edward's jaw tightened.

"Which office?"

"R-04."

The room seemed to become quieter.

"Dr Hale," Edward said, "you created the R-04 trust."

"I did."

"You knew it had no beneficiary."

"Yes."

"You knew the receiving identity custodian had never been appointed."

"Yes."

"Then why would Charles say a notification had been handled through R-04?"

"Because R-04 was a correspondence address. It was not a receiving family."

"Did you tell him that?"

"Of course."

"Did he understand?"

"I can't answer that."

Edward ended the call only after Hale promised to locate the medical verification file.

For several minutes, neither man spoke.

Then Jonathan said, "Child 28-B."

Edward nodded.

The temporary child reference had appeared repeatedly: Greenfield, Meridian, the residential review, the proposed name Elena, the day-103 transfer to Westbridge.

It had never been confirmed as Elena.

It had never been confirmed as Isabelle.

It had never been confirmed as either Aurelius infant.

And now, on day one hundred and eighteen, someone had asked Dr Hale to use its medical record to support a family allocation.

"Get the day-118 medical verification file," Edward said.

Jonathan began typing.

"And find out whether the notification and the Child 28-B request were part of the same sequence."

"Do you think they were?"

"I think someone wanted them to appear connected."

Edward returned to the table.

He placed Eleanor's review record beside the Northbridge schedule, the day-92 residential review, and the day-56 Schedule 4 revision.

The references did not align neatly.

That was the problem.

They had been arranged to look like a continuous chain, but each document had a different purpose. A medical reference. A temporary care record. A proposed allocation. A notification acknowledgement.

Someone had taken those separate pieces and presented them as though they established one conclusion.

Edward wrote a new heading in his notebook.

**DAY 118 — POSSIBLE MISUSE OF TEMPORARY CHILD REFERENCE**

Under it, he added:

**No confirmed identity. No confirmed receiving family.**

He underlined both statements.

At the Valmont Estate, Elena was trying to fit three weeks of academic preparation into a suitcase that had been designed for a much shorter trip.

The suitcase lay open on her bed.

Beside it were two stacks of clothes, a folder of programme documents, her laptop, a small notebook, and a collection of charging cables that had somehow multiplied overnight.

Nicholas stood in the doorway.

"Are you packing for Singapore or moving there permanently?"

Elena held up a sweater.

"Do you think I'll need this?"

"It's November."

"In Singapore."

"Then probably not."

She placed it on the bed anyway.

Nicholas looked at the growing pile.

"You know the programme provides accommodation."

"I know."

"And the university has a laundry service."

"I know."

"And you don't need to carry your entire wardrobe."

Elena gave him a suspicious look.

"Why are you so interested in my luggage?"

"Because I have discovered that you pack like someone preparing for an expedition across an uninhabited continent."

"I'm being practical."

"You're carrying four notebooks."

"They're different notebooks."

"One of them is empty."

"That doesn't mean it will remain empty."

He laughed.

She returned to the suitcase, then stopped.

"Actually, I need to check something."

"What?"

"The programme's expense policy."

Nicholas's expression became wary.

"Why?"

"Because the accommodation is covered, but meals and local transport aren't fully covered. I need to calculate what I can spend without using the emergency amount."

"You already made a budget."

"I made a preliminary budget."

"Which means you made a second budget."

Elena ignored him.

She opened her laptop and brought up the spreadsheet.

The programme had provided a modest allowance for academic materials. Travel expenses were partly reimbursed, subject to documentation. Personal spending remained her responsibility.

She had already separated the costs into categories.

Transport.

Meals.

Books.

Project materials.

Emergency reserve.

She had even added a column for expenses that might be reimbursed later.

Nicholas leaned over her shoulder.

"Your spreadsheet has a section for mistakes."

"Unexpected expenses."

"That's a very polite name for mistakes."

"It's also a useful name."

He studied the figures.

"You've left enough for emergencies."

"Yes."

"And you haven't included the money Adrian offered."

"No."

"Or the extra allowance Lucien suggested."

"No."

"Or the private travel arrangement Damien mentioned."

Elena closed the laptop halfway.

"Nicholas."

He raised both hands.

"I'm only checking."

"I know. But I want to do this myself."

His expression softened.

"Then do it yourself."

She looked at him.

"You're not upset?"

"Why would I be?"

"Because I keep refusing things."

Nicholas considered that.

"You're not refusing the family. You're deciding what you need."

Elena's shoulders relaxed.

"That's what I was trying to explain."

"I understood."

"Did you?"

"Eventually."

She smiled.

Nicholas picked up the empty notebook.

"What's this one for?"

"Project observations."

"Your digital records project?"

"Yes. I want to compare how different institutions handle document verification, consent, and correction histories. Not the private cases, obviously. Just public systems and the programme's own administrative structure."

Nicholas turned the notebook over.

"You've written the title already."

Elena took it from him.

"Because I know what I want to study."

"That's good."

She looked down at the notebook.

For a moment, the excitement of the programme felt larger than the suitcase, larger than the uncertainty of leaving home.

She had earned the place.

She had prepared the application.

She had presented her own idea.

Whatever happened with the family investigations, this opportunity belonged to her.

And she intended to make the most of it.

That afternoon, Isabelle sat in Catherine's foundation office with a research brief open in front of her.

The assignment was modest.

The foundation was reviewing its educational assistance programme, particularly the process through which applicants submitted documents and received decisions. Isabelle had been asked to study the public guidelines and identify places where applicants might misunderstand the requirements.

She had expected a simple task.

Instead, she had found three different versions of the application form.

One required a recommendation letter.

Another listed it as optional.

The third did not mention it at all.

Isabelle placed the documents side by side.

"Mother?"

Catherine looked up from her desk.

"Yes?"

"Who approved these?"

Catherine came over.

She read the forms carefully.

"These are from different years."

"Then why are they all available on the same page?"

Catherine's expression changed.

"That should not have happened."

"An applicant could submit the wrong form and still believe they followed the instructions."

"You're right."

Isabelle hesitated.

"I don't want to sound as though I'm criticising the foundation."

"Then don't criticise it. Explain the problem."

Catherine pulled a chair beside her.

"Tell me what you found."

Isabelle began describing the discrepancies.

She spoke more confidently as she went, pointing to the conflicting requirements, the missing dates, and the absence of a clear revision history.

Catherine listened without interrupting.

When Isabelle finished, her mother nodded.

"Prepare a short recommendation. We can ask the administrative team to review it."

"Will they know I wrote it?"

"Of course."

Isabelle looked down.

"I don't want them to accept it just because I'm your daughter."

"Then ask them to evaluate the recommendation on its merits."

Catherine smiled faintly.

"You don't have to pretend you aren't part of this family to prove that you deserve to contribute."

Isabelle absorbed the words.

She had spent so much time imagining what it meant to return to the Valmonts that she sometimes forgot the difficult part was not simply being accepted.

It was learning how to belong without becoming dependent on the family's approval for every decision.

She turned back to the brief.

"I'll finish it tomorrow."

"Take your time."

Isabelle nodded.

For once, the task before her had nothing to do with Elena, the missing children, or the family history.

It was something she could do herself.

And that mattered.

At Unit Six, the acknowledgement was waiting.

The archive custodian had located the transfer record and confirmed that the document had been placed in a restricted correspondence compartment.

Edward and Jonathan arrived with the required authorisation shortly before four.

The compartment was narrow, lined with grey archival boxes. Each item had been listed by reference, date, and preservation status.

The custodian removed a sealed folder.

**VC/NR-118/ACK**

The seal had been replaced during a later preservation review, but the original transfer label remained attached.

Edward examined the label.

"Transferred by Adrian Holt."

"Yes," the custodian said.

"Received by whom?"

"The receiving signature is incomplete."

Jonathan looked at the register.

"Again."

The custodian opened the folder.

Inside lay a single acknowledgement slip.

It was smaller than Edward had expected, printed on ordinary paper with a faint watermark. The upper section contained the reference number and date.

The lower section had been completed by hand.

**Document received: Schedule 4 — Proposed Family Allocation**

**Receiving party: Not recorded**

**Status: Acknowledgement of receipt only**

**Identity verification: Pending**

Edward read the final line.

"Who wrote this?"

The custodian checked the preservation notes.

"The handwriting was not attributed."

"Was the slip attached to the original schedule?"

"No. It was filed separately."

"Was it signed?"

"Yes."

Edward looked at the signature.

It was a single initial and surname.

**S. Bell**

Jonathan's pen stopped.

"Susan Bell?"

Edward did not answer immediately.

Susan Bell had appeared before: the residential review officer who refused to approve the permanent name for Child 28-B, who had retained the sealed envelope because the onward-delivery instructions were incomplete.

The acknowledgement did not prove she had accepted a child.

It did not prove she had notified a family.

It proved that she had received a document.

Edward turned the slip over.

On the reverse, a handwritten note had been added.

**I have received the proposed allocation. I have not accepted responsibility for the child.**

Beneath it:

**Please do not record this receipt as family confirmation.**

Edward stared at the words.

The acknowledgement had not been a family's acceptance.

It had been a refusal to let a receipt become one.

"Where is the original Schedule 4?" he asked.

The custodian checked the compartment inventory.

"Stored separately."

"Can we examine it?"

"Your authorisation covers the acknowledgement only."

Edward nodded.

"Then we'll request the schedule."

Jonathan photographed the slip under the archive's approved procedure.

As they prepared to leave, the custodian handed Edward a final page from the transfer inventory.

It listed the acknowledgement's attachments.

There had been two.

The first was the proposed allocation schedule.

The second was a notification receipt.

The second attachment had been marked:

**Returned without delivery confirmation.**

Edward stopped.

"Why wasn't this in the folder?"

The custodian checked the inventory again.

"It was transferred separately."

"To where?"

The entry contained a destination reference.

**R-04 / RECEIVING CORRESPONDENCE**

Edward felt the familiar chill of a pattern repeating.

R-04 had been a correspondence address.

A trust without a beneficiary.

A receiving office without a receiving family.

And now, an acknowledgement from Susan Bell that explicitly refused to accept responsibility for a child had been filed beside a notification receipt returned without delivery confirmation.

Someone had taken that incomplete chain and written that a family was already informed.

Edward looked down at the reference.

The next document would not necessarily tell them which child belonged to which family.

But it might finally tell them who had tried to make an unverified arrangement look complete.

And that person had left a paper trail.
