# **Chapter 157 — The Request That Was Refused Before It Was Read**

The day-133 request had been refused at 10:26 a.m.

The sealed personal response had arrived the previous afternoon.

The request sought permission to verify the sender's identity through the original C-19 channel.

Its purpose was recorded clearly:

*Establish whether the sender of the OC-19 personal response is the proposed verifier, the protected principal, or an authorised intermediary.*

Edward read the sentence again.

It was the first document that explicitly acknowledged three possibilities.

Until then, the records had treated the response as personal correspondence without establishing whose correspondence it was.

"Who refused it?" he asked.

Jonathan examined the refusal field.

"Personal Custody."

"Named officer?"

"None."

"Reason?"

"Proposed verifier's identity remains unconfirmed. No release authority established."

Edward looked at the date.

"Someone requested verification, and the request was rejected because verification had not already been completed."

Jonathan nodded.

"Another circular condition."

"Find the original request. I want to know who submitted it and what information they supplied."

The original request was kept in Meridian's restricted correspondence compartment.

It was sealed separately from the day-132 personal response.

Dr Mira Sen photographed the packaging before it was opened under the preservation order.

Inside was a two-page form.

The first page contained the formal request.

**REFERENCE:** OC-19 / ORIGINAL RESPONSE VERIFICATION

**SUBJECT:** Unverified personal correspondence received through C-19.

**REQUESTED ACTION:** Establish the identity and authority of the sender without disclosing protected principal information to the administrative nomination office.

**PROPOSED METHOD:** Independent confirmation through the original channel.

The second page contained the supporting information.

It listed the day-36 conditional acceptance, the day-92 procedural enquiry and the day-132 personal response.

The request did not claim that all three communications came from the same person.

Instead, it asked whether they could be linked through an independently verified source.

Edward looked at the signature field.

It was blank.

A small code appeared in the corner.

**OR-VER-06.**

"Is that an individual identifier?" he asked.

The archive officer checked the index.

"No. It identifies a restricted verification request."

"Who submitted it?"

"The submitting office is listed as Original Channel Review."

"Is that the same as the Original Channel Office?"

"No. It was a separate review function."

Edward turned to Jonathan.

"Find its authority schedule."

The authority schedule was stored in an older administrative archive.

It established that Original Channel Review could:

- Examine delivery records.
- Request confirmation of a protected correspondence address.
- Compare communication routing references.
- Determine whether a channel had been used consistently.
- Recommend an independent verification process.

It could not identify a protected principal without the required authority.

It could not appoint an OC-19 verifier.

It could not release the PR-06 key.

And it could not treat a sender's identity as established merely because several letters used the same address.

Edward read the final restriction.

"Then the request was within its authority."

"Yes," Jonathan said.

"It wasn't asking them to identify P-04. It was asking them to establish which role the sender occupied."

"That appears to be the distinction."

Edward looked at the refusal.

"Then why was it rejected?"

The refusal record contained a second page that had not been included in the migrated index.

It was a checklist.

**IDENTITY VERIFICATION REQUIREMENTS**

- Verified sender identity.
- Original procedure delivered.
- Verification key released.
- Independent channel verifier appointed.
- Receiving authority confirmed where applicable.

Four of the five requirements were incomplete.

The refusal had been generated automatically after the clerk marked the first field as unverified.

But a handwritten note appeared at the bottom.

*Do not proceed through the proposed verifier. Appointment remains suspended.*

Edward examined the handwriting.

Dr Sen compared it with the anonymous notes.

"The main instruction is consistent with the writer we've been tracking."

"Can you date it?"

"It was added after the checklist was completed. I cannot establish whether it was added before or after the refusal was formally issued."

Edward pointed to the sentence.

"Someone specifically prevented the request from being processed through the proposed verifier."

"Yes."

"Could that have been a legitimate restriction?"

"It could have been, if the verifier's appointment was genuinely suspended and no alternative authority existed."

"Was an alternative provided?"

"No."

"Was the request referred to another office?"

"No."

Edward closed the folder.

The refusal might have been procedurally defensible in isolation.

But it left the original question unanswered.

Margaret Voss examined the refusal checklist.

She read the requirements carefully.

"This is not a verification process," she said.

"What do you mean?"

"It lists the conditions that would be necessary after a verifier had been appointed. But it uses their absence as a reason not to appoint one."

"Could the review office have appointed an alternative?"

"Not under its authority."

"Could the originating representative?"

"Not without the required appointment procedure."

"Could the receiving authority?"

"No receiving authority had been appointed."

Edward looked at the form.

"So every office was waiting for another office to act."

Margaret nodded.

"And the protected person was left outside the process."

"Do you believe that was intentional?"

"I cannot say."

She placed the form down.

"But I can say the procedure had become incapable of completing itself."

Edward looked at the day-133 request.

"Did you know Original Channel Review had tried to verify the sender?"

"No."

"Did anyone inform you?"

"No."

"Did you request a copy?"

"I requested the status of the personal response. I was told it remained under restricted custody."

Margaret paused.

"I was never told that an attempt to identify the sender had been refused."

At Vale Private Counsel, Edward met Thomas Greer.

Greer examined the original request and refusal.

"Original Channel Review was allowed to request this," he said.

"Was it allowed to receive the key?"

"No."

"Could it appoint the verifier?"

"No."

"Then what could it do?"

"It could recommend a method of verification and refer the matter to the office with appointment authority."

"Was that referral made?"

Greer checked the supporting papers.

"No."

"Why not?"

"The refusal was recorded as final."

Edward looked at him.

"Who made that decision?"

"The record does not name anyone."

"Was the automatic refusal sufficient to close the request?"

"No. Not if the request was asking for an alternative route."

Greer studied the handwritten note.

"This instruction is problematic."

"Because it says not to proceed through the proposed verifier?"

"Because it does not say what should happen instead."

Edward waited.

Greer continued.

"If the proposed verifier could not be appointed, the office should have considered whether another independent verifier could be nominated. It could not simply leave the communication unverified indefinitely."

"Did you authorise the refusal?"

"No."

"Did Charles?"

"I have no evidence of that."

"Could he have issued an instruction through V-17?"

"He could have submitted a request or preserved documents there. That did not give him authority to control the appointment."

Edward closed the file.

Greer looked at him.

"You're trying to establish whether the sender was P-04."

"I'm trying to establish who sent the letter."

"Those are not necessarily the same question."

"I know."

Greer nodded.

"Then be careful. The correspondence may have been sent by someone who was not the protected principal but was trying to protect the principal's ability to respond."

Edward remembered the day-92 letter.

*I do not authorise any person to treat my continued silence as agreement.*

It was possible the sender had been speaking about their own participation.

It was also possible they had been defending someone else's rights.

Jonathan found the original review-office dispatch register that evening.

The day-133 request had been prepared by a staff member named Helen Ward.

Edward remembered her from earlier interviews.

She had worked in the administrative correspondence division and had preserved several routing records.

He contacted her.

Helen agreed to meet the following morning.

Helen arrived with a small notebook.

"I remember the request," she said.

"Did you write it?"

"I prepared the form, but I didn't author the verification proposal."

"Who did?"

"I don't know. It came to my desk as an instruction to refer the response for independent review."

"Did you know the sender?"

"No."

"Did you believe the sender was the OC-19 nominee?"

"I believed that was one possibility."

"Did you think it could be P-04?"

"That was another possibility."

"Why did you include both possibilities?"

"Because the response had been labelled as the nominee's personal response, but the sender had not been verified. I didn't want the review office to assume the label was correct."

Edward nodded.

"What happened after you submitted the request?"

"It was returned."

"Did you appeal?"

"I asked for the reason."

"Was one provided?"

"Only the standard refusal code."

"Did you know the request had been stopped by an anonymous instruction?"

Helen looked at the handwritten note.

"No. I never saw this page."

Edward showed her the second page of the refusal.

Helen read it.

"That is not the form I prepared."

"Was the checklist part of the original process?"

"It was attached later. My request was for an independent review of the channel, not an attempt to complete the appointment."

"Could you have proceeded without the key?"

"Not to identify the sender conclusively. But we could have checked the routing history, the delivery records, and whether the correspondence had been received through the same channel."

"Could that have helped?"

"Yes."

"Then why was the whole request refused?"

Helen shook her head.

"I don't know."

At the Aurelius Estate, Alexander received Edward's latest report.

He read the distinction between channel review and identity determination.

"Original Channel Review could have examined the route without establishing the principal's identity," he said.

"Yes," Victoria replied.

"And it was refused because the verifier had not been appointed."

"Yes."

Victoria looked at the report.

"Then the system did not merely fail to identify the sender. It refused an available step toward identifying them."

Alexander nodded.

"That is what the record suggests."

"Could the sender have been trying to protect someone else?"

"Possibly."

Victoria remained thoughtful.

"Someone who did not know whether their own correspondence would be treated as their decision."

Alexander closed the report.

"We cannot decide who that was."

"I know."

She looked toward the garden.

"But I hope the person is still able to speak for themselves."

Edward returned to the original day-133 request.

There was a small attachment that Helen had not remembered.

It was a copy of the C-19 delivery register.

The entry showed three communications.

**DAY 43:** Original procedure delivery attempted. No identity verification.

**DAY 92:** Request for procedural clarification received. No response issued.

**DAY 132:** Personal response received. Sender identity unverified.

A handwritten note connected the three entries.

*The sender may have been attempting to complete the same communication process throughout. Do not separate these records without establishing whether they share an origin.*

Edward examined the handwriting.

It was not the anonymous writer's.

Dr Sen compared it with Helen Ward's known notes.

"It is consistent with Helen's handwriting."

Edward looked at her.

"Helen wrote this?"

"Possibly. We should ask her."

Helen returned the next afternoon.

She examined the note.

"Yes," she said. "That looks like mine."

"Do you remember writing it?"

"I remember reviewing the three entries together. I must have added the note."

"Why?"

"Because the records were being treated as separate administrative events. I thought they might be connected."

"Did you believe they came from the same sender?"

"I thought that was possible, but I couldn't establish it."

"Did anyone ask you to separate them?"

Helen paused.

"Yes."

"Who?"

"Someone from Personal Custody."

"Did you record their name?"

"No. They said the three communications belonged to different administrative processes and should not be compared without authority."

"Did you agree?"

"No. I said the routing references could be compared without opening the personal response."

"Was that permitted?"

"Under the review office's authority, yes."

"What happened?"

"My access to the C-19 register was suspended."

Edward looked at her.

"When?"

"Day 134."

The day after the verification request had been refused.

"Did you appeal?"

"I submitted a request to restore access."

"Was it accepted?"

"No. The reason was that the original channel review remained incomplete."

Edward stared at the date.

The office had refused the verification request, then suspended the access of the employee who had tried to examine the channel records.

"Who ordered the suspension?"

Helen opened her notebook.

"I have the notice."

She placed it on the table.

The notice was signed by the Personal Custody supervisor.

But the approval reference was not a person's name.

It read:

**PC-04 / TEMPORARY CONTROL.**

Edward recognised the shared terminal code.

The same code had appeared on the day-61 transfer of the verification key.

"Was there a named supervisor?"

"No."

"Was there a supporting authority?"

"Only the reference."

Edward examined the notice.

A handwritten sentence appeared beneath the signature field.

*Access to the original channel register shall remain suspended until the proposed verifier's identity is confirmed.*

The handwriting matched the anonymous writer.

Dr Sen compared the day-134 notice with the earlier anonymous instructions.

The match was strong.

The day-48 instruction.

The day-118 hold.

The day-152 clarification.

The day-162 custody objection.

And now the day-134 access suspension.

The same writer had repeatedly restricted access to records concerning the original communication channel.

But the evidence still did not establish who the writer was.

Edward looked at the accumulated documents.

"Could this person have been trying to protect the nominee's identity?"

"Yes," Dr Sen replied.

"Could they have been preventing anyone from establishing it?"

"Also possible."

"Could both be true?"

Dr Sen considered the question.

"Someone might have believed that only one specific procedure could safely identify the person. But if they repeatedly prevented the procedure from being completed, the result would be continued concealment."

Edward nodded.

That was the difficulty.

The same instruction could be protective in one context and obstructive in another.

Intent could not be inferred from handwriting alone.

Later that evening, Jonathan brought Edward the final missing page from the day-133 request.

It had been stored with Helen's appeal rather than the original file.

The page was a handwritten recommendation.

*The original response should remain sealed. The channel history should be independently reviewed. If the sender is not the proposed verifier, the nomination record must not be amended on the basis of this response. If the sender is the proposed verifier, the sender must be given the original procedure before any further acknowledgement is requested.*

Edward read the recommendation carefully.

It did not assume who the sender was.

It did not recommend opening the envelope.

It proposed two possible outcomes, both dependent on independent verification.

"Was this written by Helen?" he asked.

Jonathan checked the handwriting comparison.

"Yes."

Edward looked at the page again.

Helen had understood the problem.

She had offered a way forward that did not require anyone to violate the sealed response.

Yet her request had been refused, her access suspended, and the channel history left unexamined.

He placed the recommendation beside the refusal.

The refusal had been issued under PC-04.

The key transfer had also been entered under PC-04.

The access suspension had been issued under PC-04.

But PC-04 was a shared code.

It identified an office and terminal, not the person who had made the decisions.

Edward closed the file.

"Find the access roster for PC-04 on days 61, 133 and 134."

Jonathan nodded.

"All three?"

"All three. I want to know who could use the terminal, who was physically present, and who had authority to approve the actions."

"That may not identify the writer."

"It may not. But it will tell us who had the opportunity."

Before midnight, Jonathan returned with a preliminary roster.

The terminal had been accessible to four people during the relevant period.

Charles Vale had administrative access.

Peter Ashford had temporary records access.

A third employee had technical access during migration.

The fourth was listed only as:

**EXTERNAL PERSONAL CUSTODY REPRESENTATIVE — IDENTITY RESTRICTED.**

Edward read the entry.

"Who was that?"

"The personnel schedule is sealed."

"Can we obtain it?"

"Yes, through the preservation authority."

Edward looked at the three names.

Charles had collected the control copy.

Peter had handled Eleanor's open account during the migration period.

The technical employee had access to the shared terminal.

And an unnamed external representative had been allowed into the same restricted system.

He closed the roster.

The evidence still did not identify the anonymous writer.

But it had finally narrowed the question.

The person who repeatedly stopped the original channel review had not necessarily been the person who created the administrative confusion.

They might have been trying to prevent an unauthorised person from accessing the protected communication.

Or they might have been using that protection to keep the process suspended.

Edward looked at the sealed response inventory.

The sender had asked for something.

The office had refused to verify who had asked.

And now the employee who tried to establish the sender's identity had lost access to the records.

The next step was no longer another examination of the sealed letter.

It was to discover who had been allowed to control the channel through which the letter was supposed to be understood.
