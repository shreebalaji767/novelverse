# **Chapter 36 — The Child Who Was Found on the Tenth Day**

Edward read the message again.

**The second child has been located. Do not tell either family.**

The photograph was slightly blurred, but the handwriting remained legible.

The paper appeared old. Its edges were uneven, and a faint horizontal crease ran through the middle. There was no letterhead, no official seal, and no signature.

Only the date.

The tenth day after the births.

Edward placed his phone on the desk and opened the photograph on his computer.

He enlarged it.

The handwriting was unfamiliar.

It was neither Charles Vale's nor Marcus Morelli's.

He compared it with the documents already collected from Eleanor Vale's estate.

No immediate match.

He compared it with the handwriting on the hospital instructions.

Again, no match.

The sender had deliberately provided only one page.

And whoever had sent it knew exactly which document would disturb the investigation.

Edward called Jonathan Pierce.

"Have you received anything?"

"No."

"I've just received a photograph of a document dated the tenth day after the births."

Pierce went silent.

"What does it say?"

Edward read the sentence aloud.

There was a pause.

Then Pierce asked, "Who sent it?"

"Unknown number. No accompanying message."

"Can you trace it?"

"I'm working on that."

"Don't forward it to the families yet."

Edward looked at the screen.

"I wasn't planning to."

"Good. We need to establish whether it's genuine first."

Edward agreed.

But before ending the call, he added something.

"Jonathan, we may have misunderstood the ninth-day release."

"How?"

"Perhaps the two children weren't simply separated and delivered to different guardians. Perhaps one of them wasn't ready to be delivered at all."

At 10:40 p.m., the Aurelius family received an urgent request for another meeting.

Alexander arrived in the family office first.

Adrian followed with a tablet.

Damien entered carrying a folder.

Victoria came from the library, where she had been reviewing the old photographs.

Lucien was the last of the adult brothers to arrive.

Julian came downstairs in slippers.

"Is this another emergency?"

"Sit down," Adrian said.

Julian looked at him.

"That means yes."

He took a chair.

Edward joined them through a secure video call.

He explained the anonymous message and displayed the photograph.

Victoria read the sentence.

Then she read it again.

"The second child had been located."

"That's what it says," Edward replied.

"Which child?"

"We don't know."

"Does it mean the child who remained at the hospital?"

"Possibly."

Alexander leaned forward.

"Who was supposed to locate her?"

"That is the next question."

Adrian studied the date.

"The tenth day. One day after the documented releases."

"Yes."

"Could this refer to the second infant being transferred from the hospital to another guardian?"

"It could. But the wording is unusual. It says the child has been located, not delivered."

Damien looked at the document.

"That suggests she was missing."

Edward nodded.

"Or that someone had lost track of her."

Victoria's expression tightened.

"She was a newborn. How could anyone lose track of a newborn?"

Nobody answered.

Because the question had been hanging over the investigation since the beginning.

Alexander asked Edward to display the ninth-day release records.

The two entries appeared side by side.

**INFANT A — RECEIVED BY AUTHORITY OF THE ORIGINATING REPRESENTATIVE.**

**INFANT B — RECEIVED FOR THE DESIGNATED FAMILY REPRESENTATIVE.**

The names had been removed.

"Margaret received Infant A," Alexander said.

"That is what the evidence suggests," Edward replied. "Her photograph shows a hospital band marked A. Her account is consistent with the acknowledgment."

"And Infant B?"

"Charles Vale physically received Infant B on behalf of an unnamed representative. We have not identified that representative."

Alexander looked at the anonymous message.

"Then what happened on the tenth day?"

"We don't know."

"Could the child mentioned in this message be Infant B?"

"Yes."

"Or Infant A?"

"Also possible."

Victoria looked at the two entries.

"Could one infant have been released on the ninth day and the other returned to the hospital?"

Edward paused.

"That is one possibility we need to examine."

Julian frowned.

"Returned?"

"Not necessarily returned physically," Adrian explained. "The child might have been taken somewhere temporarily, or the person who received her might have failed to complete the arrangement."

"Then why would the record say she was released?"

"Because the hospital's register records the physical departure, not necessarily the final custody arrangement."

Julian looked at the document again.

"So we don't even know whether the two babies went where they were supposed to go."

"No," Edward said.

"We don't know."

Victoria stood and walked toward the window.

For eighteen years, she had believed that her daughter had disappeared because someone had taken her.

Then the investigation had revealed an elaborate system of private arrangements, missing records, provisional placements, and people who had withheld information.

Now they were being told that a child had been located after the supposed final release.

She turned around.

"Edward, I want you to find the person who wrote this."

"We will."

"And I want to know whether the child was alive on the tenth day."

Edward met her eyes.

"We have no evidence suggesting otherwise."

Victoria nodded, though the answer did not comfort her.

Alexander looked at Adrian.

"Contact the hospital's successor administration. Find every record relating to the tenth day."

"I'll begin tonight."

"Also ask whether the private nursery had any post-discharge return entries."

Adrian nodded.

Damien added, "I'll check the transport and security logs from the old facility."

Lucien leaned back.

"And I'll look at the legal side. If the original arrangement was still provisional, someone must have had authority to change it."

Alexander looked at him.

"Someone had authority, or someone claimed to have it."

"That distinction may be the entire problem."

For once, nobody disagreed with Lucien.

At the Valmont estate, Edward had not yet told the family about the anonymous message.

He wanted to verify it first.

However, the investigation had already affected the household.

Richard had postponed two business appointments.

Catherine had reduced her foundation schedule.

Sebastian was reviewing old legal correspondence.

Nicholas had begun spending more time with Elena, partly because he worried that the uncertainty was making her feel increasingly isolated.

Isabelle, meanwhile, had started attending foundation meetings regularly.

That evening, Elena found her in the small study beside the conservatory.

Isabelle had several documents spread across the desk.

"Are you working?"

Isabelle looked up.

"Trying to."

"What is all this?"

"A proposal for a scholarship programme."

Elena came closer.

"Your mother's foundation?"

"Yes. They're reviewing how they distribute educational grants."

"That sounds interesting."

"It is. Also frustrating."

"Why?"

"Because everyone has a different idea of what students need."

Elena glanced at the papers.

The proposal included financial assistance, mentoring, internship opportunities, and support for students from smaller towns.

"You're trying to include all of this?"

"Yes."

"Wouldn't it be better to separate the grants from the mentoring programme?"

Isabelle looked at her.

"Why?"

"Because students who need financial assistance may not necessarily want a mentor assigned to them. And some students might need mentoring without qualifying for financial support."

Isabelle looked down at the proposal.

She read the relevant section again.

"That actually makes sense."

Elena smiled.

"You asked."

"I did."

Isabelle picked up a pen and began making changes.

For a few minutes, they worked quietly.

Then Isabelle spoke.

"Do you think you'll leave if you get selected?"

Elena looked up.

"For Singapore?"

"Yes."

"I don't know yet. I have to see the final offer, the expenses, the programme structure."

"You're already thinking about the expenses?"

"Of course."

Isabelle gave a small laugh.

"Most people would be celebrating."

"I am celebrating. I'm just also calculating."

"That's very you."

Elena smiled.

Then Isabelle's expression became thoughtful.

"I hope you get it."

"Thank you."

"And if you do, I hope you go."

Elena looked at her.

"You don't mind?"

"Why would I?"

Isabelle put down her pen.

"You shouldn't have to stay here just because things are complicated."

Elena studied her for a moment.

Then she nodded.

"Neither should you."

Isabelle returned to her proposal.

The conversation ended without either girl noticing how much had changed between them.

They were no longer discussing who belonged in which bedroom.

They were discussing what they wanted to do with their lives.

The following morning, Edward and Pierce visited the former St. Catherine's Hospital administration archive.

The building had been renovated twice.

The private maternity wing no longer existed in its original form. The nursery had been converted into a records storage area before the hospital was acquired by a larger medical group.

The archive manager greeted them in a small office.

"I've reviewed your request," she said. "The surviving records are incomplete."

"We understand," Pierce replied.

"Most patient records from that period were transferred to the successor institution. Some administrative registers were retained here."

Edward placed a copy of the anonymous document on the desk.

"We're looking for records from the tenth day after two female infants were born."

The manager examined the page.

"Is this an original?"

"We're verifying that."

She returned it.

"I can search the administrative registers. I cannot release patient information without the appropriate authorisation."

"We're not asking you to disclose unrelated patient details."

Edward handed her the legal documents.

She reviewed them.

"Give me some time."

They waited.

An hour later, she returned with a thin ledger.

It was not the original maternity register.

It was a departmental movement log.

The manager opened it to a marked page.

"These entries concern the private nursery."

Edward leaned closer.

The ninth-day entries were familiar.

Two infant departures.

Two separate times.

Two receiving representatives.

Then, on the tenth day, another entry appeared.

**10:35 A.M. — INFANT RETURNED TO MATERNITY OBSERVATION.**

There was no name.

Only a reference number.

Edward stared at it.

"Returned from where?"

"The register doesn't specify."

"Who authorised it?"

The manager pointed to a narrow column.

The name had been partially damaged.

Only three letters remained.

**M. M.**

Pierce looked at Edward.

"Morelli."

Edward nodded slowly.

The entry continued.

**STATUS: TEMPORARY.**

**RELEASE REQUIRES NEW AUTHORITY.**

Edward read it again.

"Was the infant examined?"

The manager searched the following pages.

There was another entry.

**11:10 A.M. — MEDICAL REVIEW COMPLETED.**

**CONDITION: STABLE.**

**TRANSFER NOT AUTHORISED.**

For the first time, they had a document showing that one infant had returned to the hospital after the ninth-day release.

But the reference number did not identify which infant.

Edward asked for the original supporting file.

The manager shook her head.

"That file isn't here."

"Where was it transferred?"

She examined the register's archival notation.

Then she looked up.

"Vale International Legal Group."

The discovery changed the direction of the investigation.

The ninth-day release was no longer the final event.

At least one child had returned to the hospital on the tenth day.

Someone had authorised a medical review.

Someone had refused a further transfer.

And the record carried Morelli's initials.

Edward called Morelli from the car.

This time, he answered.

"I found a tenth-day return entry," Edward said.

Silence.

"Infant returned to maternity observation at 10:35 a.m. Medical review completed. Stable. Transfer not authorised."

Morelli did not respond.

"Was it Infant A or Infant B?"

"I don't remember the reference number."

"Marcus."

"I said I don't remember."

"Your initials are in the authorisation column."

Morelli sighed.

"I was still at the hospital."

"Why?"

"Because the arrangement had not been completed."

"Which child returned?"

Morelli was quiet.

Edward continued.

"Was it the child Margaret received?"

"No."

The answer came too quickly.

Edward sat straighter.

"Then it was Infant B?"

"I didn't say that."

"You said it wasn't the child Margaret received."

"I said I didn't remember the reference number."

"Those are not the same thing."

Morelli's voice hardened.

"Edward, you are trying to turn a fragment of an old register into a complete history. It isn't one."

"Then give me the history."

"I cannot."

"Because you don't remember?"

"Because I made a promise."

Edward stared through the windshield.

"To whom?"

Morelli did not answer.

"Was it Eleanor?"

A long silence followed.

Then Morelli said, "No."

"Charles?"

"No."

"Then who?"

Morelli's reply was barely audible.

"To the person who came to collect the child on the tenth day."

Edward felt his stomach tighten.

"Someone collected the infant?"

"Yes."

"From the hospital?"

"Yes."

"Who?"

Morelli ended the call.

That evening, Alexander received the updated report.

He read the tenth-day entry in silence.

Victoria sat opposite him.

"One of the children returned to the hospital?"

"Yes."

"Was she ill?"

"The medical review says she was stable."

"Then why was she returned?"

"We don't know."

Victoria looked at the report.

"Was it our daughter?"

Alexander did not answer.

"Could it have been?"

"Yes."

She closed her eyes briefly.

"Then she was alive."

Alexander looked at her.

"Victoria."

"On the tenth day, she was alive. Someone examined her. Someone wrote that she was stable."

"Yes."

Victoria looked down.

For a moment, she could not speak.

Then she asked, "Did someone come to collect her?"

Alexander nodded.

"The register doesn't identify the person."

"Morelli knows."

"He refuses to say."

Victoria stood.

"Then we need to find the person who collected her."

Alexander remained seated.

"We will."

At the Vale International Legal Group archive, Jonathan Pierce received an unexpected call.

The caller introduced herself as a former records assistant.

Her name was **Helen Ward**.

She had worked in the legal office for less than a year, eighteen years earlier.

"I saw your request concerning the maternity case," she said.

"How did you know about it?"

"Because I was the one who filed the tenth-day documents."

Pierce immediately sat forward.

"Do you remember the case?"

"I remember the instructions."

"What instructions?"

"Mr. Vale told me that a child had returned to the hospital and that the file must be kept separate from the original nursery records."

"Did you see the child?"

"No."

"Did you see the person who collected her?"

There was a pause.

"Yes."

Pierce waited.

"Who was it?"

"I don't know her name."

"Can you describe her?"

"She was a woman. Middle-aged, perhaps. She arrived alone."

"Was she a hospital employee?"

"No."

"Did she show identification?"

"She showed a document to Mr. Vale."

"What kind of document?"

"I never saw it clearly."

Pierce picked up his pen.

"Did she sign anything?"

"Yes."

"Do you remember the signature?"

Helen hesitated.

"I remember the first name."

"What was it?"

She answered quietly.

"Victoria."

Pierce stopped writing.

"Victoria what?"

"I don't know."

"Are you certain?"

"I remember because Mr. Vale asked me to file the document under a different name."

"Which name?"

Helen took a breath.

"Margaret."

At the Aurelius residence, Victoria was standing in the family library when Edward's call arrived.

He explained the tenth-day entry.

Then he explained Helen Ward's testimony.

A woman had collected the infant.

She had signed the document with the first name Victoria.

And the papers had been filed under Margaret.

Victoria did not move.

"Edward," she said slowly, "I never collected a child from that hospital."

"I understand."

"I never went there on the tenth day."

"We haven't established that the woman was you."

"Then why did she use my name?"

Edward had no answer.

Alexander entered the library.

He heard the last sentence.

"Who used your name?"

Victoria turned toward him.

"Someone collected a child on the tenth day."

Alexander took the phone from her.

"Edward, did the witness identify the woman?"

"No. Only the first name on the document."

"Was the document retained?"

"Apparently. Helen Ward believes it was filed under Margaret."

Alexander looked at Victoria.

Then he spoke into the phone.

"Find that document."

"We're trying."

"No. Find the original."

He ended the call.

Victoria stood motionless.

For eighteen years, she had waited for a child who had never come home.

Now someone was claiming that a woman had collected a newborn using her name.

And that the legal office had deliberately filed the record under another identity.

She looked at Alexander.

"What if someone used my name to take our daughter?"

Alexander did not answer immediately.

Then he said, "We will find out."

At 11:28 p.m., Edward received one final message from Helen Ward.

It contained a photograph of a document she had kept among her personal papers.

The heading read:

**TENTH-DAY TEMPORARY RECEIVING ACKNOWLEDGMENT**

The infant's identity field had been left blank.

The receiving person's name was covered by a black rectangle.

But the signature remained visible.

**Victoria A.**

Beneath it, a handwritten instruction had been added.

**The receiving party is not to be informed of the child's original placement.**

Edward stared at the page.

The woman who had collected the infant had been deliberately kept unaware of where the child had been before the tenth day.

Then he noticed a second line.

It was written in a different hand.

**This is not the child assigned to her.**

Edward read the sentence twice.

Someone had collected a child who was not the one originally assigned to her.

And someone had made sure she never knew.
