# **Chapter 102 — The Envelope Margaret Voss Had Kept**

Margaret Voss did not remember the envelope as an important document.

She remembered it as an inconvenience.

That was how she described it when Edward placed the archive photograph in front of her.

“I was asked to keep something,” she said. “I kept it. That's all.”

Edward studied her expression.

“You deposited an envelope at Unit Six on day one hundred and thirty-three.”

“Yes.”

“Reference MV-18.”

Margaret adjusted her glasses.

“That sounds familiar.”

“You collected a refusal notice concerning Eleanor Vale's sealed instruction on the same day.”

“I did.”

“Why?”

“Because the request had been rejected.”

“That explains why you collected the notice. It doesn't explain why you deposited another envelope.”

Margaret looked at the photograph again.

“Did you open it?”

“No.”

“Did you know what it contained?”

“I knew what it was supposed to contain.”

Edward leaned forward.

“What was it supposed to contain?”

“An original correspondence record.”

“From whom?”

Margaret was silent for a moment.

Then she answered.

“Charles Vale.”

Jonathan looked up.

Edward did not move.

“Did Charles give it to you?”

“No.”

“Then how did you receive it?”

“It was sent to my office.”

“By whom?”

“I don't remember.”

“You kept it for eighteen years, but you don't remember who sent it?”

“I remember the envelope arriving. I don't remember the messenger.”

“Was there a covering letter?”

“No.”

“An address?”

“Yes. My office.”

“Was your name written on it?”

Margaret nodded.

“Did you know it was connected to the missing children?”

“I knew it concerned the restricted records.”

Edward glanced at Jonathan.

“Why didn't you submit it to the original investigation?”

Margaret's expression hardened.

“Because there was no proper investigation at the time. There were several people asking me to certify records I could not verify. There were people moving documents between offices. There were instructions to treat provisional arrangements as completed decisions.”

She paused.

“I refused to sign.”

“We know.”

“Then you know why I kept it.”

“I know you refused to certify the records. I don't know why you preserved this particular envelope.”

Margaret looked toward the window.

“Because I thought someone would eventually ask the right question.”

Edward waited.

“And what question would that be?”

She turned back.

“Who was supposed to receive the children?”

The room became quiet.

It was a question they had been circling for weeks.

Not who gave birth.

Not which hospital admitted which infant.

Not who collected a band or signed a transfer.

Who had been intended to receive the children, and who had the authority to make that decision?

Margaret continued.

“I knew the originating records were incomplete. I knew the receiving records were incomplete. But I also knew someone had prepared arrangements before the two sides had been reconciled.”

“Did you know who?”

“No.”

“Did you suspect Charles?”

“I suspected his office was involved.”

“Did you tell him?”

“Yes.”

“What did he say?”

Margaret's mouth tightened.

“He said I was making a simple administrative matter unnecessarily complicated.”

Edward almost smiled.

It was a familiar argument.

“Did he explain the proposed allocations?”

“No. He said the receiving families had already been identified.”

“Did you believe him?”

“No.”

“Why?”

“Because the receiving identity custodian had not been appointed. I had seen the appointment register myself.”

Edward looked at Jonathan.

Margaret had just confirmed something important.

The vacancy had not been an obscure technicality.

The people responsible for the records knew it existed.

And some of them had been told that the families had already been identified anyway.

The archive authorised a supervised examination of MV-18 that afternoon.

Margaret insisted on attending.

“I deposited it,” she said. “I want to see what happens to it.”

The envelope was brought into the preservation room.

Its outer paper had yellowed, but the original seal remained intact. The reference was written in dark blue ink.

**MV-18 — ORIGINAL CORRESPONDENCE**

There was no sender's name.

No institutional logo.

Only a small handwritten instruction beneath the reference:

**Retain separately from Annex C.**

Edward examined the envelope.

“Do you recognise the handwriting?”

Margaret leaned closer.

“No.”

“Not Charles's?”

“No.”

“Not Eleanor's?”

“No.”

“Not yours?”

“Definitely not mine.”

The archivist checked the preservation record.

“The envelope was deposited on day one hundred and thirty-three. The original seal was inspected during transfer and found intact.”

“Was it stored with Eleanor's instruction?” Edward asked.

“Initially, yes. Both were held in the same restricted compartment. They were separated during a later inventory review, but their cross-reference remained.”

Jonathan read the inventory note.

“Why were they stored together?”

“Both carried instructions concerning the receiving identity process.”

Edward looked at the two sealed envelopes.

One had been collected by Eleanor.

The other had been deposited by Margaret.

Two women who had refused to certify the same incomplete process.

Two envelopes preserved separately.

Neither had been opened.

“Can we examine the outside for impressions?” Edward asked.

The archivist nodded.

Dr Mira Sen was contacted again.

She arrived later that afternoon with her equipment.

She first examined the outer seal, then the envelope's surface.

After several minutes, she pointed to a faint indentation near the lower edge.

“There is writing beneath the paper.”

“Can you recover it?” Edward asked.

“I can attempt to enhance the impression. I cannot guarantee a complete reading.”

She adjusted the lighting.

A few words emerged.

**...not the receiving representative.**

Then another line.

**...do not attach to Child 28-B.**

Edward straightened.

Margaret looked at him.

“Child 28-B?”

“Yes.”

The examiner continued.

The impression was incomplete, but the final line was clearer.

**Original recipient to be established independently.**

Edward stared at the words.

“Can you tell whether the impression came from a document inside the envelope?”

“Not conclusively. It could be from an attached sheet or another document placed against it before sealing.”

“Can you recover more?”

“I'll need to process the images.”

Edward nodded.

The envelope was returned to its protective sleeve.

Margaret remained beside the table.

“Child 28-B,” she repeated.

Edward looked at her.

“Do you know what that reference means?”

“I know what it meant at the residential review. It was a temporary child reference.”

“Was it the child you were responsible for monitoring?”

Margaret's face tightened.

“I was responsible for monitoring the record, not the child.”

“Did you ever see the child?”

“I saw a child in the residential review file. I did not have physical custody.”

“Did you know why the name Elena had been proposed?”

“No.”

“Did you know whether the child was connected to the Valmont family?”

“No.”

“Or the Aurelius family?”

“No.”

She looked at the sealed envelope.

“But I remember one thing.”

Edward waited.

“Charles was very interested in making sure that Child 28-B was not treated as an Aurelius child.”

Edward's eyes narrowed.

“Did he say that directly?”

“He said the proposed arrangement had no connection to the Aurelius Family Office.”

“When?”

“During the residential review period.”

“Did he explain why?”

“No.”

“Did he say the child belonged to the Valmonts?”

“No.”

Margaret shook her head.

“He only said the child was not to be recorded as an Aurelius child.”

Edward wrote the statement down.

It was important.

But it was not proof.

Someone had repeatedly tried to separate Child 28-B from the Aurelius family, while no reliable record established who the child actually was.

The proposed name Elena had appeared on a temporary residential record.

That did not establish the child's identity.

It did not establish a biological connection to either family.

And it certainly did not establish that the child was the young woman now living at the Valmont Estate.

At the Valmont Estate, Elena was reviewing her final programme schedule.

Her departure date was approaching.

The programme office had sent a welcome packet with information about orientation, student registration, accommodation access, and the first academic workshops.

She had printed the schedule and placed it beside her laptop.

Catherine entered with a small tray.

“You've been working for hours.”

Elena looked up.

“I was checking the programme timetable.”

“Have you eaten?”

“Not yet.”

Catherine placed a plate beside her.

“Then take a break.”

Elena smiled.

“Thank you.”

Catherine looked at the printed schedule.

“Your first workshop is about institutional innovation?”

“Yes. And there's a session on digital administration systems.”

“That sounds interesting.”

“It is. I want to understand how large institutions manage records across different departments.”

Catherine sat down.

“Do you think you'll enjoy the programme?”

“I think so.”

“You sound uncertain.”

Elena considered the question.

“I'm excited. But it's still a new place. New people, new routine, new expectations.”

“That's normal.”

“I don't want to spend the entire programme worrying about whether I'm doing everything correctly.”

Catherine smiled.

“You won't.”

“How do you know?”

“Because you usually learn quickly.”

Elena laughed.

“That's a very confident assessment.”

“It's an accurate one.”

Catherine looked at the schedule again.

“Have you decided what you'll do after the programme?”

“Not completely.”

“University applications?”

“Yes. I want to compare a few courses before deciding.”

“Business?”

“Possibly. Or something related to public administration and technology.”

Catherine nodded.

“Whatever you choose, take the time to understand it.”

Elena looked at her mother.

For a moment, she almost said something about how strange it was that their relationship had become more complicated since Isabelle returned.

Instead, she asked, “Will you miss me?”

Catherine's expression softened.

“Of course.”

“Then why aren't you trying to convince me to stay?”

“Because loving someone doesn't mean keeping them in the same place.”

Elena looked down.

Catherine gently touched her shoulder.

“You worked hard for this opportunity. Go and enjoy it.”

Elena nodded.

“I will.”

Isabelle was in the foundation office when Nicholas delivered a message from Elena.

“She wants to see your research outline,” he said.

Isabelle looked up.

“Really?”

“Yes.”

She glanced at the papers on her desk.

“I haven't finished it.”

“She didn't say it had to be finished.”

Isabelle hesitated.

“Does she want to see it because she's interested, or because she thinks she should be supportive?”

Nicholas considered the question.

“Why don't you ask her?”

Isabelle gave him a small, embarrassed smile.

“That's fair.”

She picked up her phone.

**Isabelle:** Elena said you wanted to see the outline?

A reply arrived a few minutes later.

**Elena:** Yes! I'd like to read it. I'm interested in what you found about the application process.

**Isabelle:** It's still rough.

**Elena:** Mine usually are at this stage too.

Isabelle looked at the message.

Then she sent the file.

She did not feel suddenly close to Elena.

She did not know whether they would ever become the kind of sisters who shared everything.

But for the moment, there was something they could discuss without returning to the past.

That was enough.

Edward received Dr Sen's preliminary report the following morning.

The enhanced impression from MV-18 contained more text than they had initially recovered.

The first line was incomplete.

**The person holding this record is not the receiving representative.**

The second was clearer.

**Child 28-B must not be assigned by reference to the proposed name or temporary placement.**

The third line read:

**Original recipient to be established independently from the originating family record.**

At the bottom, a final instruction appeared.

**Do not allow the receiving identity to be inferred from the person holding the record.**

Edward read it slowly.

Jonathan leaned over his shoulder.

“That's almost exactly what Eleanor has been saying.”

“Yes.”

“Could she have written it?”

“We don't know.”

“Could Margaret?”

“Possibly.”

“Could Charles?”

Edward looked at the report.

“Possibly.”

The wording did not identify its author.

But it established something they had not understood before.

The person holding a child's record was not necessarily the person meant to receive the child.

The person collecting an identification band was not necessarily the child's parent.

The person accepting a sealed package was not necessarily the receiving family.

And the person assigned to monitor a record was not necessarily responsible for the child.

Every one of those distinctions had been violated or misunderstood.

“There's something else,” Jonathan said.

He pointed to the final section of the report.

Dr Sen had recovered a faint reference beneath the envelope's original label.

**OR-28 / SEPARATE RECIPIENT REVIEW**

Edward frowned.

“OR-28?”

“Could be an originating record.”

“Or an office reference.”

“Do we have anything matching it?”

Edward searched the investigation index.

Nothing.

Then he noticed a small handwritten note beside the reference.

**For review only after the originating representative has been identified.**

Edward looked at Jonathan.

“Find every occurrence of OR-28.”

Jonathan began searching.

The first result came from a restricted correspondence index.

The second came from an old payment ledger.

The third came from a day-34 archive inventory.

And the fourth came from a document they had already examined.

Annex C.

A small entry had been overlooked in the original schedule.

**OR-28 — Separate recipient review. Not part of maternal comparison.**

Edward sat back.

They had been treating Annex C as two parts: originating medical records and receiving identity records.

But OR-28 appeared to be a separate review altogether.

It was not a third maternal identity.

It was not a child reference.

It was not a receiving family.

It was a review that had been deliberately kept outside the main comparison process.

“Who was responsible for OR-28?” Jonathan asked.

Edward examined the entry.

The authority field contained two initials.

**E.V.**

Eleanor Vale.

But beside her initials was another designation.

**Independent reviewer — pending appointment.**

Edward read the line again.

Eleanor had been assigned responsibility for preserving the review.

She had not been appointed to conduct it.

And someone had expected an independent reviewer to examine the original recipient records before any family allocation could be confirmed.

The appointment field was blank.

Edward closed the file.

The investigation had found another missing authority.

But this time, it was not the receiving identity custodian.

It was the independent reviewer who had been expected to determine whether the proposed recipient records could be trusted at all.

And the review had never been completed.
