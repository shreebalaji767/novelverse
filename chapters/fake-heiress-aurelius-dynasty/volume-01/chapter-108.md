# **Chapter 108 — The Record That Was Not Supposed to Have a Copy**

Edward read the instruction again.

**R-04 / RECIPIENT STATUS — DO NOT DUPLICATE.**

It was written beneath the revised OR-28 Status Continuity Request, in faint type that had almost disappeared into the paper.

He placed the document beside the original draft.

The difference between the two versions was no longer a matter of wording.

The original draft preserved the unfinished review.

The revised version closed the administrative process while leaving the impression that no further independent review was necessary.

And somewhere between those two versions, someone had decided that the recipient status held at R-04 should not appear in the final record.

“Daniel,” Edward said, “what exactly were you told not to duplicate?”

Daniel Mercer sat opposite him in the conference room.

He had already answered the question once.

Now he looked at the paper for a long moment before speaking.

“The recipient-status entry.”

“Which entry?”

“The one held by R-04.”

“Was it a name?”

“No.”

“An appointment?”

“No.”

“Then what?”

Daniel rubbed his forehead.

“It was a status.”

Edward waited.

Daniel finally explained.

“R-04 maintained a separate record of whether a proposed receiving representative had acknowledged an allocation. It wasn't supposed to contain the full identity file. It only recorded the procedural position.”

“Such as?”

“Whether the representative had been notified. Whether they had accepted responsibility. Whether the receiving authority had been verified. Whether the originating identity had been confirmed.”

Edward glanced at Jonathan.

Those were precisely the conditions that had remained unresolved.

“And you were instructed not to copy those details into the Northbridge record?”

“Not in full.”

“Who gave the instruction?”

“Charles Vale's office.”

“Charles himself?”

“I can't say that. The instruction came through the review desk.”

“Written?”

Daniel nodded.

“There was a routing note attached to the request. I remember the wording because it seemed unnecessarily specific.”

Edward leaned forward.

“Can you reproduce it?”

Daniel hesitated.

“I can try.”

Jonathan opened his notebook.

Daniel spoke slowly.

“R-04 retains restricted recipient acknowledgements. Final administrative record to reflect approved task status only. Do not reproduce recipient-level qualifications in the distribution copy.”

Jonathan stopped writing.

“Recipient-level qualifications,” he repeated.

“Yes.”

“Meaning the refusal by Susan Bell?”

“Possibly. I didn't have her name in front of me.”

“Meaning the fact that no receiving representative had been appointed?”

Daniel looked down.

“That would have been included.”

“And the fact that the independent review remained incomplete?”

Daniel did not answer immediately.

“I don't remember whether that was classified as recipient status or review status.”

Edward looked at the two versions again.

“That distinction mattered.”

“It did.”

“Because one office could record the administrative task as closed while another continued to record that the identity process was unfinished.”

Daniel nodded reluctantly.

“That was how I understood it.”

Edward's expression hardened.

“And you didn't think anyone might use the first record without consulting the second?”

“I thought the two systems were maintained for different purposes.”

“That isn't an answer.”

“No,” Daniel admitted. “It isn't.”

For several seconds, nobody spoke.

Then Daniel added something.

“There was another instruction.”

Edward looked up.

“What instruction?”

“Not to destroy the original recipient-status material.”

Jonathan's pen paused.

Daniel continued.

“The wording said it had to remain in restricted custody. We weren't permitted to amend it, replace it, or distribute it through the ordinary family-allocation system.”

Edward stared at him.

“So the original was to be preserved.”

“Yes.”

“But its contents were not to be duplicated.”

“Yes.”

“And the ordinary system would show only the administrative closure.”

Daniel nodded.

Edward slowly leaned back.

Someone had created two records that could be read independently.

One said the administrative procedure had ended.

The other said the receiving authority had never been appointed.

Neither document was necessarily false in isolation.

Together, they revealed a contradiction.

And the people who needed to understand that contradiction had not necessarily been given both records.

Jonathan spent the next morning examining the distribution history of the revised OR-28 request.

The document had passed through four administrative points.

First, the review desk.

Second, the Northbridge family-allocation department.

Third, the Vale Private Counsel administrative archive.

Fourth, the restricted correspondence channel associated with R-04.

The fourth entry was unusual.

It did not record a complete copy being delivered.

Instead, it stated:

**REFERENCE ONLY — ORIGINAL RETAINED AT R-04.**

Below that, another line appeared.

**NO DUPLICATE RECIPIENT SCHEDULE ATTACHED.**

Jonathan placed the printout on Edward's desk.

“Someone deliberately prevented the R-04 schedule from travelling with the revised status request.”

Edward examined the page.

“Who authorised the distribution?”

“The departmental routing record lists the FA-ADMIN account.”

“An individual?”

“No. Shared departmental access.”

“Any operator identification?”

“Not on the surviving copy.”

Edward pointed to the fourth entry.

“What does reference only mean?”

“Possibly that the receiving department was informed that a restricted record existed without being given its contents.”

“Which department?”

Jonathan turned the page.

“The Family Allocation Records Office.”

Edward read the name twice.

“Not the Family Office?”

“No. It's an internal administrative department that maintained proposed placements and temporary care records.”

“Who worked there?”

“Several people. Some are still employed elsewhere. Others left years ago.”

“Can we establish who received this reference?”

“I'm trying.”

Edward returned his attention to the document.

The wording was carefully chosen.

The ordinary department knew that R-04 held something relevant.

It did not receive the underlying recipient information.

That meant the restricted register could remain accurate while the administrative system moved forward without showing why the proposed arrangement had never been accepted.

It was an arrangement that depended on separation.

Not necessarily secrecy from everyone.

But secrecy from the people reading only one part of the process.

“Find out who requested the reference-only distribution,” Edward said.

Jonathan nodded.

“And get the original routing instruction, not another summary.”

At Unit Six, the investigation had reached another obstacle.

The archive supervisor refused to release the original R-04 distribution register without a formal review of its custody restrictions.

Edward did not argue.

He requested an inspection under the existing preservation authority.

The supervisor returned the following afternoon with a sealed archival container.

“Everything inside has been preserved as received,” she said. “No document has been removed.”

Edward signed the inspection register.

The container was opened in the presence of two witnesses.

Inside were three folders.

The first contained correspondence between R-04 and Vale Private Counsel.

The second contained the restricted recipient acknowledgements.

The third was marked:

**DISTRIBUTION CONTROL — OR-28 / SCHEDULE 4.**

Edward opened the third folder.

The first pages were ordinary administrative records.

Requests for copies.

Acknowledgements of receipt.

Corrections to reference numbers.

Then he found a distribution table.

| Document                      | Permitted distribution         |
| ----------------------------- | ------------------------------ |
| OR-28 review status           | Administrative summary         |
| Schedule 4                    | Preservation extract only      |
| Annex C Part One              | Medical verification authority |
| Annex C Part Two              | Restricted recipient custody   |
| R-04 acknowledgement register | Reference only                 |
| Recipient identity schedule   | No distribution                |

Edward stopped at the last line.

“No distribution,” Jonathan read.

“Not even to the Family Allocation Records Office?”

The archive supervisor checked the next page.

“No.”

“Then how was Schedule 4 used to notify a proposed receiving family?”

“I can't determine that from this register.”

Edward continued reading.

A handwritten note appeared beneath the distribution table.

**The existence of a recipient schedule does not establish that the proposed recipient has been verified or appointed.**

The initials were not visible.

The note had been folded against another page, leaving only a partial impression.

Edward turned the sheet over.

There was a faint reference.

**E.V. — REVIEWED DAY 61.**

Eleanor Vale.

He read the note again.

Eleanor had reviewed the distribution controls on day 61.

That was the same day Annex C Part One, Part Two, and Schedule 4 had been received at Unit Six.

She had not merely checked the seals.

She had examined the restrictions governing which documents could be shared.

And she had specifically recorded that a recipient schedule could exist without a verified recipient.

“Was this note added before or after the distribution table was completed?” Edward asked.

The archive supervisor examined the paper.

“Based on the ink and attachment marks, probably afterward. But I would need a formal examination to establish the sequence.”

“Request one.”

Jonathan photographed the page.

Edward looked at the distribution table once more.

The recipient identity schedule had never been authorised for distribution.

Yet someone had prepared a proposed allocation, used it to notify a family, and later altered the notification record to make the attempt appear complete.

The contradiction was becoming harder to dismiss as an ordinary administrative mistake.

But it still did not tell him who had received the proposed allocation.

Nor did it establish which child the schedule concerned.

At the Valmont Estate, Elena had reached the final stage of her travel preparations.

Her suitcase stood open on the bed.

One side contained clothes.

The other contained books, documents, and the folders she had prepared for her academic programme.

She had also placed a small notebook in the front compartment.

It contained her estimated monthly expenses, emergency contacts, transport information, and a list of things she wanted to complete before the first day of classes.

Nicholas stood in the doorway holding a paper bag.

“You're packing as if you're moving to another country.”

Elena looked at him.

“I am moving to another country.”

“For a programme.”

“For several months.”

“Still sounds unnecessarily dramatic.”

She returned to folding a shirt.

“Then why did you bring another bag?”

Nicholas raised it.

“Because I know you.”

“I already have enough bags.”

“This isn't luggage.”

He placed it on the desk.

Elena opened it.

Inside were several notebooks, a compact desk lamp, and a small portable document organiser.

She examined the items.

“These are useful.”

“I know.”

“You didn't buy anything ridiculous?”

“I considered it.”

“That isn't reassuring.”

Nicholas grinned.

“Sebastian stopped me.”

Elena looked toward the door.

“That's probably why I should thank him.”

“I'm standing right here.”

“You're the one who needs supervision.”

Nicholas laughed.

For a moment, the room felt almost like it had before the DNA test, before the questions about who belonged to which family had become unavoidable.

But the familiarity was no longer effortless.

Both of them knew it.

Nicholas leaned against the desk.

“Have you checked your accommodation confirmation?”

“Twice.”

“Travel documents?”

“Twice.”

“Emergency funds?”

“Three times.”

“Insurance?”

“Twice.”

“Then what are you worried about?”

Elena considered the question.

“Nothing specific.”

“That's suspicious.”

She smiled faintly.

“I think I'm just aware that I'll have to manage everything myself.”

“You've been doing that for a while.”

“Not entirely.”

Nicholas did not argue.

He looked at the open suitcase.

“Do you want someone to accompany you to the airport?”

Elena paused.

“Would that be inconvenient?”

“No.”

“Then perhaps you can come.”

Nicholas nodded.

“Of course.”

She returned to her packing.

After a moment, she added, “But don't bring the entire family.”

“I wasn't planning to.”

“You were absolutely planning to.”

“I was considering a small delegation.”

“That's still a delegation.”

He laughed again.

Then he picked up the empty paper bag.

At the door, he stopped.

“Elena?”

She looked up.

“I'm glad you're going.”

She blinked.

“Really?”

“Yes. You wanted this. You worked for it. You should go.”

His voice was unusually serious.

Elena looked at him for a moment.

“Thank you.”

Nicholas nodded and left her to finish packing.

She returned to the suitcase.

There was still a great deal she did not know about her past.

But for the first time in months, the next stage of her life had a clear destination.

And she intended to reach it.

Isabelle was in the foundation office when Catherine entered.

Several folders were spread across the desk.

Isabelle had been reviewing a proposed education-support programme, comparing its projected expenses with the foundation's previous annual reports.

Catherine placed a cup of tea beside her.

“You're still working?”

“I wanted to finish the comparison.”

“It's nearly seven.”

“I know.”

Catherine glanced at the papers.

“You found the discrepancy?”

“Not exactly a discrepancy. The projected administrative costs are lower than last year's, but the number of applications has increased.”

“Which means?”

“Either the programme has become more efficient, or some costs have been moved into another department.”

Catherine smiled.

“That is a sensible question.”

“I don't want to approve a budget just because the total looks attractive.”

“Good.”

Isabelle looked down at the report.

“I've been thinking about the university applications.”

“Have you decided which courses interest you most?”

“Economics and public policy. Possibly business administration later.”

“You don't have to decide everything immediately.”

“I know.”

Catherine sat opposite her.

“Are you worried about Elena leaving?”

Isabelle's expression changed slightly.

“Not exactly.”

“Then what is it?”

Isabelle hesitated.

“I think everyone is treating it as a celebration. And it is. She worked hard for the place.”

“But?”

“But once she leaves, things will be different again.”

Catherine did not interrupt.

Isabelle closed one folder.

“I don't want her to think I'm relieved she's going.”

“Do you feel relieved?”

“No.”

“Then tell her that.”

Isabelle gave a small, uncertain laugh.

“It's not always that simple.”

“No,” Catherine agreed. “It isn't.”

She reached across the desk and straightened a page that had slipped out of the folder.

“You and Elena do not have to become close overnight. You don't have to pretend nothing happened. But you can still choose how you treat each other now.”

Isabelle looked at the report.

“I asked her to review my foundation research outline.”

“And?”

“She found three things I had overlooked.”

Catherine smiled.

“That sounds like Elena.”

“It was annoying.”

“I'm sure.”

Isabelle's expression softened.

“But she was right.”

Catherine did not push the conversation further.

She simply stayed with her daughter while Isabelle returned to the report.

The next morning, Jonathan received a call from a former employee of the Family Allocation Records Office.

Her name was Helen Ward.

She had worked in the department during the period when OR-28 and Schedule 4 were being processed.

She agreed to meet them.

The meeting took place in a small records consultancy office.

Helen arrived carrying a thin folder.

“I don't have much,” she said immediately. “Most of the old records were transferred.”

“We're looking for the distribution of the recipient schedule,” Edward replied.

Helen nodded.

“I remember the restriction.”

“Who imposed it?”

“I'm not sure.”

“Was it a legal instruction?”

“It was presented as one.”

“By whom?”

“Vale Private Counsel.”

Edward opened his notebook.

“Did the Family Allocation Records Office receive the recipient schedule?”

“No.”

“Did it receive a copy?”

“No.”

“Did it receive a summary?”

Helen considered the question.

“Not initially.”

“What happened later?”

She opened her folder.

“I kept a personal index of restricted references. It wasn't a copy of the documents. It was a list of incoming and outgoing file numbers.”

She placed a page on the table.

One entry was underlined.

**OR-28 / RS-04 — RECIPIENT STATUS REFERENCE RECEIVED.**

The date was day 56.

Edward looked up.

“Who received it?”

Helen checked the margin.

“Family Allocation Records Office. Desk 3.”

“An individual's name?”

“No. Just the department.”

“Was there an attachment?”

Helen shook her head.

“Not according to the index.”

“Then why did you keep this?”

“Because the reference was unusual. We normally received a document or a summary. This one specifically stated that the underlying recipient record was not to be requested through ordinary distribution.”

Edward studied the entry.

“Did you know what it meant?”

“No. But I remember the discussion.”

“What discussion?”

Helen looked uncomfortable.

“Someone asked whether the proposed family allocation could be processed without the recipient schedule.”

Edward remained still.

“What was the answer?”

“That the current care arrangement could be recorded, but permanent allocation could not be completed.”

“Who said that?”

“I think it was a senior records officer. I can't remember the name.”

“Was the instruction followed?”

Helen looked at her index.

“Partly.”

“What does that mean?”

“The temporary care arrangement was entered. The permanent allocation field remained incomplete in our system.”

Edward frowned.

“But another version of Schedule 4 was used to notify a family.”

“That may have happened outside our department.”

“Were you aware of it?”

“Only afterward.”

“What did you hear?”

Helen looked at Jonathan.

“Someone said a receiving representative had already been informed.”

“Who?”

“I don't know.”

“Was the representative named?”

“No.”

Edward leaned forward.

“Was it the same person who had refused the allocation?”

Helen's eyes moved back to the page.

“I can't confirm that.”

The answer was not enough.

But it gave Edward something important.

The Family Allocation Records Office had received the reference to R-04 on day 56.

It had not received the recipient schedule.

And its own system had retained the permanent allocation field as incomplete.

That meant the administrative department had not independently verified a family allocation.

Someone had used another route to create the impression that one existed.

Edward thanked Helen and requested a formal statement.

Outside the office, Jonathan reviewed his notes.

“Day 56,” he said. “The same day the OR-28 wording was revised.”

“Yes.”

“And the reference went to Desk 3.”

“Which retained the incomplete allocation field.”

Jonathan looked at the page again.

“So the department knew it couldn't complete the permanent allocation.”

Edward nodded.

“Yet the notification record was later altered to suggest a family had been informed.”

He looked toward the street.

“We need the original Desk 3 system record.”

“I'll request it.”

“And find out who had authority to alter the permanent allocation field.”

Jonathan closed his notebook.

“Do you think it was the same person who changed the OR-28 wording?”

“I don't know.”

Edward looked at the photocopy of the index.

There were too many separate actions to treat them as one.

Someone had changed the review status.

Someone had restricted the recipient schedule.

Someone had used Schedule 4 for a notification.

Someone had altered the notification summary.

And someone had preserved the original records in a separate archive.

Those actions might have been coordinated.

They might also have been carried out by people following different instructions, each believing they were handling a limited administrative responsibility.

The investigation had to establish which.

Edward was not prepared to replace one unproven explanation with another.

His phone vibrated.

A message from the archive supervisor had arrived.

**Additional item located in the R-04 distribution container. Original routing cover, previously attached to the day-56 status request. Not included in the initial inventory because it was filed separately under correspondence control.**

Edward read it twice.

“Jonathan.”

His colleague looked up.

“Another document?”

“An original routing cover.”

“From R-04?”

“Apparently.”

Edward opened the attached photograph.

The cover bore the same reference as the revised OR-28 request.

But beneath the distribution instruction was a handwritten line that had not appeared on any of the copies they had examined.

**The receiving authority must not be inferred from the party currently providing care.**

Below it was another sentence.

**Where no receiving authority has been appointed, the child remains under temporary administrative protection.**

Edward stared at the second line.

Then he noticed the initials.

Not E.V.

Not M.V.

Not S.B.

A different set.

**C.V.**

Charles Vale.

But the signature had not been examined.

And the date beside it was not day 56.

It was day 34.

The same day the original OR-28 recipient review had been requested.

Edward enlarged the photograph.

At the bottom of the cover, beneath Charles's initials, was a final handwritten instruction.

**Do not allow the temporary receiving arrangement to become permanent merely because no one has challenged it.**

For the first time in several days, Edward did not immediately speak.

The document did not prove that Charles had intended the children to be permanently allocated to the wrong families.

It did, however, establish that he had been warned of precisely that danger before the independent review was suspended.

And someone had kept the warning.

Someone had preserved it.

Someone had decided that, despite the altered records, the original instruction must survive.

Edward looked at the date again.

Day 34.

The investigation had been following the changes made after the review failed.

But perhaps the more important question was what Charles had been trying to prevent before it failed.

He turned to Jonathan.

“Find out who received this routing cover originally.”

Jonathan examined the photograph.

“It's marked for restricted correspondence.”

“Then trace the custody record.”

Edward returned his attention to the handwritten warning.

The children had remained in temporary care.

The receiving authority had never been appointed.

And yet, over the following months, proposed identities had been treated as though they were settled facts.

The next document might finally reveal who had tried to stop that from happening.

Or who had decided the warning was no longer worth following.
