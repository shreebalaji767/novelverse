# **Chapter 196 — The Two Signatures That Were Not the Same**

The first response arrived at 8:06.

The second arrived at 8:09.

Both came from the Programme Authority Office.

Neither contained a signature.

Edward placed the messages side by side.

The first concerned OI-07.

The second concerned PA-190/2.

The wording was almost identical.

**The originating authority has confirmed that limited procedural disclosure may proceed.**

But the final paragraphs differed.

For OI-07:

**The disclosure will identify the office responsible for issuing the instruction. Individual authorship remains subject to separate confirmation.**

For PA-190/2:

**The disclosure will identify the authority responsible for approving the administrative outcome. Individual execution remains subject to separate confirmation.**

Sophie read both messages.

“So they're releasing the authority behind each record, but not the individuals.”

“Yes.”

“Why separate office responsibility from individual authorship?”

“Because an office can issue an instruction through an authorised process without every person involved being its author.”

“And the person who signed the approval may not be the person who made the decision.”

“Exactly.”

Edward opened the attached release schedule.

There were two separate appointments for review.

OI-07 could be examined at 11:00.

PA-190/2 could be examined at 14:00.

The same custodian would oversee both.

But the records were not to be opened together.

Edward looked at the instruction.

**Separate examination required. No combined authorship assessment permitted.**

He closed the schedule.

“Let's do it properly.”

At 10:55, Eleanor Vale entered the records room.

The archival case was already on the table.

She checked the room's attendance sheet.

Edward and Sophie were the only investigators present.

“Before we begin,” she said, “I need to confirm that you understand the disclosure limits.”

Edward nodded.

“We will examine the originating office, the authority reference, the execution record and the distribution history. We will not infer individual authorship from office responsibility.”

“Good.”

She opened the first case.

The OI-07 instruction was inside a transparent archival sleeve.

Its original paper was slightly creased at the upper corner.

Edward recognised the wording immediately.

**Subject:** Independent verification and protected-principal communication controls.

**Originating office:** Programme Authority Office.

**Instruction date:** Day 42.

**Authority basis:** PAO/42/17.

**Issuing capacity:** Restricted originating instruction.

The authority reference was different from the document's ordinary administrative reference.

Edward wrote it down.

“PAO/42/17,” he said.

“Yes,” Eleanor replied.

“Does that identify the person who issued it?”

“No. It identifies the authority under which the office issued the instruction.”

“Was the instruction approved by the Chair?”

“The record confirms that the originating office acted under the Chair's authority. It does not establish that the Chair personally drafted or signed the instruction.”

Edward examined the lower portion.

There was a handwritten notation.

**Proceed with procedural review only. No appointment authority is conferred.**

He looked at Eleanor.

“Is that part of the original?”

“Yes.”

“Who wrote it?”

“Individual authorship remains restricted.”

“Was it added before or after the instruction was issued?”

“The preservation record places it on the original before circulation.”

Edward noted the distinction.

The instruction had been issued under a recognised authority.

The handwritten restriction had been present before it entered the administrative process.

That meant the restriction was not a later attempt to reinterpret the document.

It was part of the original instruction.

“Was the restriction communicated to all receiving departments?”

“Yes. It appears in the original and the distribution copy.”

“Then Programme Administration received it.”

“Yes.”

“Vale Private Counsel?”

“Yes.”

“Restricted Correspondence Administration?”

“Yes.”

Eleanor showed him the distribution acknowledgements.

Each office had recorded receipt.

The acknowledgements confirmed that the instruction had reached the departmental systems.

They did not establish that every individual employee had read it.

Edward closed the first sleeve.

“Thank you.”

Eleanor recorded the end time.

11:28.

The OI-07 examination was complete.

She sealed the case again.

At 11:42, Edward reviewed the distribution acknowledgements.

One entry stood out.

**Receiving office:** Restricted Correspondence Administration.

**Receipt date:** Day 42.

**Acknowledging function:** Administrative intake.

**Individual recipient:** Not recorded.

**Follow-up action:** Query preparation requested.

The next entry showed that the query preparation had been assigned the following morning.

That was the beginning of GOV/OC19/02.

Sophie pointed to the sequence.

“The original instruction reached the correspondence office on day 42. The day-44 query followed.”

“Yes.”

“So the C-19 transmission wasn't an independent request from an unknown source. It was part of the response to OI-07.”

“Correct.”

“But the query still needed an originating authority.”

Edward nodded.

“The instruction established the need for a review. It did not give the correspondence address authority to make a nomination.”

Sophie checked the day-44 task.

“Daniel's routing now makes more sense.”

“He was carrying out an administrative task created from the instruction.”

“And the person who originated the instruction remains unidentified.”

“Yes.”

Edward looked at the clock.

They still had the second examination.

At 13:50, Eleanor returned.

This time the archival case was smaller.

She checked the seal number.

The document inside was PA-190/2.

**Approval confirmation:** PAO/190/CONF-2.

**Date:** Day 190.

**Responsible office:** Programme Authority Office.

**Authority basis:** PAO/190/06.

**Scope:** Administrative acceptance of AR-164/09 findings.

**Execution status:** Confirmed.

Edward read the authority reference.

PAO/190/06.

It was not the same reference as PAO/42/17.

He looked at Eleanor.

“Different authority instrument.”

“Yes.”

“Different date.”

“Yes.”

“Different scope.”

“Yes.”

“Does the record establish a different author?”

“No.”

“Does it establish the same author?”

“No.”

Edward nodded.

He would not make the mistake the custody instruction warned against.

The approval certificate contained a typed statement.

*The findings of AR-164/09 are accepted for administrative effect within the stated scope.*

Below it was a signature block.

The signature itself was covered by a narrow restriction strip.

The role remained visible.

**Authorised Programme Authority Officer.**

Edward examined the execution certificate.

It confirmed that the approval had been signed using a valid authority credential.

The credential was not the same as the one used to issue OI-07.

But the certificate did not identify the individual behind either credential.

“Was the approval personally executed by the Chair?” Edward asked.

“The certificate does not establish that.”

“Was the officer acting under delegated authority?”

“The record confirms an authority basis. The individual delegation schedule remains restricted.”

“Can we request it?”

“You can.”

Edward looked at the two documents.

One instruction had initiated a procedural review.

One approval had accepted the review's findings.

They were separate instruments.

Separate authority references.

Separate execution records.

No proof of common authorship.

“Did PA-190/2 amend OI-07?” he asked.

“No.”

“Did it withdraw it?”

“No.”

“Did it appoint an OC-19 verifier?”

“No.”

“Did it close PR-06?”

“No.”

“Then what changed?”

“The administrative status of the reconciliation review.”

Edward nodded.

The review was complete.

The underlying procedure remained active.

At 14:35, Edward asked Eleanor about the delegation schedule.

“Can you tell us whether the same officer had authority to issue OI-07 and approve PA-190/2?”

“I cannot identify the officer.”

“But can you establish whether the authority schedules overlap?”

“The records show different authority instruments.”

“That wasn't my question.”

Eleanor looked at the certificates.

“The originating office has not authorised disclosure of the individual delegation schedules.”

“Then we don't know whether the same person could have exercised both authorities.”

“Correct.”

“Could two different people have exercised them?”

“Yes.”

“Could the same person have exercised them?”

“Yes.”

Edward made a note.

The possibility remained open.

He would not turn a missing identity into a conclusion.

“Dr Vale, who requested the separate preservation of these records?”

“The custody instruction was issued by the Programme Authority Office.”

“On whose authority?”

“The custody schedule refers to a separate preservation authority.”

“Is that another sealed instrument?”

“Yes.”

“Does it identify the person who required the separation?”

“It identifies the office and authority basis. Individual authorship remains restricted.”

Edward almost smiled.

Every answer led to another record.

But this time, the chain was becoming more coherent.

“Please include the preservation authority in our next request.”

“I will.”

At 15:20, Sophie received a response from the correspondence gateway.

It concerned the day-44 transmission.

The gateway had completed a deeper audit of the temporary administrative credential used to send RC-44/OC19.

The credential belonged to a support officer named Miriam Cole.

Sophie read the report.

“That's a name.”

Edward leaned forward.

“Does it say she prepared the message?”

“No. It says her credential was used.”

“Did she receive the instruction?”

“The access log shows she opened the task at 14:56.”

“Did she write the query?”

“Not established.”

“Did she transmit it?”

“The gateway records her credential as the transmitting credential.”

Edward took the report.

The activity sequence showed:

- Task received at 14:31.
- Task opened at 14:56.
- Attachment reviewed at 15:04.
- Transmission initiated at 16:12.
- Delivery accepted by Programme Administration at 16:13.

There was no draft history.

No author field.

No evidence showing who composed the attachment.

“Was Miriam interviewed?” he asked.

“Not yet.”

“Then arrange it.”

Sophie checked the personnel record.

Miriam Cole had left the correspondence office on day 119.

Her employment had ended through a standard administrative separation.

There was no disciplinary finding.

No recorded restriction.

No connection to the receiving route.

Edward read the note twice.

“She may have been nothing more than the person who sent a prepared document.”

“Or she may have prepared it.”

“We need to ask her.”

Sophie added her name to the interview list.

At 16:05, Elena received the result of her demonstration.

The programme coordinator had approved a second-stage test.

It was not a full deployment.

The platform would be tested against a small set of anonymised grant applications.

Elena read the email.

Then she read it again.

“What's the verdict?” Clara asked over the call.

“Second-stage test.”

“That's good.”

“Yes.”

“Why do you look like you've been asked to pay a bill?”

“Because now I have to make sure it works.”

Clara laughed.

Elena opened the test schedule.

There were twelve applications.

Each would have a different combination of missing documents, eligibility conditions, reviewer assignments and decision authorities.

She started creating test cases.

One application had a completed review but no authorised decision-maker.

Another had a valid decision authority but an incomplete review.

A third had received a document that had not yet been verified.

The platform handled the first two correctly.

The third was more complicated.

A document could be received by the system without being verified.

It could be verified without being accepted as sufficient evidence.

It could be accepted as sufficient evidence without proving the truth of every statement it contained.

Elena added another status.

**Evidence assessment:** Not started / In progress / Accepted / Rejected / Further clarification required.

Clara watched the change.

“You keep finding new ways to separate things.”

“Because people keep treating different things as though they're the same.”

“Maybe that's why your project is useful.”

Elena paused.

She thought about the long administrative records Edward had occasionally described.

The names.

The references.

The forms.

The endless confusion between receipt, acceptance and authority.

She had never wanted to become involved in the investigation.

But the principles were familiar now.

A document could exist without proving what someone claimed it proved.

A message could be delivered without establishing that the intended person had received it.

And a system could be technically complete while the underlying decision remained unresolved.

She saved the new status.

“Now I need to test it,” she said.

Clara groaned.

“Of course you do.”

At 17:18, Edward received a response concerning Miriam Cole.

She had agreed to an interview.

But she had attached a request.

**I would like to see the original RC-44/OC19 transmission record before answering questions about the document's preparation.**

Edward read the message.

“Reasonable,” Sophie said.

“Yes.”

“Could she be trying to coordinate her account with the record?”

“Possibly. Or she may want to avoid being asked to remember something she hasn't seen in years.”

“Should we provide it?”

“We can provide the transmission envelope and the document she transmitted, with unrelated restricted fields redacted.”

Sophie prepared the release.

Edward added a note that the interview would distinguish between document preparation, task assignment, credential use and transmission.

He did not want to ask Miriam whether she had sent the message and then treat her answer as proof that she had written it.

The interview was scheduled for the following morning.

At 18:02, a second message arrived from Eleanor.

The Programme Authority Office had approved a limited disclosure of the preservation instruction.

Edward opened the attachment.

It was dated day 49.

**Subject:** Separation and preservation of originating authority records.

The instruction required that:

1. The original OI-07 authorisation remain separate from the procedural instruction.
2. Any later approval concerning the reconciliation review remain separately preserved.
3. No inference of common authorship be made solely from shared custody.
4. No administrative summary replace the original authority records.
5. The preservation custodian must not certify an individual identity without the relevant originating record.

At the bottom was a reference.

**Issued under authority: PAO/49/03.**

Edward looked at Sophie.

“Another authority instrument.”

She opened the index.

The reference had one result.

**PAO/49/03 — Preservation Separation Direction.**

**Responsible office:** Programme Authority Office.

**Individual author:** Restricted.

**Execution status:** Confirmed.

Edward looked back at the day-49 instruction.

It had been issued two days after Dr Evelyn Shaw declined Adrian Holt.

Two days after the OC-19 proposal failed.

Two days after the alternative candidate discussion had remained unresolved.

Someone had decided that the original instruction and its later approval records must be preserved separately.

That decision might have been routine.

It might have been precautionary.

Or it might have reflected a concern that the records would eventually be mistaken for one another.

Edward read the final paragraph.

*The purpose of preservation is to prevent administrative continuity from being mistaken for substantive authority.*

He placed the page beside the day-190 outcome.

The same principle appeared again.

He had seen it in the instructions, the correspondence records, the review findings and the custody schedules.

The rule was consistent.

The people applying it had not always been.

At 18:24, he opened the next request.

**Please provide the authorisation and execution records for PAO/49/03, including the office responsible for initiating the separation direction and any stated reason for separating the original authority from subsequent approval records.**

He sent it.

The acknowledgement arrived immediately.

**Request received.**

Then another line appeared.

**Related preservation authority identified: PAO/49/01.**

Edward stared at the reference.

PAO/49/01 had been issued two days earlier.

Day 47.

The same day Adrian Holt's proposed appointment had been declined.

And the same day OC-19 had become officially vacant.

He looked at Sophie.

“Add it to tomorrow's request.”

“Do you think it's connected to the rejection?”

“I think the dates are connected.”

“That isn't the same thing.”

“No,” Edward said.

“It isn't.”

He wrote the reference on the board.

**PAO/49/01 — Preservation Authority.**

Below it, he added:

**Purpose unknown.**

For now, that was all they had.
