# **Chapter 199 — The Participant Who Had Left the Route**

The request for Credential E-3 was refused at 8:32.

Not rejected.

Refused.

Edward read the distinction in the response from Restricted Governance Review.

**The identity associated with Credential E-3 cannot be disclosed through the ordinary records-access process.**

**The credential was issued under a restricted external participation arrangement.**

**The originating office may confirm whether the credential remains valid, but disclosure of its holder requires separate authority.**

Sophie read the response over his shoulder.

“They're protecting the person.”

“They're protecting the arrangement.”

“Is there a difference?”

“There may be.”

Edward opened the final paragraph.

**Any request concerning the identity of the credential holder must be directed to the office responsible for the continuing personal communication arrangement.**

He looked at the screen.

“Which office?”

Sophie checked the attachment.

“Restricted Personal Communication Administration.”

Edward had seen the name before.

It appeared in the preservation instructions for PR-06, but never as an originating authority.

He opened the old correspondence index.

There it was.

**RPCA — Restricted Personal Communication Administration.**

The office had received a copy of the day-47 preservation instruction.

It had also received the original PR-06 procedure.

But its role had never been clearly defined.

Edward sent a narrow request.

He did not ask for the person's identity.

He asked whether Credential E-3 belonged to an individual whose participation in the receiving-route process had ended before day 47, and whether that individual remained involved in any personal communication arrangement.

The request was submitted at 8:47.

At 9:20, Edward returned to the day-36 proposal.

RA-36/11.

The record had originally been treated as a conditional receiving-route proposal.

A candidate had been identified.

A response had been received.

The appointment certificate had remained incomplete.

The name was sealed.

Edward asked the archive for a redacted copy of the proposal, retaining only the candidate's stated role, response conditions and any recorded limitations.

The archive responded that the request could be considered.

Then another message arrived.

**The candidate's response was not an acceptance of appointment.**

Edward read the sentence.

It was the same conclusion the day-47 report had raised.

He opened the original response receipt.

There were three sections.

**Section A — Receipt of proposal**

Completed.

**Section B — Conditional willingness to participate**

Completed.

**Section C — Acceptance of appointment**

Blank.

Below them was a handwritten note.

*I am willing to assist with the verification process if the competent authority is independently established. I do not accept responsibility for the receiving identity or for any placement decision.*

Edward read it twice.

The candidate had not refused every form of participation.

They had agreed to assist with verification under conditions.

But they had explicitly declined responsibility for the receiving identity and any placement decision.

The distinction was unmistakable.

Sophie pointed to the bottom of the page.

“There's a date.”

Day 36.

“And the report was submitted on day 47.”

“Eleven days later.”

Edward opened the appointment certificate.

The certificate had been prepared on day 38.

Its first section identified the proposed receiving-route representative.

Its second section listed the authority under which the appointment would take effect.

Its third section required confirmation from the competent receiving authority.

The first section was completed.

The second was partially completed.

The third was blank.

The certificate had never been executed.

“Why was it prepared if the candidate had only agreed conditionally?” Sophie asked.

“Because someone expected the conditions to be satisfied.”

“Were they?”

“Apparently not.”

Edward examined the document's routing history.

On day 39, it had been sent to the Programme Authority Office.

On day 40, it had been returned with a request for clarification.

On day 42, a second version had been created.

On day 44, the second version had been marked incomplete.

On day 47, the discrepancy report had been submitted.

The record was not a simple case of someone signing the wrong form.

It was a process that had repeatedly approached completion without reaching it.

And at some point, its incomplete status had been represented differently.

At 10:15, Marianne Vale called.

“I understand you've requested information concerning Credential E-3.”

“Yes.”

“The identity remains restricted.”

“I haven't requested disclosure of the identity. I asked whether the credential holder's participation had ended before day 47.”

“That question falls within the scope of the restricted arrangement.”

“Can you answer it?”

“Not without confirmation from the responsible office.”

“Which is RPCA?”

“Yes.”

Edward glanced at the correspondence on his screen.

“Does your office know who submitted RR-47/02?”

“We know the submission was accepted under Credential E-3.”

“That isn't the same thing.”

“No.”

“Does the Governance Secretariat know the identity?”

“I cannot confirm access to restricted identity information.”

“Did you review the report?”

“I reviewed its administrative classification.”

“Did you see the candidate response?”

“I saw the document reference. I did not review the sealed response itself.”

“Did you determine that the candidate had not accepted appointment?”

“No. That determination was made through the originating authority review.”

“Who made it?”

“The Programme Authority Office confirmed that the appointment was incomplete.”

“Was the candidate informed?”

“I don't know.”

Edward paused.

“Did the report request that the candidate be protected?”

Marianne took a moment.

“The report requested preservation of the existing communication restrictions.”

“Why?”

“Because the person submitting it was participating in a continuing protected communication arrangement.”

“Was that arrangement connected to P-04?”

“I cannot confirm that.”

“Was it connected to Gabriel Vale?”

“I cannot confirm that either.”

“Was it connected to C-19?”

“C-19 was a correspondence location. It was not a personal communication authority.”

Edward made a note.

“Then what did the arrangement protect?”

“The communication rights and confidentiality of a restricted participant.”

“Could the participant appoint a receiving representative?”

“No.”

“Could they approve a receiving identity?”

“No.”

“Could they act as the protected principal?”

“Not on the basis of the credential alone.”

Edward looked at the screen.

“Then the credential holder had access to the receiving-route process, but no authority to complete it.”

“That is correct.”

“Thank you.”

Before ending the call, Marianne added something.

“Mr Valmont, please be careful not to treat the submission as proof that the credential holder was the candidate named in RA-36/11.”

“I haven't.”

“Several records have been confused in that way before.”

Edward looked down at his notes.

“Are you aware of a specific instance?”

“I am aware that the records contain multiple restricted participants, each with different permissions. I cannot discuss their identities.”

The call ended.

Edward sat quietly for a moment.

There might be one person who submitted the report and another person who had been proposed as the receiving-route representative.

They might be the same person.

They might not.

Nothing in the available records established either possibility.

At 11:40, Elena's second-stage evaluation meeting began.

The programme reviewers had asked her to demonstrate how her platform handled conflicting evidence.

She shared her screen.

The first example showed an application with three documents.

The first had been received and verified.

The second had been received but was awaiting source confirmation.

The third had been submitted twice under different filenames.

Elena opened the evidence history.

“The system records each submission separately,” she explained. “If the same document appears twice, the reviewer can mark the relationship between them without deleting either record.”

One reviewer leaned forward.

“Why not simply replace the first file?”

“Because the first submission may already have been reviewed. Replacing it would make the history misleading.”

“And if the second file corrects the first?”

“Then the system records a superseding version. It doesn't pretend the earlier version never existed.”

Another reviewer asked, “What happens if the applicant withdraws the second document?”

“The withdrawal is recorded, but the original submission remains in the audit history.”

“Can the reviewer still see it?”

“Only if their role permits access to withdrawn material.”

The reviewer nodded.

Elena moved to the next screen.

“This is the authority panel.”

It displayed four separate sections.

- Application ownership.
- Evidence verification.
- Reviewer assignment.
- Final decision authority.

“These are deliberately separated,” she said. “A person who receives an application isn't automatically authorised to verify its contents. A person who verifies evidence isn't automatically authorised to make the final decision.”

The panel remained quiet for a moment.

Then one reviewer said, “You've built the system around preventing administrative assumptions.”

Elena smiled slightly.

“I've built it around recording what actually happened.”

The reviewer made a note.

“That's a useful distinction.”

The demonstration continued.

By the end, the panel approved her platform for a supervised pilot.

It was not a full institutional adoption.

It was not a funding award.

But it was permission to test the system with a real programme team under controlled conditions.

Elena closed the presentation.

Clara, watching from the back of the room, gave her a small thumbs-up.

Elena returned the gesture.

For once, she did not immediately start worrying about what might go wrong next.

At 14:05, Edward received a response from RPCA.

It contained no personal identity.

But it answered his question.

**Credential E-3 was issued to a participant whose formal receiving-route participation had ended on day 41.**

Edward read the sentence.

Day 41.

The report had been submitted on day 47.

Six days after the participant's formal involvement ended.

The response continued.

**The credential remained active for the limited purpose of preserving access to an existing personal communication arrangement.**

**The credential did not confer appointment, nomination, receiving identity, placement or decision authority.**

Edward looked at Sophie.

“So the participant had left the receiving route.”

“But the communication arrangement continued.”

“Exactly.”

He opened the day-41 records.

There were two entries.

The first concerned the candidate response.

**Receiving-route participation concluded.**

The second concerned personal communication.

**Restricted communication arrangement remains active.**

The entries had been recorded by separate offices.

The first by Programme Administration.

The second by RPCA.

Edward compared their timestamps.

The receiving-route participation ended at 16:20.

The personal communication arrangement remained active at 16:34.

Fourteen minutes apart.

Someone had deliberately preserved one relationship while ending another.

The participant could no longer act within the receiving-route process.

But they could still communicate through the protected arrangement.

And they had used that continuing access to submit the discrepancy report six days later.

Edward added the dates to the chronology.

He did not yet know whether the participant had been the proposed candidate.

But the report's author had known enough about the process to identify the difference between conditional participation and an actual appointment.

At 15:30, Sophie found another record.

It was a short closure notice.

**Reference:** RPCA-41/06.

**Date:** Day 41.

**Subject:** Continuation of restricted personal communication arrangement.

**Former receiving-route participant:** Identity sealed.

**Continuing permissions:**

- Receive restricted procedural correspondence.
- Submit questions concerning personal communication conditions.
- Request correction of administrative descriptions affecting the participant's recorded role.
- Preserve original communication records.

**Prohibited permissions:**

- Nominate receiving representatives.
- Confirm receiving identities.
- Accept placement responsibility.
- Authorise family allocation.
- Waive principal acknowledgement requirements.

Edward read the last section.

The participant's authority had been narrowed, not eliminated.

They could challenge how their role was described.

They could preserve records.

They could ask questions.

But they could not decide where a child went or who received responsibility for that child.

The report now made more sense.

The person who submitted it had not tried to take control of the receiving process.

They had objected to the administrative representation of an incomplete appointment.

Edward opened the submission record again.

The report's requested action was precise:

**Preserve original instruments and suspend any administrative representation of completed appointment pending competent authority review.**

The participant had not asked for the candidate to be appointed.

They had not asked for the process to be abandoned.

They had asked for the record to remain accurate.

At 17:05, an additional note arrived from RPCA.

It had been authorised for limited disclosure.

**The former participant's continuing communication arrangement was established before day 41. It was not created in response to RR-47/02.**

Edward read it.

The arrangement had existed before the report.

That meant the participant's identity had been protected for an earlier reason.

The report had not created the protection.

It had used an existing channel.

Sophie opened the original arrangement reference.

**PC-41/02.**

The reference was unfamiliar.

The index showed it had been created on day 29.

Its purpose was described as:

**Continuity of protected personal correspondence during receiving-route withdrawal.**

Edward looked at the date.

Day 29.

The receiving-route process had not yet reached the day-36 conditional proposal.

The participant's communication arrangement had been established seven days earlier.

“Could the person have been involved in an earlier receiving proposal?” Sophie asked.

“Possibly.”

“Could they have been a family member?”

“Possibly.”

“Could they have been an external representative?”

“Yes.”

Edward closed the record.

“None of those possibilities is established.”

He opened a new request.

This time he asked for the authority under which PC-41/02 had been created, the office that requested it, and whether the arrangement had been established for the benefit of the participant or at the participant's own request.

He sent it.

The response came back almost immediately.

**The arrangement was established under a restricted personal instruction.**

**The instruction was not issued by Programme Administration, Governance Secretariat or Vale Private Counsel.**

Edward read the second line.

“Then who issued it?”

Sophie opened the authority field.

The individual name was sealed.

The office field was blank.

The record listed only an authority reference.

**PI-07.**

Edward stopped.

PI-07.

The sealed personal instruction that required joint authority.

The instruction that had been preserved separately from OI-07.

The instruction whose custodian had not been authorised to certify its author.

He looked at the reference again.

The former receiving-route participant's communication arrangement had been established under PI-07.

That did not mean the participant was P-04.

It did not mean they were a family member.

It did not mean they had authored the instruction.

But it established that their protected communication arrangement was connected to the sealed personal instruction.

Edward opened the chronology.

For the first time, he placed three records beside one another:

**Day 29 — PC-41/02:** Restricted personal communication arrangement established under PI-07.

**Day 36 — RA-36/11:** Conditional receiving-route proposal and candidate response.

**Day 47 — RR-47/02:** Discrepancy report submitted by former receiving-route participant using Credential E-3.

The same protected authority sat behind the first and third records.

The second remained unconnected.

Edward leaned back.

The participant had left the receiving route.

Their communication rights had survived.

And their report had helped prevent an incomplete appointment from being treated as a completed one.

But the identity behind the credential remained sealed.

He looked at the request he had just sent.

The next record would not identify the participant directly.

It would identify who had requested the communication arrangement in the first place.

And that request had been made under PI-07.
