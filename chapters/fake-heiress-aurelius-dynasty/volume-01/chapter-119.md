# **Chapter 119 — The Response That Had No Author**

The R-12 file arrived in three separate boxes.

Edward had requested the complete correspondence history, including draft reports, internal memoranda, recipient communications, dispatch records, and every version of the conditional response.

The archive had initially warned him that the request might take several days.

It had taken nine.

Jonathan placed the boxes on the conference table.

“Everything from day 31 to day 61,” he said. “The archive claims this is the complete surviving file.”

Edward looked at the labels.

“Claims?”

“They cannot certify that no documents were removed before the preservation order.”

Edward opened the first box.

“Then we start with what remains.”

The earliest document was a request from Vale Private Counsel.

**Day 33 — R-12 Alternate Recipient Review**

The request asked Dr Evelyn Shaw to examine the proposed recipient's profile and conditional consent.

It included three questions:

- Was the proposed recipient's identity independently verified?
- Was the conditional consent sufficiently informed?
- Could the recipient be considered for formal appointment after the outstanding checks?

Dr Shaw's report answered all three.

No.

Not established.

No appointment recommended.

Edward placed the report beside the day-47 administrative summary.

The contrast was becoming familiar.

The original review had remained cautious.

The later administrative wording had become increasingly confident.

“Find the response documents,” he told Jonathan.

They worked through the first box.

There were acknowledgements from the archive, correspondence with Northbridge, payment records, and two internal notes concerning the preparation of the alternate recipient package.

Nothing identified the proposed recipient.

The second box contained the conditional acknowledgement templates.

The third contained correspondence marked **R-12 / RECIPIENT RESPONSE**.

Edward stopped.

“That's it.”

The first document was a routing slip.

**Day 40 — Conditional Response Material**

The description read:

*Response materials received for restricted review.*

No sender was named.

No recipient was named.

The receipt field contained an administrative code:

**CA-17/RESTRICTED INTAKE.**

Jonathan checked the accompanying register.

“Cross Administrative Services?”

“Possibly.”

“Same company?”

“The reference appears in their preserved correspondence index.”

Edward looked at the date.

Day 40.

The original package had been prepared on day 36 and sealed on day 37.

The modified acknowledgement template had been created on day 39.

A response material had supposedly arrived on day 40.

“Could it be the proposed recipient's reply?”

“We don't know.”

“Can we establish whether the envelope was delivered?”

“There's no courier number.”

“Internal delivery?”

“There's a receipt stamp, but no receiving signature.”

Edward examined the next page.

It was a short administrative note.

*Conditional response material to remain separate from recipient appointment record. No appointment may be entered on basis of receipt alone.*

The instruction was handwritten.

The initials were E.V.

Eleanor Vale again.

Edward read the note carefully.

She had apparently seen the material.

But she had not treated it as proof of acceptance.

“Find the original envelope,” he said.

Jonathan searched the inventory.

“There is a reference to one.”

“Where?”

“Restricted correspondence storage. Box R-12/CR-04.”

Edward stood.

“Then we're going there.”

The envelope was stored in a separate archive room.

It was smaller than Edward expected.

Its exterior bore no personal name.

Only the reference:

**R-12/CR-04**

A preservation sticker covered the flap.

Dr Mira Sen examined it.

“The seal is intact.”

“Can you determine whether it has been opened previously?”

“Not conclusively. The preservation sticker was applied after the original envelope was received. I can only establish that the current seal has not been disturbed.”

Edward checked the archive record.

The envelope had been transferred from Cross Administrative Services on day 43.

The receiving signature was illegible.

Below it, someone had written:

*Restricted response material. Not to be entered as recipient consent.*

He recognised the handwriting.

Eleanor.

Jonathan read the transfer date.

“Three days after the response was supposedly received.”

“And one day after the second modification of the acknowledgement template.”

Edward looked at the envelope.

“Let's see what the legal custodian permits us to examine.”

The permission arrived later that afternoon.

The envelope could be opened for preservation and evidentiary review, provided the original seal was documented and the contents were not treated as an identity determination.

Dr Sen photographed every surface before opening it.

Inside was a single sheet.

Not a letter.

Not a signed acceptance.

A conditional response statement.

The heading read:

**RESPONSE TO PROPOSED RECEIVING ARRANGEMENT**

The body contained four sentences.

*I acknowledge that information concerning a possible receiving arrangement has been presented for consideration.*

*I am willing to receive further information once the originating identity and the authority of the proposed arrangement have been independently established.*

*I do not accept permanent responsibility for an unidentified child.*

*This response must not be recorded as consent to appointment or placement.*

There was a signature at the bottom.

But no printed name.

Only an initial.

**S.**

Edward stared at it.

“Who is S.?”

Jonathan checked the document index.

“No matching recipient name.”

“Is there a witness?”

“No.”

“Date?”

“Not on the statement.”

The reverse contained a faint receiving stamp.

**CA-17 — DAY 40**

The document was real.

It had been received by Cross Administrative Services.

But its author remained unidentified.

And its wording was not an acceptance.

Edward looked at the third sentence again.

*I do not accept permanent responsibility for an unidentified child.*

Someone had been willing to consider receiving a child.

But only after the identity and authority were established.

That was very different from agreeing to become the child's permanent family.

Dr Sen examined the paper under magnification.

“The signature and body text appear to have been written with the same instrument,” she said. “The statement itself appears to be one original document, not an assembled copy.”

“Can you identify the writer?”

“Not from this alone.”

“Can you compare the handwriting with Susan Bell's?”

“I can compare it. I cannot promise attribution.”

Edward looked at the initial.

“S.”

Jonathan spoke quietly.

“Susan Bell.”

“Or someone else.”

“She's the obvious possibility.”

“She is one possibility. We will not turn an initial into an identity.”

Edward photographed the document.

“Find Susan.”

Susan Bell agreed to meet them at the former residential administration centre.

She had been retired for years.

Her office was now used for records storage, and she appeared uncomfortable when Edward placed the photograph of the statement before her.

“I've seen that wording,” she said.

Edward leaned forward.

“Where?”

“In a proposed allocation document.”

“Did you write it?”

Susan looked again.

“I don't remember signing this.”

“Do you recognise the handwriting?”

She studied the initial.

“I wrote my name as Susan Bell. I didn't usually sign with a single initial.”

“Could you have done so on an administrative receipt?”

“Possibly. But this isn't a receipt.”

Edward waited.

Susan read the four sentences again.

Then she shook her head.

“I would have written something similar.”

“Why?”

“Because I was asked to accept responsibility for a child whose originating identity had not been established.”

“Was this the same proposed arrangement concerning Child 28-B?”

“I cannot say. I never received a confirmed identity for that child.”

“Did anyone ask you to become the permanent receiving representative?”

“Charles Vale's office sent me a proposed allocation. I refused to acknowledge it.”

“Why?”

“Because I was not the child's family. I was an administrator responsible for temporary residential review. I had no authority to accept a permanent placement.”

Edward placed the day-118 refusal record beside the statement.

“Is this your wording?”

Susan examined it.

“Yes. That is my handwriting.”

The day-118 record was the document in which she had explicitly refused to accept responsibility for Child 28-B.

Edward pointed to the earlier statement.

“Could this have been your response on day 40?”

Susan took a long moment.

“It could have been.”

“Do you remember sending it?”

“No.”

“Did you ever tell anyone you were willing to receive further information?”

“Yes.”

“Who?”

“An administrative liaison. I believe it was Marianne Cross.”

Edward exchanged a glance with Jonathan.

“Did you tell her you would accept the child if the identity was established?”

“No. I told her I would review proper documentation if it was provided.”

“Did she ask you to sign anything?”

“She asked whether I would consider a proposed arrangement. I said I would consider information. I would not accept a child without knowing who the child was and who had authority to place them.”

“Did you receive the originating identity?”

“No.”

“Did anyone explain the child's biological family?”

“No.”

Susan pushed the document back.

“If that is my response, then someone has preserved only the part that suited them.”

Edward did not disagree.

But he still needed proof that the document was hers.

The handwriting comparison took four days.

Dr Sen compared the statement with Susan Bell's signed day-118 refusal, several older administrative reports, and a handwritten note from her residential review file.

Her conclusion was cautious.

The handwriting was consistent with Susan Bell's known writing.

The initial S was also consistent with her usual abbreviated signature.

However, the short length of the statement prevented definitive identification.

Edward read the report.

“So it may be hers.”

“Yes.”

“And if it is?”

“Then it establishes that Susan Bell was willing to consider receiving further information.”

“Not that she agreed to receive a child.”

“Correct.”

Edward closed the report.

The distinction was becoming the central problem in the entire investigation.

A person could agree to review a proposal.

A person could acknowledge receipt.

A person could express conditional willingness.

None of those things meant the person had accepted permanent responsibility.

At the Valmont Estate, Elena was making her final preparations.

The travel folder had been checked twice.

Her suitcase was closed.

Her laptop charger, passport, programme documents, and financial papers were arranged in a smaller carry-on bag.

Nicholas stood near the doorway holding a printed sheet.

“I made a list.”

Elena looked at him.

“Of course you did.”

“Emergency contacts, transport arrangements, university administration, local medical services, bank details, and what to do if your accommodation key doesn't work.”

“You've written a guidebook.”

“It's three pages.”

“That's a guidebook.”

He handed it to her.

She read the first page.

It was genuinely useful.

The second contained several unnecessary reminders.

The third included a note that said:

**Do not attempt to repair electrical appliances yourself.**

Elena looked up.

“Why is this here?”

“Because you once tried to fix the kitchen toaster.”

“It was a loose connection.”

“It was smoking.”

“It was only a little smoke.”

Nicholas folded his arms.

“Please don't argue with the toaster in Singapore.”

Elena laughed.

“I'll try.”

His expression softened.

“Call us when you arrive.”

“I will.”

“Not three hours later.”

“Fine.”

“And if something goes wrong—”

“I'll tell you.”

Nicholas nodded.

He looked as though he wanted to say something else, but couldn't decide how to phrase it.

Elena closed the folder.

“I'm going to be fine.”

“I know.”

“You don't have to supervise me from another country.”

“I know that too.”

He smiled faintly.

“I'm still allowed to worry.”

“Yes. Just don't turn it into a spreadsheet.”

He laughed.

For a few minutes, the conversation became ordinary again.

That was what Elena wanted.

Not a dramatic farewell.

Not a reminder of everything that had changed.

Just the knowledge that she could leave without being expected to carry everyone else's emotions with her.

Later that evening, Isabelle appeared at Elena's door.

She carried a small envelope.

“What is that?” Elena asked.

“Your revised foundation proposal.”

“You printed it?”

“I wanted you to have a copy.”

Elena accepted the envelope.

“I'll review it when I have time.”

“I know. Don't feel obligated to do it immediately.”

Isabelle hesitated.

Then she added, “I hope Singapore goes well.”

“Thank you.”

“You've worked hard for this.”

Elena looked at her.

“So have you.”

Isabelle glanced at the suitcase.

“It's strange.”

“What is?”

“When you first left the estate, I thought your departure meant you were leaving my place in the family.”

Elena was quiet.

“And now?” she asked.

“Now I think I was treating everything like a competition.”

Elena looked down at the envelope.

“I think we both were.”

Isabelle gave a small nod.

“I don't know how things will be between us.”

“Neither do I.”

“But I don't want every conversation to feel like we're measuring who belongs more.”

Elena looked up.

“Then let's stop measuring.”

Isabelle's expression softened.

It was not forgiveness for everything that had happened.

It was not a promise that they would become close overnight.

It was simply an agreement to try something different.

“Good luck,” Isabelle said.

“You too.”

Edward returned to the investigation the next morning.

Susan Bell's statement had created another question.

If she had provided a conditional response on day 40, who had received it?

The Cross Administrative Services intake register showed the document arriving under CA-17.

The transfer record showed it was later placed in the R-12 archive.

But there was no sender name.

No courier number.

No delivery acknowledgement.

No original cover letter.

Edward requested the company's internal intake ledger.

Marianne Cross supplied it that afternoon.

The ledger contained a short entry:

**Day 40 — CA-17 — Conditional recipient response. Received without external delivery confirmation.**

The next line was more important.

**Forwarded to R-12 review custody, day 41.**

And beneath it:

**Originating cover sheet retained separately.**

Edward looked up.

“Where is the cover sheet?”

Marianne checked her index.

“It was transferred with the administrative records.”

“Which box?”

She searched again.

“CA-17 / INTAKE CORRESPONDENCE.”

Jonathan opened the preservation inventory.

There was a box with that exact label.

It had been stored separately from the AV-18 package.

They requested it immediately.

The cover sheet was recovered late that afternoon.

It was a single page with a narrow printed header.

**PRIVATE CORRESPONDENCE — CA-17**

The sender field was blank.

The destination field read:

**Cross Administrative Services — Restricted Intake**

The subject line:

**Response concerning possible receiving arrangement.**

Edward examined the lower half.

There was a handwritten instruction.

*Please preserve the original response. It must not be treated as acceptance, and the sender's identity must not be disclosed until the originating records are verified.*

No signature.

No initials.

But the final line contained a reference.

**V-17 / PERSONAL DELIVERY**

Jonathan frowned.

“V-17 again.”

Edward nodded.

The private residential property associated with Charles Vale.

The place where Annex C, Schedule 4, and the private instruction archive had been preserved.

But this did not establish that Charles had sent the response.

The cover sheet merely showed that the correspondence had been delivered through a channel connected to V-17.

Edward read the final instruction once more.

“The sender didn't want their identity disclosed.”

“Or someone wanted it hidden.”

“Both possibilities remain.”

Jonathan turned the sheet over.

There was a faint pressure mark on the reverse.

Dr Sen examined it.

After several minutes, she adjusted the lighting.

A few words emerged.

**Do not send another request. The first response was conditional.**

Below that, almost invisible:

**Wait for originating confirmation.**

Edward stared at the words.

The sender had not merely refused permanent responsibility.

They had specifically warned against another request before the child's originating identity was confirmed.

And yet the day-120 alternate notification attempt had later been prepared.

He looked at the date of the cover sheet.

Day 40.

The day-40 response had arrived.

Dr Shaw's report had not yet been completed.

The proposed recipient had not been appointed.

The originating identity remained unresolved.

And someone had already received a warning not to ask again.

Edward placed the document into its preservation sleeve.

“Find out who delivered this to Cross Administrative Services.”

Jonathan nodded.

“And if the delivery record is missing?”

“Then we find out who removed it.”

Edward looked once more at the blank sender field.

For the first time, the proposed recipient was no longer the only unknown person in the arrangement.

There was also someone who had written a careful refusal, hidden their identity, and asked the administration to wait.

Someone who might have known more about the child than the records had ever admitted.

But the most important part was still missing.

**The identity of the person who had been asked to become a family—and had refused to do so without knowing whose child they were being asked to receive.**
