# **Chapter 100 — The Man Appointed Without His Knowledge**

Adrian Holt did not like unexpected visitors.

He liked them even less when they arrived carrying documents from eighteen years earlier.

Edward found him in his private office shortly after ten the following morning. Holt was reviewing a set of financial papers when his assistant announced the visitors.

He looked at Edward, then at Jonathan.

“I assume this isn't about the archive inventory.”

“No,” Edward replied. “It's about a document bearing your name.”

Holt closed the financial folder.

“Which document?”

Edward placed a copy of the day-131 confirmation on his desk.

Holt read the heading.

His expression remained neutral until he reached the final section.

Then he put the paper down.

“I didn't sign this.”

Edward remained standing.

“The signature appears to be yours.”

“It may be my signature. That doesn't mean I signed this document.”

Jonathan opened his notebook.

“Can you explain that distinction?”

Holt looked at him.

“Certainly. A signature can be genuine and still be attached to a document that the signer never approved.”

Edward exchanged a brief glance with Jonathan.

It was the same problem they had encountered with Richard Valmont's signature.

“Did you sign any acknowledgement on day one hundred and thirty-one?” Edward asked.

“Yes.”

“What did it acknowledge?”

“A sealed preservation transfer.”

“From whom?”

“Charles Vale's office.”

“To where?”

“To V-17.”

Edward placed a second document beside the first.

The original transfer receipt.

Holt examined it.

“This is the document I signed.”

“What was inside the package?”

“I wasn't told.”

“Did you ask?”

“Yes. I was told it contained records requiring restricted preservation.”

“Did you accept responsibility for the contents?”

“I accepted responsibility for the sealed package during transfer. Not for the identities recorded inside it.”

Edward pointed to the fourth version of the notification.

“Then how did your name appear as receiving representative?”

Holt read the wording again.

His jaw tightened.

“Someone has confused custody with receiving authority.”

“Did you authorise that?”

“No.”

“Did you appoint yourself?”

“No.”

“Did Charles Vale?”

“Charles could nominate someone. He could not appoint a receiving identity custodian by himself.”

“Who could?”

“The process required an independent appointment, followed by acceptance from the nominee and confirmation by the relevant authorities.”

“Was that ever completed?”

“No.”

Holt's answer was immediate.

Edward sat down.

“Then why did you sign the transfer receipt?”

“Because a preservation transfer was necessary. The documents could not remain in an unsecured administrative office.”

“Were you told that the transfer would be used to support a family allocation?”

“No.”

“Were you told that the receiving family had already been informed?”

“No.”

“Did you ever meet the receiving family?”

Holt's expression hardened.

“I have never met anyone under that description.”

Jonathan turned a page in his notebook.

“Did you know Susan Bell had refused to accept the proposed allocation?”

“I knew she had refused to sign a permanent placement acknowledgement.”

“Did you know the refusal had been changed into an administrative completion?”

“No.”

Edward pushed the day-131 document closer.

“Then someone used your name to create an authority that never existed.”

Holt studied the page.

“I want to see the original.”

“We're arranging access.”

“Do so. I want the original examined.”

“Why?”

“Because if someone attached my signature to this document, I want to know when it happened and who handled it.”

Edward watched him carefully.

Holt's reaction was not the reaction of a man who had been caught.

It was the reaction of someone discovering that a document he had signed for one purpose had been used for another.

But Edward had learned not to mistake anger for innocence.

“Mr Holt,” he said, “you've told us before that you never opened Annex C.”

“Correct.”

“You held both parts.”

“Yes.”

“You transferred them to Unit Six.”

“Yes.”

“And you signed the day-131 preservation transfer.”

“Yes.”

“Yet a document now identifies you as the receiving representative.”

Holt looked directly at him.

“I was a custodian of records. I was never authorised to decide which family received a child.”

“Why didn't you report that the role had been attributed to you?”

“I didn't know it had been.”

Edward gathered the papers.

“Then we'll establish who did it.”

Holt stopped him.

“One more thing.”

Edward turned.

“The day-131 transfer was not the first time someone attempted to use my name as an authority.”

Edward waited.

Holt opened a locked drawer and removed an old file.

It contained a copy of a letter dated day forty-seven.

The subject was:

**PROPOSED RECEIVING IDENTITY CUSTODIAN — APPOINTMENT CONFIRMATION**

The letter named Adrian Holt as the proposed custodian.

At the bottom, a handwritten note appeared.

**Nominee declined. No appointment to be recorded.**

Edward looked up.

“You declined the nomination?”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“Because I had already handled originating records. I could not independently verify the receiving identity.”

“Who nominated you?”

“Charles Vale.”

“Did he accept your refusal?”

“He acknowledged it.”

“Was the nomination withdrawn?”

Holt turned the page.

“The formal withdrawal is here.”

Edward read it.

The nomination had been withdrawn on day forty-seven.

The document was signed by Charles Vale.

The receiving identity custodian position had remained vacant.

“Then someone knew you had declined,” Jonathan said.

“Of course.”

“And they still used your name.”

Holt's face became very still.

“Yes.”

Edward closed the file.

For the first time, the investigation had a clear contradiction.

Holt had been nominated.

He had refused.

His refusal had been recorded.

The nomination had been withdrawn.

And eighty-four days later, a document identified him as the receiving representative.

Someone had not merely misunderstood his role.

Someone had revived an appointment that had never existed.

The document examiner arrived at Unit Six shortly after noon.

Dr Mira Sen had already examined several signatures in the investigation. She had established that Alexander Aurelius's signature on the Northbridge financial schedule was genuine but mechanically reproduced, and that Richard Valmont's genuine signature had been attached to a different page.

Now she examined Adrian Holt's signature.

The original day-131 transfer receipt was brought out under supervision.

The confirmation document was placed beside it.

Mira used magnification and comparison software, then examined the paper under angled light.

After nearly an hour, she straightened.

“The signature on the transfer receipt is original,” she said.

Edward nodded.

“And the confirmation?”

“The signature image is consistent with the transfer receipt. However, it has been reproduced.”

“Mechanically?”

“Yes. The line characteristics are consistent with a scanned or photocopied source.”

“Could it have been copied from the transfer receipt?”

“Very likely.”

“Can you determine who did it?”

“No. The document does not preserve enough information about the copying process.”

She turned the confirmation over.

“There's something else.”

Edward leaned closer.

A faint impression appeared beneath the lower-right corner.

It was barely visible, but Mira had enhanced the image.

The words were incomplete.

**...PRESERVATION TRANSFER ONLY.**

Edward read them twice.

“The original transfer wording.”

“Yes. It appears that the confirmation was prepared using a document that had previously contained the preservation-transfer receipt.”

“Could the entire page have been assembled from the transfer document?”

“Possibly. I need more time to compare the paper fibres, printing patterns, and alignment.”

Jonathan pointed to the lower section.

“What about the typed wording?”

“It's on a separate print layer. I cannot say whether it was printed before or after the signature image was added.”

Edward looked at the confirmation.

The document had not simply misused Holt's name.

It had taken an authentic signature from a limited custody receipt and placed it beneath a statement claiming he was the receiving representative.

A false authority had been constructed from a genuine administrative act.

“Prepare a full report,” Edward said.

Mira nodded.

“And preserve the original exactly as it is.”

At the Aurelius Global headquarters, Helena Brooks received the preliminary findings that afternoon.

She read the report in her office while Alexander stood near the conference table.

“Adrian Holt was nominated and refused,” she said.

Alexander nodded.

“His refusal was recorded on day forty-seven.”

“And the nomination was withdrawn.”

“Yes.”

“Yet his name appears as receiving representative on day one hundred and thirty-one.”

“Apparently.”

Helena placed the report down.

“This is not a clerical mistake.”

“No.”

“Someone knew he had refused.”

“Yes.”

“And someone used a genuine signature from a preservation receipt to make the appointment appear legitimate.”

Alexander looked toward the windows.

“Who had access to the original receipt?”

“The people who handled the transfer and the archive copies. Charles Vale's office, Holt, and the Unit Six preservation staff.”

“Could Margaret Voss have accessed it?”

“Not through the documented chain.”

“Marianne Cross?”

“Not after the correspondence was transferred.”

“Dr Hale?”

“He had access to the originating medical records, not Holt's transfer receipt.”

Alexander considered the names.

“Then find the records.”

Helena nodded.

He looked again at the document.

“Someone has spent eighteen years creating the appearance of a completed decision.”

“Or preserving the appearance that a decision was made.”

Alexander's gaze hardened.

“Those are not the same thing.”

“No,” Helena agreed.

“And until we know who benefited, we cannot assume why.”

At the Valmont Estate, Elena had reached the final stage of her travel preparations.

Her suitcase was finally closed.

Her documents were arranged in a folder, her passport and programme acceptance had been checked twice, and her laptop had been backed up.

Nicholas appeared at her door holding a small package.

“What is that?” she asked.

“A practical item.”

“That sounds suspicious.”

“Open it.”

Elena took the package.

Inside was a compact document organiser with several labelled sections.

Passport.

Programme documents.

Travel receipts.

Emergency contacts.

Medical information.

She looked up.

“This is actually useful.”

“I know.”

“Did you make it?”

“No. I bought it.”

“Then why are you looking so proud?”

“Because I selected the correct size.”

Elena laughed.

She opened the organiser and examined the compartments.

“Thank you.”

Nicholas shrugged.

“Don't lose it.”

“I won't.”

“And keep copies of your documents separately.”

“I already have digital copies.”

“Printed copies too.”

She gave him a look.

“Are you giving me travel advice or preparing me for a disaster?”

“Both.”

Elena shook her head, smiling.

Then she became quiet.

“Nick?”

“Hmm?”

“Do you think things will be strange when I come back?”

He stopped adjusting the package.

“What things?”

“Everything. The investigation. Isabelle. The family. All of it.”

Nicholas considered his answer.

“Some things will probably be different.”

“That's not very reassuring.”

“I'd rather be honest.”

She looked down at the organiser.

“I don't want the Singapore programme to become an escape from everything.”

“Then don't make it one.”

“I want to go because I earned the place.”

“Exactly.”

“And I want to come back without feeling like I have to choose between studying and dealing with family problems.”

Nicholas nodded.

“You don't have to solve everything while you're away.”

Elena smiled faintly.

“Promise?”

“I can't promise what other people will do. But I can promise that you won't be expected to abandon your own plans.”

She looked at him for a moment.

Then she placed the organiser into her suitcase.

“That's enough.”

Nicholas leaned against the doorframe.

“By the way, Isabelle asked whether you wanted to see her project outline.”

Elena blinked.

“Her foundation project?”

“Yes.”

“What did you say?”

“That she should ask you herself.”

Elena nodded.

“I'd like to see it.”

Nicholas smiled.

“Then I'll tell her.”

As he left, Elena returned to her desk.

Her programme documents were ready.

Her project proposal was saved.

And for the first time in several days, the future seemed to be moving forward at a pace she could control.

That evening, Edward and Jonathan visited the successor archive responsible for C-17.

The building was smaller than Unit Six, but its records were arranged with equal care.

The archivist found the reference in a restricted correspondence index.

“C-17 was a family correspondence account,” she explained. “It received documents that could not be sent directly to the named parties.”

“Who operated it?”

“The account was administered through Vale Private Counsel.”

“Was it a person?”

“No. It was a correspondence function.”

“Who could collect material?”

“Authorised representatives.”

“Names?”

The archivist checked the register.

“There were three.”

She placed the list on the table.

The first was Charles Vale.

The second was Marianne Cross.

The third was Eleanor Vale.

Edward looked at the names.

“Eleanor?”

“Yes.”

“Was she the account custodian?”

“No. She was an authorised recipient.”

Jonathan examined the dates.

“Was she authorised on day one hundred and thirty-one?”

The archivist checked.

“Yes.”

Edward felt a familiar tension settle in his chest.

Eleanor had reviewed Schedule 4 on day one hundred and eighteen.

She had written that no family notification could be based on it.

Thirteen days later, a false receiving-authority document had been routed to an account for which she was an authorised recipient.

That did not establish that she had collected it.

It did not establish that she had known what it contained.

But it meant she had access to the correspondence channel through which the document had travelled.

“Is there a collection receipt?” Edward asked.

The archivist searched the register.

“Yes.”

She removed a thin folder.

The collection receipt was dated day one hundred and thirty-two.

The name was clear.

**Collected by: Eleanor Vale.**

Edward read it again.

The document had been collected one day after the false confirmation was sent.

He looked at Jonathan.

Neither spoke.

The archivist turned the page.

A second line had been added beneath the collection entry.

**Recipient requested that the document remain sealed pending review of originating and receiving authority.**

Edward's eyes narrowed.

“Was it returned?”

“No.”

“Was it opened?”

“The register does not say.”

“Was it transferred?”

The archivist checked the final column.

“Yes.”

The destination was written in small type.

**UNIT 6 — SEPARATE PRESERVATION COMPARTMENT.**

Edward stared at the entry.

Eleanor had collected the document.

She had kept it sealed.

Then she had transferred it to the archive where the other disputed records were stored separately.

She had not accepted the false appointment.

She had not allowed the document to become part of the verified identity file.

But she had never told them she had collected it.

Edward looked at the archivist.

“Is there a record of what happened to the document afterward?”

The archivist turned the page.

There was a final note.

**Retained under restricted instruction. Do not release to originating or receiving family without joint authority.**

The instruction carried Eleanor Vale's initials.

E.V.

Edward slowly closed the folder.

The question had changed again.

Eleanor had not merely discovered that someone was misusing Schedule 4.

She had personally collected the document that falsely appointed Adrian Holt as receiving representative.

She had preserved it.

She had kept it sealed.

And she had never mentioned that she had held it for eighteen years.

Why?

Was she protecting the records from further misuse?

Or was there something inside that document she had not wanted either family to see?

For the first time, Edward wondered whether Eleanor's insistence on keeping the records separate had concealed a decision of her own.

And the only person who could explain it was the woman who had collected the sealed document.
