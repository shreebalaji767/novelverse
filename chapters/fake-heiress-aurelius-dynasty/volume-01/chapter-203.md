# **Chapter 203 — The Address That Came From Another File**

The request for CR-29/08 was denied at 8:16.

Edward read the response twice.

**Access cannot be granted through the ordinary reconciliation process.**

**The reference forms part of a protected correspondence record established under PI-07.**

**Any request concerning its use in another administrative file must identify the receiving record, the date of transfer, and the authority under which the transfer occurred.**

Sophie placed her coffee beside the keyboard.

“They want us to prove how the address was transferred before they'll tell us how it was transferred.”

“Not exactly.”

Edward turned the screen toward her.

“They're asking us to identify the transfer event. We already have the receiving record.”

“RA-36/11.”

“And the date.”

“Day 36.”

“Then we can request the transfer metadata without requesting the protected address itself.”

Sophie nodded.

“That should avoid exposing the correspondence location.”

Edward opened a new request.

He specified that the investigation did not require the address, the identity of the protected participant, or the contents of PI-07.

It required only the administrative history of the address field.

Who entered it.

Which source record they used.

Whether the transfer was authorised.

And whether the candidate had been notified.

He submitted the request.

At 9:40, Elena's pilot review began.

The first problem appeared before the second application had been assessed.

A reviewer had uploaded a supporting document under the wrong applicant reference.

The document was genuine.

The applicant was eligible.

But the evidence belonged to someone else.

The reviewer had selected the wrong record from a dropdown menu.

Elena examined the audit history.

“Don't delete the attachment,” she said.

The reviewer looked concerned.

“But it is incorrect.”

“We should mark it as incorrectly attached, preserve the event, and move the document through the correction procedure.”

“Won't that make the record unnecessarily complicated?”

“It will make the history accurate.”

The programme manager leaned over the screen.

“Can the correction be approved by the same reviewer?”

Elena considered it.

“Not if the correction changes which applicant the evidence belongs to. Someone independent should confirm the source.”

The manager nodded.

“Add that to the pilot observations.”

Elena entered the requirement.

She was beginning to understand that a reliable system was not one in which mistakes never occurred.

It was one in which mistakes could not quietly become facts.

At 11:05, the Programme Authority Office responded.

The address transfer had occurred on day 36 at 10:18.

The entry was made by a restricted administrative user.

The source field displayed:

**CR-29/08 — Protected Correspondence Address Reference.**

The destination field displayed:

**RA-36/11 — Candidate Contact Details.**

The transfer method was listed as:

**Manual administrative transcription.**

No automated link existed between the two records.

No separate transfer approval was attached.

Edward read the final field.

**Reason:** Continuity of contact.

“Who authorised it?” Sophie asked.

“The system doesn't say.”

“Could the user have had permission to access CR-29/08?”

“Possibly. But access permission and authority to reuse the information are different things.”

He examined the user classification.

**Restricted Administrative Support — Temporary Assignment.**

The credential was active on day 36.

Its access scope included correspondence indexing.

It did not include receiving-route appointment decisions.

Edward requested the user's identity through the credential custodian.

At 12:30, Marianne Vale joined the review by secure video.

She listened as Edward explained the transfer.

“CR-29/08 was not created as a general contact directory,” she said.

“What was its purpose?”

“To preserve a protected correspondence location under the personal communication arrangement.”

“Could it be used for another candidate's notification?”

“Not without a separate authority establishing that the location was appropriate for that purpose.”

“Was there one?”

“I have not found one.”

Edward brought up the transfer entry.

“Someone copied the address manually.”

“Yes.”

“Could that person have believed the transfer was permitted?”

“Belief is not the same as authority.”

Marianne's expression remained composed.

“CR-29/08 was not an appointment record, a candidate declaration, or a receiving-route nomination. Its existence did not make the address suitable for every subsequent communication.”

“Do you know who requested the transfer?”

“No.”

“Would the person who entered it have been able to see the protected correspondence record?”

“Some administrative credentials could access the index. Fewer could access the underlying material. The transfer log does not establish what the user actually viewed.”

Edward looked at the screen.

“Then the next question is whether they copied the address from the protected record itself or from an extract.”

“Correct.”

Marianne paused.

“And there is another distinction.”

“What?”

“CR-29/08 was created to preserve a correspondence location. It did not establish that the person associated with that location was the person who controlled it.”

Edward wrote that down.

The address was a location.

It was not an identity.

At 14:10, the credential custodian supplied the access history.

The temporary administrative credential belonged to a records assistant whose employment had ended several months earlier.

Her name was **Lydia Park**.

The record showed three actions on day 36.

- 10:02 — Opened the RA-36/11 candidate record.
- 10:11 — Opened the CR-29/08 correspondence index.
- 10:18 — Entered the address into RA-36/11.

At 10:21, she closed both records.

There was no evidence that she opened PI-07.

There was no evidence that she viewed the identity attached to CR-29/08.

There was no record of a direct instruction from the candidate.

Edward looked at the timeline.

“Three actions. Seven minutes between opening the source and entering the destination.”

Sophie checked the task register.

“Was she assigned to RA-36/11?”

“No. Her assignment covered correspondence indexing and administrative migration.”

“Was RA-36/11 part of a migration?”

“Not according to its task record.”

Edward requested an interview.

Lydia Park agreed to speak the following afternoon.

Before the interview, she sent a written clarification.

She remembered the reference.

She did not remember the candidate's name.

She had not retained copies of restricted records after leaving the office.

Her statement ended with one sentence:

**I entered the address because the task instructed me to maintain continuity of contact, not because I had verified the candidate's identity or correspondence arrangements.**

Edward read it again.

The phrase was familiar.

It appeared in the day-36 administrative note.

**Maintain continuity of contact.**

The wording had not established who gave the instruction.

It had only explained the task.

At 16:25, Edward received a separate message from Dr Eleanor Vale.

She had reviewed the request concerning CR-29/08.

Her response was brief.

**The address reference may be examined for its administrative use, but the protected personal correspondence arrangement must remain separate.**

**The investigation should not infer that the person associated with the address was the participant, the candidate, or the person who refused the notification.**

**The original correspondence location must not be disclosed to the investigation team unless a separate necessity is established.**

Edward appreciated the clarity.

The investigation was becoming crowded with people who had been assigned roles by paperwork they had never accepted.

He had no intention of adding another.

At 17:40, Elena received the pilot assessment.

The reviewers had approved the correction-history feature.

They had also requested one additional function: a warning whenever information was transferred from a restricted source record into an unrelated application.

Elena read the recommendation.

**A source record may be valid for its original purpose without being valid for reuse in another administrative process.**

She smiled faintly.

It was almost exactly what she had been trying to explain during the morning review.

Clara called her shortly afterward.

“How did it go?”

“Better than expected.”

“Did they like the system?”

“They found three ways it could be improved.”

Clara laughed.

“That sounds like approval.”

“It sounds like unpaid software testing.”

“Are you going to complain?”

“I already did.”

“Good. Progress.”

Elena leaned back in her chair.

For a moment, she thought about how often a record could be correct in one context and misleading in another.

A name could be accurate.

An address could be genuine.

A signature could belong to the person whose name appeared beneath it.

None of those things, by themselves, established authority.

She opened her development notes and added a new rule.

**Transferred information must retain its original purpose, source, and permission status.**

Then she saved the file.

The following morning, Lydia Park arrived at the Valmont investigation office.

She looked nervous but composed.

Edward introduced Sophie and explained the limits of the interview.

“We are not asking you to identify the protected participant or the candidate,” he said. “We want to understand the administrative instruction you received.”

Lydia nodded.

“I understand.”

Edward placed a copy of the day-36 task history before her.

“Do you recognise this entry?”

“Yes.”

“You entered the correspondence address?”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“I was asked to complete a missing contact field.”

“By whom?”

Lydia looked at the task reference.

“I don't remember the person's name.”

“Was the instruction written?”

“Yes. It came through the administrative task queue.”

“Do you still have a copy?”

“No. I didn't have permission to retain restricted work documents.”

“Did the task tell you to use CR-29/08?”

“Yes.”

“Did it tell you that the candidate was the person associated with that record?”

“No.”

“Did it say the candidate had authorised the use of that address?”

“No.”

Edward waited.

Lydia looked at the task history again.

“I remember asking whether the address had been confirmed.”

“What were you told?”

“That it was already approved for correspondence.”

“Approved for whose correspondence?”

“I don't know.”

“Did you ask?”

“I asked whether I should verify it with the candidate.”

“And?”

She hesitated.

“The instruction said the candidate's identity was restricted and that contact should remain within the existing route.”

Edward looked at Sophie.

“Do you remember the exact wording?”

Lydia shook her head.

“Not exactly. But I remember the instruction because it seemed unusual. I was being asked to complete a contact field without contacting the person whose contact field it was.”

“Did you raise an objection?”

“I asked for clarification. The task was returned to me with a note.”

“What note?”

Lydia closed her eyes briefly, trying to remember.

“Something like, 'Use the existing protected reference. Do not create a parallel contact route.'”

Edward did not interrupt.

“Was that enough for you?”

“I was an administrative assistant. I understood that the task had been reviewed by someone with access to the restricted system. I didn't understand that the address was being used for a different purpose.”

“Did you believe you were completing a valid transfer?”

“Yes.”

“Did you personally verify the address?”

“No.”

“Did you know whether the candidate had ever received correspondence there?”

“No.”

Edward closed the folder.

“Thank you. That's all we need for now.”

Lydia looked relieved.

Then she spoke again.

“There is something else.”

Edward reopened the folder.

“What is it?”

“The task wasn't originally assigned to me.”

“Who was it assigned to?”

“I don't know. It appeared in my queue after it had been returned.”

“Returned by whom?”

Lydia looked at the task history.

“The review desk.”

Edward leaned forward.

“Which review desk?”

“The Programme Administration Review Desk.”

The same shared account that had modified the day-46 source classification.

The same account that had changed the wording of the day-47 administrative entry.

Lydia continued.

“I remember the first version of the task had a different instruction.”

“What did it say?”

“I can't remember every word.”

“Do you remember the difference?”

She nodded.

“The original task asked for confirmation of the candidate's contact details.”

“And the revised one?”

“It asked me to populate the contact field from the protected reference.”

Edward exchanged a glance with Sophie.

“Was the change documented?”

“I believe so.”

“Where?”

“In the task history. There should be an earlier version.”

Edward looked at the screen.

The current record showed only the final instruction.

But Lydia had just confirmed that an earlier version existed.

A version asking for confirmation.

A later version replacing confirmation with transcription.

Edward made a note.

**Preserve all versions of task RA-36/11. Do not rely on final task view alone.**

He looked at Lydia.

“One last question. Did you ever speak to the person who issued the revised instruction?”

“No.”

“Did they identify themselves?”

“No.”

“Was the instruction signed?”

“No. It came from the shared review account.”

Edward thanked her.

After she left, Sophie opened the task history request.

The system returned a new status.

RECORD RETRIEVAL RESULT

**RA-36/11 — Original task versions**

Version 1

Preserved

Request confirmation of candidate contact details through an authorised channel.

Version 2

Preserved

Populate candidate contact field from existing protected correspondence reference.

Modification source

Shared account

PA-REVIEW-06

Individual operator not established.

Edward stared at the two versions.

The original task had asked for confirmation.

The revised task had removed the need for confirmation.

And the revision had been made through the same shared account that had later altered the administrative description of the candidate's status.

He opened a new request for the complete version history, including the review comments and the individual access events.

This time, he did not ask only who had changed the task.

He asked who had requested the change.

Because someone had decided that the candidate did not need to be contacted before their address was entered.

And that decision had come before the envelope was ever sent.
