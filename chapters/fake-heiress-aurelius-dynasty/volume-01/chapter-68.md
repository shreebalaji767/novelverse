# **Chapter 68 — The Schedule Margaret Never Submitted**

Margaret Voss did not answer the telephone.

Edward tried twice.

The first call rang until it disconnected.

The second went directly to voicemail.

He placed his phone on the desk and looked at the notation on the recovered expenditure instruction.

**Supporting beneficiary schedule retained separately — original custodian: M. Voss.**

Jonathan stood opposite him, reading the same line.

"She told us she had the Category 17 register."

"She did."

"And she gave us the comparison report."

"Yes."

"But she never mentioned another schedule."

Edward closed the document.

"Which means we need to find out whether she forgot to mention it, or deliberately withheld it."

Jonathan glanced at the clock.

"Do you want to go to her residence?"

"Not tonight."

"Why?"

"Because I want to know what we are asking for before we arrive."

He gathered the financial instruction, the Northbridge reconciliation schedule, and the earlier carbon copy.

The three documents belonged together, but they described different things.

The expenditure instruction established that Alexander had approved a general financial ceiling.

The reconciliation schedule recorded three child-related administrative references.

The carbon copy showed that the reconciliation schedule had originally been marked as pending confirmation.

The copied signature had been added later.

And somewhere between those documents lay a beneficiary schedule that Margaret Voss had been responsible for retaining.

Edward opened his laptop.

"Let's find out what that schedule was supposed to contain."

The following morning, Margaret Voss received a letter from Edward.

It was brief.

He requested an appointment to discuss a document associated with the Northbridge expenditure instruction, specifically the supporting beneficiary schedule identified under reference NB-17/04.

Margaret read the letter twice.

Then she placed it beside her breakfast plate.

For several minutes, she did nothing.

Her apartment was quiet.

The curtains were half-open, allowing the morning light to fall across a small dining table. A cup of tea stood untouched beside a newspaper.

She had spent eighteen years trying to keep certain documents separate.

Not because she believed the separation would protect everyone.

Because, at the time, she had believed that keeping them separate was the only way to prevent someone from making an irreversible mistake.

She had not expected the old records to survive.

She had certainly not expected Edward Valmont to find them.

Margaret reached for her telephone.

She dialled a number she had not used in years.

The call connected.

"Yes?"

"This is Margaret Voss."

There was a pause.

"Mrs Voss."

"I need to ask you something about the Northbridge archive."

"That matter is closed."

"Apparently not."

"Who has been asking?"

"Edward Valmont."

Another silence.

Margaret looked at the letter.

"He has found the expenditure instruction."

"That is unfortunate."

"He has also found the notation about the beneficiary schedule."

The person on the other end of the line did not respond immediately.

Then a voice said, "You were supposed to have destroyed that."

Margaret's fingers tightened around the telephone.

"No. I was supposed to retain it."

"You were instructed to dispose of the supporting material once the account had been reconciled."

"I never received that instruction."

"You received it."

"I received a request. I did not receive a lawful instruction from the originating office."

The voice became colder.

"Do not turn this into a legal argument."

Margaret looked toward the window.

"It became a legal argument eighteen years ago."

She ended the call.

For a long time, she remained seated.

Then she opened a locked drawer in the cabinet beside her desk.

Inside was a thin grey folder.

On its cover, in faded handwriting, were the words:

**NB-17/04 — ORIGINAL SUPPORTING RECORDS**

Margaret took it out.

She had kept it all these years.

Edward and Jonathan arrived at Margaret's apartment shortly after eleven.

She opened the door herself.

"You found it," Edward said.

Margaret looked at him.

"I assume you mean the schedule."

"Yes."

"Come in."

They followed her into the sitting room.

Margaret did not offer tea.

Instead, she placed the grey folder on the table.

Edward looked at it.

"Is this the original?"

"It is the original supporting file."

Jonathan leaned forward.

"May we examine it?"

"You may examine it here."

Edward opened the folder carefully.

The first document was a typed index.

The second was a handwritten receiving summary.

The third contained several numbered entries.

The fourth was a blank template.

The fifth was a folded sheet with a red border.

Edward stopped.

"What's this?"

"The beneficiary schedule."

He unfolded it.

Unlike the Northbridge reconciliation document, this one did not contain the three familiar child references.

It contained five.

Two were marked with hospital-originating references.

One was identified by a private maternity clinic number.

The remaining two were temporary residential references.

Edward read the entries.

Then he looked up.

"Why are there five?"

"Because the schedule recorded administrative beneficiaries, not necessarily five separate children."

Jonathan frowned.

"Explain."

Margaret sat opposite them.

"Northbridge required a separate reference for every receiving arrangement. A single child could appear under more than one reference if the child moved between medical supervision, temporary accommodation, and a receiving representative."

Edward looked down again.

"So the five entries could represent fewer than five infants."

"Yes."

"Could they represent only one?"

Margaret hesitated.

"In theory. But I cannot tell you that."

Edward turned the page.

There were columns for originating record, temporary reference, receiving institution, responsible representative, and confirmation status.

The first two entries were marked:

**Identity unresolved.**

The third read:

**Maternal record under review.**

The fourth:

**Receiving arrangement pending verification.**

The fifth:

**Do not disclose identity status to temporary representative.**

Edward's eyes stopped at the final line.

"Child 28-C."

Margaret nodded.

"That is the entry associated with Isabella Vale."

"Why was she listed as a temporary receiving representative?"

"Because she had signed a request for temporary medical supervision."

"Was she supposed to receive a child?"

"That was not what the document said."

"Then why was her name placed in the receiving representative column?"

"Because the request was being processed through her."

Jonathan leaned closer.

"That sounds like a distinction without a difference."

"It is not."

Margaret's voice was firm.

"She was listed as the person through whom the request had been submitted. That did not mean she was authorised to take physical custody of the infant."

Edward studied the entry.

"Was she told that?"

"No."

"Was she told anything?"

"She was told that a child connected with her maternity record might be placed under temporary supervision. She was not told whether the child was hers."

"Who told her?"

"Eleanor Vale."

Edward looked up.

"Eleanor?"

Margaret nodded.

"She had the medical file at that stage."

"Was Eleanor authorised to make that statement?"

"She was authorised to explain the administrative status of the file. She was not authorised to confirm biological identity."

Edward returned to the schedule.

The distinction was becoming familiar.

A name could be used without proving identity.

A representative could be listed without receiving custody.

A signature could be authentic without authorising the action attached to it.

The records had been built around ambiguity.

Someone had repeatedly taken that ambiguity and presented it as certainty.

"Why didn't you give us this folder earlier?" Edward asked.

Margaret looked at the documents.

"Because I was not sure what would happen if I did."

"You knew we were investigating the children's movements."

"I knew."

"You knew the schedule contained information about Isabella's request."

"I knew that too."

"And you still withheld it."

Margaret accepted the accusation without argument.

"Yes."

Jonathan's expression hardened.

"People have spent eighteen years searching for children whose identities may have been deliberately concealed."

"I understand."

"Do you?"

Margaret looked at him.

"I have spent eighteen years knowing that a child may have been moved under a false name because someone decided that a temporary arrangement was more convenient than a proper investigation."

Edward intervened before Jonathan could respond.

"Then tell us what happened."

Margaret folded her hands.

"The beneficiary schedule was prepared after the first round of temporary arrangements. Northbridge wanted a consolidated list so it could reconcile its expenses. I was asked to verify the references against the originating records."

"By whom?"

"Charles Vale."

"Did he explain why?"

"He said the accounts needed to be closed."

"Did you verify the entries?"

"Some."

"Which ones?"

"Those supported by original receiving documents."

"And the others?"

"I refused to certify them."

Edward looked at the red-bordered sheet.

"Was this the reason?"

"Yes."

She pointed to the final column.

"Several entries had been marked as confirmed even though the underlying records were incomplete."

"Who marked them?"

"Charles's office."

"Did you challenge him?"

"I did."

"What did he say?"

Margaret's expression became distant.

"He told me the children had already been placed, and that the paperwork only needed to catch up."

Edward's attention sharpened.

"Did he say where they had been placed?"

"No."

"Did he name the receiving institutions?"

"Not all of them."

"Did he identify the children?"

"No."

Margaret paused.

"That was when I understood the schedule was not merely an accounting document anymore."

Edward examined the five entries again.

The first carried the reference **STC-A/0217**.

The second carried **STC-B/0546**.

The third was **IV-204**.

The fourth was **28-B**.

The fifth was **28-C**.

He placed the sheet beside his notes.

"These are the records we've been examining."

"Yes."

"But you said they may not represent five children."

"They may not."

"Can you establish whether any of these references belong to the same infant?"

"Not from this schedule."

"Then what is the purpose of the schedule?"

"To record which administrative office was responsible for the next step."

Jonathan pointed to the fourth entry.

"Child 28-B. Margaret Voss."

"That was my temporary receiving assignment."

"Were you physically responsible for the child?"

"Not continuously."

"What does that mean?"

"The child was under a residential arrangement. My responsibility was to monitor the paperwork and ensure that the receiving institution submitted the required reports."

"Which institution?"

"Greenfield Children's House initially. Meridian Children's Medical Centre afterward."

Edward glanced at Jonathan.

That matched the records they had already found.

"And Child IV-204?" he asked.

"That was under Eleanor Vale's medical supervision."

"Was Eleanor physically caring for the child?"

"No. She was responsible for the medical file and the transfer coordination."

"And Child 28-C?"

"Isabella Vale was listed as the temporary representative. That did not establish that she ever received the child."

Edward closed his eyes briefly.

The five entries had created the appearance of a complicated network of transfers.

But the schedule did not prove that five children existed.

It did not even prove that every listed child had been physically transferred.

It was a record of responsibility, or claimed responsibility, within an administrative system.

That system had been used to make incomplete arrangements appear orderly.

"Who was supposed to confirm the identities?" he asked.

Margaret pointed to the final column.

"The originating representative."

"Who was that?"

"For the hospital records, the relevant medical authorities. For Isabella's maternity record, the clinic. For the Valmont records, the originating hospital."

"And for the Aurelius records?"

Margaret did not answer immediately.

Then she said, "Victoria Aurelius was listed as the originating representative."

Edward looked up.

"She never authorised the placement."

"I know."

"Yet her name appeared on the original Category 17 register."

"Yes."

"And the signature was mechanically reproduced."

"Yes."

"Did you know that when you received this schedule?"

"I knew the authorisation was disputed."

"Did you know the signature was false?"

Margaret looked down.

"I knew it had not been personally signed by Victoria."

Edward remained still.

"Why didn't you tell us that earlier?"

"Because I did not know whether the reproduction had been made with or without her knowledge."

"We have since established that she did not authorise it."

"Then you know more than I did at the time."

It was not a satisfying answer.

But Edward could not dismiss it as dishonest.

Margaret had been working with incomplete information too.

The difference was that she had possessed documents capable of revealing more.

Jonathan found something on the reverse of the beneficiary schedule.

A small handwritten notation had been added beneath the red border.

**Final verification deferred pending return of originating identity materials.**

Beside it were two initials.

**M.V.**

Jonathan looked at Margaret.

"Your initials?"

"Yes."

"When did you write this?"

"On the twenty-eighth day."

"The same day you received the comparison report?"

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because the comparison report confirmed that the photograph and the bracelet associated with Isabella's maternity record did not belong to the same infant."

Edward leaned forward.

"Did you know where Isabella's child was?"

"No."

"Did you know where Infant A was?"

"No."

"Did you know whether they were the same child?"

"No."

"Then what did you do?"

"I refused to sign the final verification."

Edward studied her face.

"Was that the reason the schedule remained incomplete?"

"Partly."

"What was the other reason?"

Margaret's fingers moved toward the folder.

She did not touch it.

"The final verification required a document from the originating representative. That document never arrived."

"Which representative?"

"Charles Vale said the confirmation would come from the Aurelius Family Office."

Edward's expression tightened.

"Did it?"

"No."

"Did you ask Victoria Aurelius directly?"

"I tried."

"How?"

"I sent a written request through the Family Office."

"To whom?"

"To the chairman's administrative office."

Alexander's office.

Edward took out his notebook.

"Did you receive a response?"

Margaret nodded.

"Yes."

"What did it say?"

"That the Family Office had not authorised the placement and that no confirmation would be issued."

Edward looked at her.

"Do you still have the response?"

"Yes."

"Where?"

Margaret opened the grey folder again and removed a folded letter.

The paper was older than the rest.

Its edges had become soft.

Edward unfolded it.

The letter was dated thirty-one days after the recorded births.

It was addressed to Margaret Voss.

The wording was concise.

The Aurelius Family Office denied having issued an instruction authorising the placement of any infant under Victoria Aurelius's name.

It also requested that all documents bearing the disputed authority reference be preserved and returned for independent examination.

At the bottom was a typed name.

**Adrian Holt — Office of the Chairman.**

Edward looked at Margaret.

"Why wasn't this in the archive?"

"I kept it."

"Why?"

"Because Charles Vale told me to disregard it."

Jonathan stared at her.

"He told you to disregard a direct letter from the Aurelius Family Office?"

"Yes."

"On what grounds?"

"He said it was an internal dispute and that the matter had already been settled."

"Did you believe him?"

"No."

Margaret looked at the letter.

"That was why I kept it."

Edward photographed the letter and returned it to the folder.

He had expected to find a hidden document.

He had not expected to find a direct denial from Alexander's office.

It changed the timeline.

The Aurelius Family Office had formally denied the disputed authorisation on the thirty-first day.

Margaret had received the denial.

Charles had told her to disregard it.

Yet the Northbridge reconciliation schedule had later acquired Alexander's copied signature.

Someone had used the chairman's identity to make a financial document look like a placement approval, despite the Family Office having explicitly denied the authority.

It was deliberate.

But Edward still needed to know who had attached the signature.

"Was the beneficiary schedule ever submitted?" he asked.

"No."

"Did you send it anywhere?"

"No."

"Did anyone collect it?"

Margaret hesitated.

"Someone attempted to."

"Who?"

"Charles Vale."

"When?"

"Approximately two months after the births."

"Did you give it to him?"

"No."

"Why not?"

"Because the schedule contained unverified entries, and the Aurelius office had already instructed me to preserve the disputed records."

"Did Charles threaten you?"

Margaret looked away.

"He said that if I refused to cooperate, I would be blamed for the delay in the children's placements."

Edward said nothing.

"He also told me that the families involved would be better served if the records were closed. He said continuing the investigation would only create more suffering."

"Did he mention Catherine Valmont?"

"Yes."

"How?"

"He said the Valmont family had already received the child they were supposed to receive."

Edward's expression changed.

"Which child?"

Margaret shook her head.

"He did not specify."

"Did he say Elena?"

"No."

"Isabelle?"

"No."

"Did he say whether the child at the Valmont Estate was connected to any of these records?"

"No."

The answer was frustratingly consistent.

Margaret knew about the administrative arrangements.

She knew that the records had been manipulated.

She did not know the children's biological identities.

And, despite everything, she had no proof that the infant associated with any particular reference was Elena.

Edward closed the folder.

"One more question."

Margaret waited.

"Why did you say the child you were responsible for was never supposed to be Elena?"

Her expression changed.

For the first time that morning, she seemed genuinely uncomfortable.

"Because the name Elena appeared in a later document."

"What document?"

"A residential review."

"Where?"

"Meridian."

Edward leaned forward.

"Was the child identified as Elena?"

"No. The document referred to a child who was expected to receive that name."

"Expected by whom?"

Margaret looked at the folder.

"I don't know."

"Was it connected to Child 28-B?"

"I cannot establish that."

"Then why did you say it?"

"Because I knew the assignment I was given was not intended to be the final placement for the child who later appeared in the residential review."

Edward stared at her.

"You knew that child would eventually be called Elena?"

"No."

Margaret's voice was quiet.

"I knew someone intended to use that name. I did not know whether they were referring to the same child."

The room became still.

That was the distinction.

Someone had intended to use Elena's name in a residential document.

But that did not establish that the child was Elena Valmont.

Nor did it establish that the child had been the infant Margaret monitored.

Edward stood.

"Thank you for showing us the schedule."

Margaret looked at him.

"Will you tell the Aurelius family about the letter?"

"Yes."

"Will you tell them I kept it?"

"Yes."

She nodded slowly.

"I thought they deserved to know."

Edward paused at the door.

"Then why did you wait eighteen years?"

Margaret did not answer immediately.

Finally, she said, "Because I was afraid that if I gave the records to the wrong person, the truth would disappear again."

Edward left without replying.

He understood fear.

But he also understood what eighteen years of silence had cost.

That evening, the Aurelius family received copies of the beneficiary schedule and the letter from Alexander's office.

Victoria read the letter first.

She recognised the formal language.

She remembered the period.

The Family Office had been dealing with a series of troubling reports, and she had been told that the disputed placement documents were being reviewed.

She had never been shown the full beneficiary schedule.

"Someone knew we had rejected the authorisation," she said.

Alexander nodded.

"And continued using it."

Adrian studied the dates.

"The denial was issued on the thirty-first day. The schedule was retained on the twenty-eighth."

"So Margaret had the unresolved schedule before the denial arrived," Damien said.

"Yes," Edward replied through the video call. "She refused to certify it, and she kept the original."

Lucien looked at the five entries.

"Can we tell whether any of these references belong to our daughter?"

"Not yet."

"Not even STC-A?"

"Not even STC-A."

Julian had been listening quietly from the other end of the room.

He looked at the schedule.

"Then we still don't know where she was."

Victoria turned toward him.

"No, sweetheart."

"Or who took care of her?"

"No."

"Or what name she had?"

Victoria's expression softened.

"Not yet."

Julian lowered his eyes.

He had imagined many different versions of his missing sister.

A baby in a hospital.

A little girl learning to walk.

A child going to school.

A teenager who might have grown up with an entirely different family.

Every new document made those possibilities more complicated.

But he did not want to stop searching.

"At least we know someone tried to stop the wrong records from being accepted," he said.

Edward nodded.

"Margaret did refuse to certify the schedule."

"Then she helped."

"She also withheld it for eighteen years."

Julian considered that.

"Both things can be true."

For a moment, nobody spoke.

Victoria reached for the letter again.

She read the final paragraph.

The Aurelius Family Office had requested that all disputed documents be preserved.

But the letter did not contain an instruction to investigate the children's physical locations.

It did not contain a list of receiving facilities.

It did not contain any confirmed identity.

The office had rejected the false authorisation.

It had not found the children.

Alexander looked at the schedule.

"Find Adrian Holt."

Adrian glanced up.

"He's still alive. Retired, but I can locate him."

"Do it. I want to know who received the original response and what happened afterward."

Edward nodded.

"I'll also request the Meridian residential review."

"Good."

Alexander looked at him.

"And Edward?"

"Yes?"

"Do not let anyone turn an administrative reference into a biological identity."

"I won't."

The call ended.

At the Valmont Estate, Elena finished her university application shortly before ten.

She checked the documents one final time.

Her personal statement was shorter now.

It no longer tried to describe a perfect future.

It explained what she wanted to learn, what interested her, and why she wanted the opportunity.

She submitted the application.

A confirmation message appeared.

**Application received.**

Elena stared at it.

Then she let out a long breath.

"Done."

Nicholas, who had entered the library to retrieve a book, looked over.

"Did you finish?"

"Submitted."

"Congratulations."

"Thank you."

He glanced at the confirmation screen.

"Which programme?"

"Singapore International Academic Programme."

"Good."

Elena closed the laptop.

"I don't know why I was so nervous."

"Because you care about it."

"That's an irritatingly reasonable answer."

Nicholas smiled.

"Would you like to celebrate?"

"How?"

"Food."

Elena considered.

"Something inexpensive."

Nicholas looked offended.

"Why must everything be inexpensive?"

"Because I am perfectly capable of buying food without involving the family treasury."

"That was a simple invitation."

"And I am giving you a simple answer."

Nicholas shook his head.

"Fine. I'll ask the kitchen to make something."

"That is still involving the family treasury."

"Elena."

She laughed.

He left the library pretending to be deeply disappointed.

Elena picked up her laptop again.

The confirmation message remained on the screen.

For the first time in several weeks, she felt that something in her life was moving forward because of a decision she had made herself.

She did not know that somewhere in a restricted archive, her name had appeared eighteen years earlier.

She did not know that someone had written it beside an unidentified child.

And she certainly did not know that the name had been used without any reliable proof of the child's identity.

At 11:38 p.m., Jonathan sent Edward a message.

**Meridian has located the residential review. It is not part of the original medical file. They say it was received from an external representative.**

Edward immediately called him.

"Who was the representative?"

"That's the problem."

"What problem?"

"The document carries two receiving stamps."

"Two?"

"One from Meridian. One from Vale Private Counsel."

Edward sat up.

"Read the names."

Jonathan took a breath.

"The Meridian stamp identifies the document as a residential review concerning an unnamed female child."

"And the second?"

"Vale Private Counsel marked it as an identity confirmation request."

Edward closed his eyes.

"Who signed the request?"

Jonathan paused.

Then he answered.

"Charles Vale."

Edward looked at the beneficiary schedule on his desk.

The final entry remained visible beneath the red border.

**Do not disclose identity status to temporary representative.**

He thought of Margaret's explanation.

The child might receive a name.

The representative might be told nothing.

The record might be reviewed.

But none of those steps established who the child actually was.

"Send me the complete document," Edward said.

"I already have it."

"Does it contain the name Elena?"

Jonathan was silent for a moment.

"Yes."

Edward looked toward the dark window.

"Where?"

Jonathan read the line.

**Proposed residential name: Elena.**

Beneath it, in a different handwriting, was a final instruction.

**Do not enter this name into the permanent identity register until the originating family confirms.**

Edward slowly lowered the phone.

Someone had planned to name an unidentified child Elena.

Someone had also instructed the institution not to make that name permanent.

And the instruction had been signed by Charles Vale.

But the document was dated after the day Elena was already recorded in the Valmont family's household papers.

Edward looked at the date again.

Then he noticed something else.

The residential review had been prepared for a child whose age was recorded as **ninety-two days**.

The same day Child 28-B was transferred from Greenfield Children's House to Meridian.

The same child whose receiving representative was Margaret Voss.

Edward stared at the page.

For the first time, the name Elena and the reference 28-B appeared in the same document.

But the document still did not establish that the child was the girl raised by the Valmont family.

It established only that, eighteen years earlier, someone had proposed giving an unidentified child the name Elena.

And someone had stopped that name from becoming permanent.
