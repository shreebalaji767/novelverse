# **Chapter 210 — The Source That Could Not Be Reconstructed**

St. Catherine's Maternity and Records Hospital had changed its name twice.

Its maternity department had closed eleven years earlier.

The hospital itself still operated, but its historical records were managed by a separate preservation division established after the closure.

Edward read the institutional history at 8:12.

The original hospital had been responsible for maternity care, neonatal admissions and the initial creation of birth-related medical records.

The preservation division had inherited the physical archives.

It had not inherited every authority once exercised by the hospital.

“Another separation,” Sophie said.

“Medical custody and administrative authority,” Edward replied.

He opened the contact details.

The preservation division's director was Dr Miriam Cole.

Her office had already received the request for SD-27/06.

She had agreed to a meeting, but attached a condition.

**The source description may be examined in its original form. No copy or extract may be released until the depositing authority confirms the scope of disclosure.**

Edward accepted.

At 9:40, Dr Cole appeared on the conference screen.

She was in her late fifties, with short grey hair and a stack of archive folders arranged beside her.

“I understand you're investigating several historical identity records,” she said.

“Yes. We are trying to establish the provenance of materials preserved under HIST-27/06.”

“Then I should clarify something immediately. St. Catherine's preserved medical records. It did not certify the identity of every person later associated with those records.”

“Understood.”

“And the preservation division cannot correct historical entries simply because later documents disagree with them.”

“Would you correct an entry if the original was demonstrably altered?”

“We would preserve the original, document the alteration and establish when it occurred. We would not silently replace it.”

Edward appreciated the answer.

“Can you explain SD-27/06?”

Dr Cole opened a folder.

“It is a source description prepared when certain original materials were deposited for preservation. It identifies the records as they existed at the point of transfer.”

“Does it identify the patients?”

“Some patient references appear in the original. Those are restricted.”

“Does it identify the person who deposited the materials?”

“The institutional authority is recorded. The individual depositor's identity is held separately.”

Edward looked at Sophie.

The pattern was familiar, but the distinctions mattered.

“Can we examine the document?”

“Yes. Within the approved scope.”

The original source description arrived in a secure viewing session.

It consisted of four pages.

The first page identified the depositing institution as St. Catherine's Maternity and Records Hospital.

The second described the materials.

The third listed the original seals.

The fourth contained a transfer chronology.

Edward began with the first page.

**Deposit category:** Historical maternity and neonatal identity records.

**Preservation purpose:** Retention of original source materials pending authorised identity review.

He moved to the second page.

The contents were divided into separate groups.

**Group A:** Maternity admission and delivery records.

**Group B:** Neonatal clinical records.

**Group C:** Original infant identification materials.

**Group D:** Transfer and receiving documentation.

He checked the inventory numbers.

Group A contained a record corresponding to the hospital's historical maternity index.

Group B contained clinical material associated with more than one neonatal admission.

Group C contained two original identification items.

Group D contained transfer records from different dates.

Sophie leaned closer.

“Two identification items?”

“Yes.”

“Do they have the same patient reference?”

Edward checked.

They did not.

One was associated with the hospital's original maternity register.

The other had been preserved under a separate neonatal reference.

He made a note.

**Two identification items. Separate original references. No established common identity.**

At 10:35, Edward asked Dr Cole about the transfer chronology.

“Were these materials deposited together?”

“No.”

“Why does the preservation register group them under one historical reference?”

“Because they were received under one preservation request.”

“Not because they belonged to the same patient?”

“Correct.”

Dr Cole opened the fourth page.

The first transfer had taken place on day 27.

The second had occurred on day 28.

A third set of materials had been added on day 31.

The final group had arrived on day 34.

Edward recognised the sequence.

It matched the deposit history already obtained.

“Who requested the first transfer?”

“The source description records an institutional request from the hospital's former medical records authority.”

“Was that authority also responsible for the later transfers?”

“Not necessarily. The later deposits were accepted under separate instructions.”

“Were they all authorised?”

“The preservation office accepted them for safekeeping. Acceptance did not establish that every proposed comparison or disclosure was authorised.”

Edward wrote that down.

The original hospital had transferred records.

The preservation office had protected them.

Neither action established that the records described the same child.

At 11:20, Sophie noticed a small annotation beside Group C.

**Identification item C-2 — original band material, condition incomplete.**

She pointed to it.

“Is that the band connected to IV-204?”

Dr Cole checked the restricted catalogue.

“The reference is not visible in the public inventory.”

“Can you confirm whether C-2 is IV-204?”

“I cannot disclose that through this viewing permission.”

Edward intervened.

“We aren't asking you to identify the patient. We are asking whether the source description itself establishes a link between C-2 and the hospital's maternity record.”

Dr Cole examined the page.

“No. It identifies the original reference associated with the item. It does not certify that the item belonged to a particular maternity patient.”

“Was the band altered?”

“The description says its condition was incomplete at the time of deposit.”

“Does it say when?”

“No.”

Edward looked at the annotation again.

The item could be important.

But its condition alone did not establish what had happened to it.

He requested a physical examination under the independent examiner's authority.

At 12:05, Dr Cole opened another section.

It contained a note from the former hospital records authority.

**The original maternity and neonatal records must remain separate until a competent identity review establishes the relationship, if any, between their respective references.**

Edward read it carefully.

“Who wrote this?”

“The former records authority. The signature is on the restricted original.”

“Was this instruction followed?”

“According to our preservation records, yes. The materials were stored separately.”

“Were they ever compared?”

“Not by the preservation division.”

“Could someone have compared copies elsewhere?”

“That is outside our records.”

Edward thanked her.

The note was important because it established that the separation was intentional.

Someone at St. Catherine's had understood that the maternity and neonatal records could not simply be treated as interchangeable.

But the instruction did not explain why the comparison had never occurred.

At 13:30, Elena met the grant programme coordinator for lunch.

The coordinator had brought a printed list of feedback from the pilot reviewers.

Most comments concerned minor interface improvements.

One comment was more substantial.

**Applicants should be able to see whether a submitted document is awaiting review, has been reviewed, or has been rejected as insufficient. These states should not be represented as interchangeable.**

Elena read it twice.

“I agree.”

The coordinator smiled.

“You already have those distinctions.”

“Yes, but the reviewer is asking for a clearer explanation.”

Elena opened her laptop.

She added a short description beneath each status.

**Awaiting review:** No assessment has been completed.

**Reviewed:** An authorised reviewer has submitted an assessment.

**Insufficient evidence:** The submitted material does not establish the required fact.

**Rejected:** The application has received a formal decision from the authorised decision-maker.

The coordinator studied the changes.

“You understand why we're being so particular?”

“Because a missing document isn't the same as a failed application.”

“Exactly.”

Elena saved the update.

She had begun the platform because she disliked systems in which people could not tell what had actually happened to their applications.

Now she was discovering that the same problem appeared in much larger institutions.

A record could exist without proving what people thought it proved.

At 14:15, Edward returned to the source description.

Dr Cole had authorised a closer examination of the transfer notes.

One entry had been added on day 34.

**Supplemental identity material received under restricted instruction.**

The instruction stated that the material must not be used to complete a receiving identity field without independent verification.

Edward recognised the wording from Northbridge's review.

“Was this instruction attached to the original deposit?”

“Yes.”

“Was it part of the hospital's original records?”

“No. It was supplied with the later deposit.”

“Who supplied it?”

“The depositing authority is recorded, but the individual identity remains restricted.”

Edward examined the reference.

It was not a hospital department.

It was an external institutional office.

**Restricted Personal Records Authority.**

He had seen the abbreviation before.

RPRA.

The same authority had appeared in the handwritten continuity annotation on PAO/29/02.

Sophie looked at him.

“Does that mean the same office handled both?”

“It suggests the same authority was involved in both administrative processes. It doesn't establish that it controlled the original medical records.”

Edward searched the investigation index.

The result appeared.

**RPRA/27/03 — Original Identity Material Preservation Instruction.**

Created on day 27.

The same day as the family communication.

At 15:10, Eleanor Vale joined the review again.

She read the new reference.

“RPRA/27/03 was issued before F-02,” she said.

“Can you identify the authority's role?”

“Restricted Personal Records Authority managed certain protected identity materials. It could preserve them and coordinate approved verification.”

“Could it decide who a person's family was?”

“No.”

“Could it appoint a receiving family?”

“No.”

“Could it change a hospital's original maternity record?”

“No.”

“Then why was it involved with the St. Catherine's material?”

“Possibly because the records contained protected identity information. We need the original instruction to establish the actual purpose.”

Edward asked for access.

Eleanor agreed to submit the request through the proper authority.

Before leaving, she added something.

“There is one more issue.”

“What?”

“RPRA was not the originating authority for the maternity records. It could request preservation, but it could not authorise the hospital to disclose every patient identity contained in those records.”

“So we need both authorities.”

“Yes.”

Edward looked at the growing list.

Hospital authority.

Preservation authority.

Restricted identity authority.

Independent examiner.

And eventually, a separate authority to compare the identities.

The investigation was becoming more complicated.

But the boundaries were finally becoming clearer.

At 16:05, the Aurelius family received a brief update.

Adrian listened while Edward explained the source description.

“So the hospital records were deliberately kept separate.”

“Yes.”

“And the external identity authority later requested preservation of additional material.”

“Yes.”

“Does that establish that the records concern our missing daughter?”

“No.”

Adrian nodded.

“Then we wait for the original instruction.”

Victoria looked at the document on her screen.

“Did the hospital record any missing pages?”

“Not in the accessible description.”

“Any alterations?”

“Not established. The physical examination is pending.”

Alexander folded his hands.

“Continue with the evidence. Nothing else.”

Julian had been listening quietly.

“Will they let us see the original records?”

“Not unless the relevant authority approves it,” Edward said.

Julian nodded.

He seemed disappointed, but he did not argue.

At 17:20, the preservation office sent a new message.

The physical examination had been scheduled for the following afternoon.

The examiner would inspect the original containers and verify the seals.

However, one of the originating institutions had attached a supplementary condition.

**The examination must not be treated as an identity comparison or a determination of biological relationship.**

Edward accepted the condition.

Then he noticed a second attachment.

It was a short note from the hospital's former records authority.

**The original maternity register contains a correction entry made after the initial delivery record was completed.**

Edward stopped reading.

Sophie looked at him.

“A correction?”

“Yes.”

“Do we know what was corrected?”

“No.”

He opened the note.

The correction had been recorded in the hospital's original register.

It was not described as an alteration.

It was classified as a post-entry amendment.

The reason field was restricted.

The amendment date was day 6.

Edward looked at the date again.

Day 6.

The same day that appeared in the St. Catherine's Infant A transfer history involving Gabriel.

But that was not enough to connect the records.

He opened a request for the amendment's audit history.

The system acknowledged it.

**Request accepted.**

At 18:10, Dr Cole sent one final clarification.

The correction entry had not been made by the original delivery attendant.

It had been entered by a records officer after the maternity ward had closed its initial admission file.

The original entry remained visible beneath the amendment.

No page had been removed.

No replacement page had been inserted.

The amendment had been signed and dated.

Edward read the note again.

The records were not necessarily false.

But the correction might explain why later copies differed.

He returned to the source description and opened the original register reference.

**SC-MAT/02-17.**

He knew the time associated with that reference.

2:17 a.m.

Victoria Aurelius's recorded maternity time.

He did not yet know whether the amendment concerned her entry, another patient's entry or a linked administrative field.

He requested the full amendment history without asking for the patient's identity.

The request went through.

A moment later, a final line appeared.

**Amendment reason: Receiving identification reconciliation.**

Edward stared at the words.

The correction had been made on day 6.

The hospital had not described it as a change to the birth itself.

It had described it as a reconciliation of receiving identification.

And the original entry had been preserved.

For the first time, the investigation had found a correction inside the hospital's own original register—not in a later family schedule, not in a copied summary and not in an administrative database.

Someone had changed how an infant's identity was recorded after the initial delivery entry.

The original remained.

So did the correction.

What neither record yet established was whether the correction had accurately identified the infant—or merely made the hospital's paperwork consistent with a transfer that had already taken place.
