# **Chapter 51 — The Name That Appeared Three Days Later**

Edward read the name again.

**Margaret Victoria.**

It appeared beneath Anna M.'s emergency contact information, written in blue ink three days after the girl's admission to St. Agnes Children's Recovery Centre.

The original entry had been made in black ink.

The handwriting was different.

And the instruction beneath it was unmistakable.

**Do not contact the family. The child has not been informed.**

Edward placed the scanned register beside the earlier documents.

"Could Margaret Victoria be the guardian?" Jonathan asked.

"Possibly."

"Or someone who visited after Anna was admitted."

"That's more likely."

"Why?"

"Because the original guardian field was left blank. If this person had been the legal guardian, there would have been no reason to add her name three days later in the emergency contact section."

Jonathan examined the entry.

"Then who was she?"

Edward leaned back.

"Someone who wanted to be reachable without being officially responsible."

He opened the St. Agnes closure archive.

The centre had closed seven years earlier after its medical department was merged with a larger regional institution. Its administrative records had been transferred to a records storage company.

Most of the children's files had been retained.

Some had been destroyed under the centre's ordinary retention policy.

Edward searched for Anna M.

The results were incomplete.

There were four references.

- Temporary admission.
- Educational assessment.
- Medical review.
- Transfer authorisation.

No discharge summary.

No confirmed guardian.

No final destination.

Edward looked at the fourth entry.

"Someone authorised her transfer."

Jonathan read the reference number.

"Can we identify the person?"

"Not from this register."

Edward opened the scanned transfer authorisation.

The signature section was blank.

But the approval field contained two initials.

**M.V.**

He stared at them.

"Margaret Victoria," Jonathan said.

"Or someone using those initials."

Edward saved the document.

"Let's find the person who maintained the register."

The records storage company responded the following morning.

Its archive contained the original St. Agnes administrative index, but not every individual file.

A former records clerk, Denise Parker, had agreed to speak with them.

She arrived carrying a small notebook and a folder of her own old employment documents.

"I haven't thought about St. Agnes in years," she said.

Edward showed her the register.

Denise examined the entry.

"Anna M."

"Do you remember her?"

"Not personally. I handled hundreds of children's files."

"What about this name?"

He pointed to the blue-ink addition.

"Margaret Victoria."

"Does it mean anything to you?"

She looked at it again.

"Not as a full name."

"Was it a name used by a staff member?"

"No."

"A guardian?"

"Possibly. We had several temporary contacts."

"Could you explain the procedure?"

Denise nodded.

"Children admitted without complete family documentation were assigned an internal case number. A guardian or authorised representative could be listed separately. Sometimes we received additional instructions from a legal office."

"Did those instructions come directly from the guardian?"

"Not always. Occasionally they came through an authorised representative."

"Who authorised the representative?"

"That depended on the case."

Edward placed the transfer authorisation in front of her.

"Would this have been sufficient to release a child?"

Denise studied the document.

"No."

"Why not?"

"The signature is missing. There should have been a discharge signature, a receiving person's identity, and confirmation that the child was informed of the transfer."

"Was that mandatory?"

"It was standard procedure."

"Could an exception have been made?"

Denise looked uncomfortable.

"Yes. The director could approve an exception in an urgent situation."

"Was there an emergency involving Anna?"

"I don't know."

Edward showed her the next document.

It was a photocopy of a medical review.

Denise read the heading.

Then she stopped.

"Where did you get this?"

"From the centre's archive."

She looked at him.

"This is not an ordinary medical review."

"What is it?"

"It's a safeguarding assessment."

Edward waited.

Denise turned the page.

"Someone had raised concerns about the child's identity and living arrangements."

"What concerns?"

She read the handwritten summary.

"Anna had asked about her biological mother. She had also asked why her surname did not match the name she remembered hearing from the woman who cared for her."

Edward leaned forward.

"Did she know the name?"

Denise searched the page.

"Only part of it."

"What part?"

Denise pointed to a handwritten sentence.

**The child remembers being told that her mother had another name beginning with V.**

Edward looked at Jonathan.

The letter V.

Another fragment.

"Was the assessment completed?" Edward asked.

Denise shook her head.

"It was marked for review."

"By whom?"

She turned the page.

The reviewer's name had been removed.

But the approval reference remained.

**EFO-SL/07.**

At the Aurelius Estate, Victoria was reading the latest investigation summary.

She had stopped at the same sentence three times.

**The child remembers being told that her mother had another name beginning with V.**

Her fingers rested on the paper.

"Another name beginning with V," she repeated.

Alexander sat opposite her.

"It may refer to someone unrelated."

"I know."

"Victoria."

She looked at him.

"I know we cannot assume anything."

Alexander said nothing.

Victoria returned to the document.

"How many people have used names connected to this arrangement? Margaret Voss. Margaret Ellison. Victoria A. Now Margaret Victoria."

"Several."

"And every time a child's identity appears, someone changes the name."

Alexander's expression tightened.

"Someone deliberately created a system in which no single record could establish where either child went."

Victoria looked towards the window.

"Do you think the child was ours?"

"We don't know."

"What if she was?"

"We still don't know."

Victoria closed the file.

She had spent eighteen years waiting for a daughter.

She had learned to live with uncertainty because the alternative was to spend every day imagining what might have happened.

But now the investigation was revealing something worse than uncertainty.

There might have been a child who had grown up asking for her mother.

A child who had been denied even the chance to know whether that mother was alive.

Victoria lowered her eyes.

"Alexander, I don't want them to find another child and tell her she's ours without proof."

"I agree."

"And I don't want them to frighten her with the possibility."

"We won't."

She nodded.

"Then we need to find out what happened at St. Agnes."

Alexander reached across the table and placed his hand over the report.

"We will."

At the Valmont Estate, Isabelle had discovered a problem with the scholarship programme.

Three applicants had submitted incomplete financial documentation.

One had no formal employment certificate for either parent.

Another had recently moved and could not provide the required address history.

The third had excellent academic results but had not submitted the recommendation letter.

Catherine examined the applications.

"We could reject them as incomplete."

Isabelle looked up.

"That would be easy."

"It would also be consistent with the published requirements."

"Yes, but we said the programme was supposed to help students whose circumstances made conventional applications difficult."

Catherine studied her.

"What would you suggest?"

"A clarification period. Give them five working days to provide alternative documents."

"That would delay the assessment."

"Only slightly."

"And how would we verify their circumstances?"

"Independent interviews. We can ask the review panel to examine each case separately."

Catherine considered it.

"That is reasonable."

Isabelle made a note.

"I don't want us to create exceptions for people simply because we feel sorry for them."

"Nor should we."

"But we shouldn't confuse missing paperwork with a lack of ability."

Catherine smiled faintly.

"You're learning to distinguish policy from its purpose."

Isabelle looked at the applications again.

"I suppose that's what I want to learn."

Catherine leaned back.

"Then keep asking questions."

For a moment, Isabelle looked almost surprised.

She had expected to be told what to do.

Instead, Catherine was allowing her to make decisions, explain them, and defend them.

It was unfamiliar.

But she liked it.

Elena's Singapore interview was two days away.

She had arranged her documents in a folder and checked the university's instructions twice.

Nicholas found her at the dining table, surrounded by papers.

"Are you preparing for an interview or moving countries?"

"Both, potentially."

He sat opposite her.

"How many copies do you need?"

"Three."

"Why three?"

"One for me, one spare, and one in case the first two disappear."

Nicholas laughed.

"That's excessive."

"That's preparation."

He picked up her written statement.

"Can I read it?"

Elena hesitated, then handed it over.

Nicholas read silently.

The statement was concise.

She had written about her interest in business management, her desire to understand how large organisations operated, and her hope of developing practical skills.

She had also written about the importance of financial responsibility and independent decision-making.

Nicholas reached the final paragraph.

"You're not mentioning the Valmont Group."

"No."

"Not even as an example?"

"I don't want the interview to become about our family."

He nodded.

"Fair enough."

Elena took the paper back.

"I want them to assess me."

Nicholas smiled.

"Then let them."

He stood.

"By the way, Sebastian has arranged a mock interview tomorrow."

Elena looked up.

"Without asking me?"

"He asked me to tell you."

"That's not the same thing."

Nicholas grinned.

"Welcome to the family."

Elena threw a folded paper at him.

He caught it before it reached the floor.

Edward received the original St. Agnes safeguarding assessment late that afternoon.

The document had been stored separately from Anna's medical file.

Its cover carried a restricted-access classification.

**CASE: A.M.**

**REASON FOR REVIEW: IDENTITY AND GUARDIANSHIP DISCREPANCY.**

The first page contained the child's own statement.

It had been written down by a social worker.

*I don't know if the woman who looks after me is my real mother.*

*She says she is not.*

*She says my mother knows where I am.*

*But when I ask when my mother is coming, she says she doesn't know.*

*I want to know whether my mother is alive.*

Edward read the statement slowly.

The girl had been eleven.

She had not asked for money, a different home, or a new family.

She had asked whether her mother was alive.

The next section contained the social worker's assessment.

*The child appears emotionally stable but demonstrates persistent anxiety concerning family identity. She has been given inconsistent explanations about her parentage. Her current guardian has declined to provide additional information.*

The recommendation was clear.

**A formal identity review should be initiated, with the child's participation and appropriate legal safeguards.**

But the recommendation had never been carried out.

The final page contained a handwritten decision.

**Review suspended pending instruction from originating authority.**

Signed:

**M. Voss.**

Edward stared at the signature.

This time, the handwriting matched the disputed authorisation from the hospital.

Not Martha Voss.

Not Margaret Ellison.

The other Margaret.

Margaret Voss.

Jonathan read the page over his shoulder.

"So she was still involved eleven years later."

"Yes."

"Despite leaving the hospital eighteen days after the births."

"Apparently, her authority continued through the Family Office."

Jonathan pointed to the date.

"Who authorised the suspension?"

Edward examined the approval line.

The name had been cut away.

But there was a second signature beneath the paper.

A faint impression.

Edward turned the page under the desk lamp.

The impression became clearer.

**C. VALE.**

Charles Vale had personally stopped the identity review.

The next morning, Edward contacted Marcus Morelli.

Morelli agreed to meet at the same neutral office where they had previously discussed the ninth-day release.

He arrived carrying a leather folder.

Edward placed the St. Agnes assessment on the table.

Morelli read the child's statement.

His expression changed.

"Where did you find this?"

"St. Agnes."

Morelli continued reading.

When he reached the handwritten suspension, he closed the folder.

"Did you know about this assessment?" Edward asked.

"No."

"Margaret Voss signed the suspension."

"Then she was still operating under the authority of EFO-SL/07."

"Who gave her that authority?"

Morelli did not answer immediately.

"Charles Vale."

"Why?"

"Because he believed the original arrangement was still necessary."

"Necessary for whom?"

Morelli looked at him.

"That was the question I asked him."

"And?"

"He said the child had to remain protected until the mother could be approached."

Edward opened the document.

"Eleven years later, the child was asking whether her mother was alive."

Morelli looked down.

"I know."

Edward went still.

"You knew?"

Morelli nodded slowly.

"I received a letter from Margaret Voss."

"Why didn't you tell us?"

"Because the letter didn't contain the child's identity. It only said that the arrangement had become difficult to maintain."

"Where is the letter?"

Morelli opened his folder and removed a folded page.

"I kept it."

Edward unfolded it.

The letter was dated two months after Anna's admission to St. Agnes.

*Marcus,*

*The child has requested her original records.*

*The centre has recommended an identity review. Charles has refused it.*

*I do not believe the original instruction can continue indefinitely. The girl is old enough to understand that something is being withheld from her.*

*I have asked for permission to disclose the truth in stages.*

*The request has been denied.*

*I need to know whether the mother is still being kept away deliberately, or whether nobody has made an effort to locate her.*

There was no signature.

Only the reference:

**M.V.**

Edward looked up.

"Did you respond?"

"No."

"Why?"

"Because I had no authority to change the arrangement."

"You had enough authority to carry out the original release."

"That was different."

"How?"

Morelli looked directly at him.

"Because I was instructed to release two infants. I was not instructed to decide what they should be told eleven years later."

Edward held his gaze.

"Did you ever meet Anna?"

"No."

"Did you know which infant she was?"

Morelli shook his head.

"Then why did you keep the letter?"

"Because Margaret was right."

"About what?"

Morelli's voice was quiet.

"That the arrangement could not continue forever."

Edward folded the letter.

"Where is Margaret Voss now?"

"I don't know."

"Have you spoken to her since?"

"Once."

"When?"

"Seventeen years ago."

Edward looked up.

"That's only a year after the births."

Morelli nodded.

"What did she say?"

"That she had been instructed to leave the Family Office records untouched. She was no longer permitted to access the children's files."

"By whom?"

"Charles Vale."

Edward placed the letter beside the assessment.

"Did she leave voluntarily?"

Morelli hesitated.

"No."

The room became quiet.

"She was dismissed?"

"Not formally. Her secondment ended, but she wasn't allowed to return to her original position. She was given a settlement and told to sign a confidentiality agreement."

"Did she sign it?"

Morelli nodded.

"Yes."

Edward looked at the signature.

"Then why did she keep writing letters?"

Morelli looked towards the window.

"Because she had started to believe the child was being harmed by the very secrecy that was supposed to protect her."

Later that evening, Edward received another message from the St. Agnes archive.

The institution had retained one additional document.

A visitor register from the week after Anna's safeguarding assessment.

Three visitors had been recorded.

The first was a social worker.

The second was a representative from Vale Private Counsel.

The third had signed only a surname.

**AURELIUS.**

Edward opened the scanned page.

There was no first name.

No address.

No identification number.

Only a time.

**3:20 P.M.**

And beneath the entry, someone had written:

**Visitor requested to see the child. Access refused under existing instruction.**

Edward read it twice.

Someone from the Aurelius family had attempted to visit Anna.

But the visitor had been turned away.

He called Jonathan.

"We have another lead."

"What is it?"

"An Aurelius visitor at St. Agnes."

Jonathan went silent.

"Which Aurelius?"

"The register doesn't say."

"Could it have been Alexander?"

"Possibly."

"Victoria?"

"Possibly."

"One of the sons?"

Edward looked at the date.

It was eleven years after the births.

Adrian would have been seventeen.

Lucien fourteen.

Damien eleven.

Julian five.

"Not all of them," Edward said. "We need the visitor records."

He opened the final page.

There was a note written in the margin.

**The visitor was informed that the child had been transferred. No further contact was authorised.**

Edward slowly lowered the document.

The visitor had come looking for a child.

The institution had refused access.

And someone had told that visitor the child was no longer there.

But Anna's file showed that she remained at St. Agnes for another six weeks.

Someone had deliberately sent the visitor away.

And for the first time, the investigation suggested that the Aurelius family might not have been searching only for a missing newborn.

Someone had tried to find a child years later.

Someone had reached the right institution.

And someone had made sure they never met her.
