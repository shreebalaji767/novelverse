# **Chapter 122 — The Woman Charles Vale Called Anna**

The name appeared in eleven documents.

Edward had expected more.

After nearly eighteen years of correspondence, administrative transfers, private instructions, and restricted medical records, eleven references seemed surprisingly few.

But each reference used the same name.

**Anna.**

None included a surname.

Jonathan arranged the documents chronologically.

“First appearance: day 10. Private correspondence, V-17.”

“Second?”

“Day 17. A note attached to the restricted correspondence envelope received by Victoria Aurelius.”

Edward looked at the second document.

The note had warned that Isabella Vale's IV-204 band must not be treated as proof of identity.

It had been signed by an unknown sender.

“Was this the same woman?”

“We don't know.”

“Continue.”

Jonathan moved down the list.

“Day 28. Meridian visitor register. Day 34. Private Instruction 07 correspondence. Day 40. Conditional response delivery. Day 61. Meridian preservation transfer.”

He paused.

“Then nothing until day 118.”

Edward looked up.

“What happened on day 118?”

“Eleanor's review note. It refers to an unnamed external correspondent who challenged the use of Schedule 4.”

Edward leaned forward.

“Show me.”

Jonathan placed the document before him.

The handwritten note was short.

*The proposed allocation cannot be treated as confirmed merely because the receiving party has been approached. The original family must be identified before any further notification.*

At the bottom:

**A. — Personal correspondence.**

Edward read it again.

“Anna?”

“Possibly.”

“Or someone else using the same initial.”

Jonathan nodded.

The investigation had reached a point where names were becoming dangerous.

A single repeated word could create an entire false identity.

Edward closed the folder.

“We need to establish whether Anna was a person, an administrative alias, or a name used by Charles for several different people.”

The first person they contacted was Margaret Ellison.

She had once cared for a baby she called Anna.

The similarity was too important to ignore.

Margaret agreed to a meeting at her residence.

She was older now, but her memory remained sharp.

Edward placed the day-40 correspondence reference before her.

“Do you recognise this name?”

Margaret read it.

“Anna.”

“Was that the name you gave the child you cared for?”

“Yes.”

“Did you ever use that name in correspondence with Charles Vale?”

“No.”

“Did you know another woman called Anna?”

Margaret hesitated.

“I knew a woman who sometimes introduced herself that way.”

Edward's attention sharpened.

“When?”

“After the baby was taken from me.”

“Can you describe her?”

“She was around my age, perhaps a little younger. Dark hair. Quiet voice. She came twice.”

“Where?”

“At the temporary residence where I was staying.”

“Did she say why she was there?”

“She said she wanted to know whether the child had been placed safely.”

“Did she claim to be the child's mother?”

“No.”

“Did she say she was related to the child?”

“No.”

Margaret looked down.

“She asked whether I had been told the child's real name.”

“What did you say?”

“I told her I had been instructed not to ask.”

Edward waited.

Margaret continued.

“She seemed upset by that.”

“Did she tell you her real name?”

“No.”

“Did she carry anything?”

“A brown leather case.”

Edward exchanged a glance with Jonathan.

“Was it the same woman who later visited V-17?”

“I cannot say. I saw her briefly.”

“Did she ever mention the Aurelius family?”

“No.”

“The Valmont family?”

“No.”

“Did she mention a hospital?”

Margaret nodded.

“She asked whether the baby had come from St Catherine's.”

Edward went still.

“What did you tell her?”

“I said I didn't know.”

“Did she explain why she was asking?”

Margaret shook her head.

“She only said that if I didn't know where the child had come from, I shouldn't let anyone tell me who the child belonged to.”

Edward wrote the sentence down.

It was not proof.

But it was consistent with the repeated warnings in the correspondence.

Margaret looked at him.

“Was that woman involved in the child's disappearance?”

“We don't know.”

“Did she take the baby?”

“No evidence suggests that.”

Margaret's expression tightened.

“Then please don't make her responsible for something simply because she asked questions.”

Edward nodded.

“We won't.”

The second reference came from a former employee of Vale Private Counsel.

Her name was Helen Ward.

She had worked in the correspondence department and remembered several unusual files.

Edward showed her the list.

“Do you remember anyone referred to as Anna?”

Helen studied the dates.

“Not as a client.”

“Could it have been an employee?”

“Not on the official staff list.”

“An external representative?”

“Possibly.”

“Did the name appear in your correspondence?”

Helen pointed to day 34.

“This one.”

The document was a routing instruction.

**Personal correspondence to be received through Anna. Do not include name in ordinary distribution list.**

Edward looked at her.

“Who wrote this?”

“I believe it came from Charles Vale's office.”

“Did you ever meet Anna?”

“No. But I remember a disagreement about this instruction.”

“What disagreement?”

“The correspondence department wanted a proper sender identification. We were told that the external representative had already been verified.”

“By whom?”

“Eleanor Vale.”

Edward paused.

“Did Eleanor verify the person personally?”

“I don't know. The instruction only said verification complete.”

“Was a certificate attached?”

Helen searched her old notes.

“There was a reference to one.”

“Which reference?”

**AV-18/IDENTITY-02.**

Edward wrote it down.

“Where is the certificate?”

Helen shook her head.

“I don't know. It wasn't in the general correspondence file.”

“Was it kept separately?”

“Yes. I remember being told not to duplicate the identity certificate.”

Edward looked at Jonathan.

The same instruction had appeared repeatedly.

Do not duplicate.

Keep the original separate.

Preserve the restricted record.

It had been intended to prevent unverified information from spreading.

But it had also made the investigation extraordinarily difficult.

The AV-18/IDENTITY-02 reference led them to an old document inventory at Meridian.

The inventory contained a description:

**External intermediary identity verification.**

**Status: preserved.**

**Original custodian: Eleanor Vale.**

Edward contacted Eleanor.

She recognised the reference immediately.

“I have seen that document,” she said.

“Do you still have it?”

“Yes.”

“Can we examine it?”

Eleanor agreed.

The certificate was stored in a separate envelope within her personal correspondence archive.

Its exterior read:

**AV-18/IDENTITY-02 — RESTRICTED**

The seal had been broken previously, but the document itself remained intact.

Dr Sen examined it.

The certificate contained a photograph, a legal name, and a professional verification statement.

Edward studied the photograph.

It showed a woman in her late forties at the time, with dark hair and a narrow face.

The name was clear.

**Anna Mercer.**

Jonathan looked up.

“Mercer?”

Edward did not respond immediately.

The name appeared familiar for another reason.

Daniel Mercer had been the former supervisor who altered the OR-28 status wording through the shared review desk.

But there was no evidence of a family relationship.

A shared surname meant nothing by itself.

Edward turned the certificate over.

The verification statement had been completed by Eleanor.

**Identity verified in person. Authority limited to protected correspondence and document transport. No authority to determine medical identity, accept placement, or represent any receiving family.**

Edward read it twice.

“Was she the woman who delivered the day-40 envelope?”

Eleanor nodded.

“Yes.”

“Was she also the woman who visited Margaret Ellison?”

“I don't know.”

“Did she have any connection to the hospital?”

“She was employed as a temporary records liaison at St Catherine's during the relevant period.”

Edward looked up.

That was the first direct institutional connection.

“Which department?”

“Medical records and patient administration.”

“Did she handle neonatal files?”

“I cannot confirm that.”

“Did she have access to the original maternity records?”

“She had restricted access to records awaiting transfer.”

Edward studied the photograph again.

“Why was she given a protected correspondence role?”

Eleanor took a moment.

“Because she had seen records that should not have been separated from their original context.”

“What records?”

“I was not told.”

“Did she explain?”

“She said that two different identity references were being treated as though they belonged to the same child.”

Edward's expression sharpened.

“Which references?”

“She did not name them.”

“Did she mention IV-204?”

“No.”

“STC-A?”

“No.”

“Child 28-B?”

“No.”

Eleanor shook her head.

“She was very careful not to disclose patient information outside her authority.”

Edward looked at the certificate.

Anna Mercer had been connected to St Catherine's.

She had been verified by Eleanor.

She had delivered a conditional response through V-17.

But none of that established that she had been responsible for transferring a child.

It established that she had access to the administrative environment in which the records were being handled.

That was enough to justify further investigation.

At the Aurelius Estate, Victoria read Edward's latest update.

**Anna Mercer — former temporary records liaison at St Catherine's. Identity verified by Eleanor Vale. Authority limited to protected correspondence and document transport. No confirmed role in child transfer or placement.**

Victoria set the report down.

“St Catherine's.”

Alexander looked up from his own papers.

“Yes.”

“That's where my maternity record originated.”

“It is also where Isabella Vale's separate maternity record was held.”

Victoria nodded.

“Does this mean Anna knew which child was mine?”

“No evidence establishes that.”

“Did she know about both records?”

“Possibly. Her former role gave her access to restricted correspondence, but we don't yet know what she saw.”

Victoria looked at the photograph.

The woman appeared ordinary.

A professional employee.

Someone who might have passed unnoticed through a hospital corridor.

“Why would she become involved in private family correspondence?”

Alexander folded his hands.

“That is what Edward is investigating.”

Victoria looked toward the window.

“If she knew that two children had been confused, why didn't she tell us?”

Alexander did not answer immediately.

Because the answer could be simple.

She might not have known the truth.

Or she might have been prevented from speaking.

Or she might have believed that revealing incomplete information would create another mistake.

Victoria finally looked back at him.

“I don't want another person to tell us what they think happened.”

Alexander nodded.

“We need what they can prove.”

Three days later, Edward and Jonathan visited St Catherine's successor records office.

The hospital's original medical records had been partly transferred to a regional archive after its administrative restructuring.

Anna Mercer's employment file was still available.

Her appointment began six months before the births.

Her position was listed as:

**Temporary Records Liaison — Restricted Patient Administration.**

Her duties included:

- Transfer documentation.
- Restricted correspondence.
- Verification of receiving-office acknowledgements.
- Preservation of original patient identification material.

She had not been a doctor or midwife.

She had not been authorised to make clinical decisions.

Her access to neonatal records was limited to administrative transfer files.

Edward examined her final employment record.

Her contract ended on day 19.

No formal resignation letter was attached.

No disciplinary action was recorded.

The reason for departure was simply:

**Assignment concluded.**

Jonathan looked at the date.

“Day 19.”

The same day the initial recipient schedule had been acknowledged at R-04.

Edward nodded.

“And the day after the private correspondence arrangements began.”

They requested her personnel exit interview.

The archive produced a scanned copy.

It contained three short answers.

**Reason for leaving: Personal circumstances.**

**Outstanding institutional property: None.**

**Further correspondence: Through external legal representative.**

The final line had been handwritten.

**Do not contact former employee directly regarding protected neonatal matters.**

Edward stared at it.

“Who wrote that?”

The signature field was blank.

Dr Sen later examined the handwriting.

It did not match Charles Vale, Eleanor Vale, Gabriel Vale, or Margaret Voss.

It was also too short for reliable attribution.

But the paper contained a reference.

**VPC / STAFF EXIT — RESTRICTED**

The instruction had come from outside the hospital.

Someone had requested that Anna Mercer not be contacted directly about the neonatal files.

Edward requested her current address through the employment archive.

The response arrived two days later.

Anna Mercer was alive.

She had moved twice, changed her professional name after leaving St Catherine's, and now lived in a small town outside the capital.

Her current name was listed as:

**Anna Mercer-Smith.**

Jonathan checked the address.

“She's still there.”

“Contact her.”

“Should we send the documents first?”

“No. Send a neutral request for a voluntary interview. Don't include the children's names.”

Jonathan nodded.

The letter was sent.

A response arrived the following afternoon.

It was handwritten.

**I will meet you, but I will not discuss the identity of any child until I have seen the original records. I have been asked questions about those records before. Each time, the documents presented to me were incomplete.**

At the bottom was a signature.

**Anna Mercer-Smith.**

Edward read it.

“She knows the records were incomplete.”

Jonathan looked at the letter.

“Or she wants to see whether we have the originals.”

“Either way, we will bring them.”

Edward began preparing a limited document set.

The original St Catherine's Infant A card.

The IV-204 maternity sheet.

The Meridian transfer acknowledgement.

The day-40 conditional response.

And the AV-18 identity verification certificate.

He would not bring every document.

Some contained private information unrelated to the question.

The meeting needed to establish what Anna had actually seen, not overwhelm her with every theory the investigation had produced.

At the Valmont Estate, Elena's first message from Singapore arrived late in the evening.

A photograph showed a university building with a wide glass entrance and a landscaped courtyard.

The accompanying message was simple.

**Arrived safely. The campus is enormous. I think I need a map just to find the library.**

Nicholas replied first.

**Find the library before you find the shopping district.**

Catherine sent a message asking whether the accommodation was comfortable.

Sebastian asked whether the programme coordinator had confirmed the first orientation session.

Isabelle sent a photograph of the revised foundation proposal.

**I made the changes. Tell me what you think when you have time.**

Elena read every message.

Then she opened her notebook.

The first page contained a list.

**Things to do in Singapore.**

1. Complete university registration.
2. Attend programme orientation.
3. Find the library.
4. Set up a reliable study routine.
5. Test the grant management prototype.
6. Explore the campus.
7. Learn the public transport system.
8. Find affordable groceries.

She added another line.

9\. Build a life that belongs to me.

Then she closed the notebook.

For now, that was enough.

Edward's meeting with Anna Mercer-Smith was scheduled for the following week.

Before leaving, he received one final message from her.

It contained no explanation.

Only a request.

**Please bring the original neonatal identification card, not the later administrative copy. The difference matters.**

Edward read the message twice.

She had specifically asked for the original Infant A card.

Not the IV-204 maternity sheet.

Not the alternate recipient schedule.

Not the later placement records.

The original neonatal identification card.

He looked at the file on his desk.

The card had been withheld from comparison on day six.

It had been removed from St Catherine's on day ten.

It had passed through Northbridge, Gabriel Vale, Adrian Holt, and the restricted preservation system.

It had eventually been recovered from the archive.

And now Anna Mercer wanted to see it.

Edward called Jonathan.

“Prepare the original card for the meeting.”

“Do you think she knows what it proves?”

“No.”

He paused.

“But she may know why it was never compared.”

And that distinction could change the direction of the entire investigation.
