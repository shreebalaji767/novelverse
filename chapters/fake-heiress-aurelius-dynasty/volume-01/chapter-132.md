# **Chapter 132 — The Office That Controlled a Voice**

Edward read the final instruction again.

**Personal representation to remain under the control of the originating office until further notice.**

The phrase sounded administrative.

Almost harmless.

But its consequences were not.

Someone had decided that P-04's personal representation would remain under an office's control, even though the protected principal had not been contacted directly.

The substitute participant had been withdrawn.

The receiving authority remained vacant.

And the principal's own response had never been obtained.

Edward placed the instruction beside the original PR-06 acknowledgement form.

“Jonathan, find every reference to personal representation in the records.”

Jonathan opened a search across the recovered archive.

“Do you want only PI-07?”

“Start there. Then search the original correspondence and the Family Office registers separately. I don't want a later summary deciding what the original wording meant.”

Jonathan began.

Edward returned to the instruction.

The document carried three markings:

**PI-07**

**P-04**

**ORIGINATING OFFICE CONTROL**

The third marking was not a signature.

It was a classification.

He wondered whether it referred to an actual department, a legal custodian, or simply a procedural designation.

The answer mattered.

If the originating office had only been responsible for preserving communication, it could not legitimately prevent the principal from receiving information.

If it had been given broader authority, the original appointment would have to exist somewhere.

And if no such appointment existed, the instruction might have been used beyond its intended purpose.

The first useful result came from the Family Office archive.

Jonathan found a day-34 administrative chart.

It listed the different authorities involved in the protected identity process.

**Originating medical authority:** St Catherine's Medical Records.

**Originating family representative:** Victoria Aurelius or Isabella Vale, depending on the maternal record concerned.

**Medical verification authority:** Dr Samuel Hale.

**Receiving identity custodian:** Not appointed.

**Communication intermediary:** Marianne Cross.

**Protected principal communication representative:** Gabriel Vale, limited authority.

**Personal representation custodian:** Pending designation.

Edward examined the final entry.

“Personal representation custodian.”

Jonathan enlarged the chart.

“There was supposed to be a separate custodian.”

“Was it ever appointed?”

“I can't find an appointment.”

“Who proposed the role?”

“The chart references a request from Vale Private Counsel.”

“Which request?”

Jonathan followed the reference.

**VPC/PR-06/ORIGINATING CONTROL — DAY 34.**

Edward leaned forward.

“Open it.”

The request was short.

It proposed that a personal representation custodian be appointed to preserve communications between P-04 and the receiving identity process.

The custodian's duties were described as:

- Maintain the principal's original response.
- Ensure that information presented to the principal was complete.
- Prevent a representative's conditional response from being treated as personal consent.
- Preserve the principal's right to request further information.
- Transfer the original response only under verified authority.

Edward read the list carefully.

The proposed role appeared designed to protect P-04.

But the final paragraph was different.

*Pending formal designation, the originating office shall retain control of all personal representation material.*

“Who was the originating office?” Jonathan asked.

“That is what the request should tell us.”

The designation field was blank.

There was no named department.

No responsible officer.

No appointment date.

Only a handwritten reference:

**Temporary custody accepted — C.V.**

Edward recognised the initials.

Charles Vale.

Thomas Greer agreed to meet them again.

When Edward showed him the day-34 request, Greer examined it for several minutes.

“I remember this proposal,” he said.

“Did you draft it?”

“I reviewed it. I don't believe I prepared the first version.”

“Who did?”

“I cannot identify the original author.”

“Did Charles Vale accept temporary custody?”

“He accepted preservation responsibility for the material.”

“Did he accept control over P-04's personal representation?”

Greer looked at the final paragraph.

“Not as I understood the arrangement.”

“Then what did he accept?”

“Custody of the documents until a formal custodian was appointed.”

“Could he decide whether P-04 was allowed to receive information?”

“No.”

“Could he appoint Gabriel Vale as a permanent representative?”

“No.”

“Could he prevent P-04 from responding?”

“Not under this document.”

Edward placed the day-46 instruction beside it.

“Then how do you explain this?”

Greer examined the wording.

“That's not an appointment. It is a later administrative instruction.”

“Did you author it?”

“No.”

“Did you approve it?”

“No.”

“Did you see it before the substitute nomination was withdrawn?”

Greer looked at the reference.

“Not that I recall.”

“Who could issue an instruction under originating office control?”

Greer paused.

“Technically, the temporary custodian could issue preservation instructions concerning the documents.”

“Could the temporary custodian control the principal's communication?”

“No. That would require a separate authority.”

“Was one ever granted?”

“I have not seen one.”

Edward closed the folder.

“Then someone treated temporary document custody as personal representation authority.”

Greer did not disagree.

But he added something.

“Mr Valmont, I want you to consider another possibility.”

Edward waited.

“The instruction may have been intended to prevent an unauthorised representative from contacting P-04. That would not automatically mean someone intended to silence the principal.”

“Who was the unauthorised representative?”

“I don't know.”

“Was Gabriel authorised?”

“Only within the limited scope of his communication appointment.”

“Was Susan Bell?”

“No.”

“Was anyone else?”

Greer looked at the document.

“That is the question.”

At the Aurelius Estate, Alexander was reviewing the latest investigation summary when Edward arrived.

Victoria sat beside him, reading the original day-46 notice.

Edward explained the new finding.

When he finished, Victoria looked at the phrase *personal representation custodian*.

“Why would someone need a separate custodian for a person's response?”

“To ensure that the principal's words were preserved independently of the people arranging the receiving process,” Edward replied.

Victoria considered that.

“Then the idea itself wasn't unreasonable.”

“No. The problem is that no one was appointed.”

“And Charles accepted temporary custody?”

“According to the record.”

Alexander's expression hardened.

“Charles had custody of the documents, but not authority over the person.”

“That is the distinction we're trying to establish.”

Victoria looked down at the day-46 instruction.

“Was P-04 ever told that someone was holding their correspondence?”

“We don't know.”

“Was the principal told that Gabriel had been appointed to communicate on their behalf?”

“We have no proof of that either.”

Victoria was silent for a moment.

Then she asked, “Could P-04 have been waiting for someone to contact them?”

Edward did not answer immediately.

It was possible.

The protected principal might have known about the proposed arrangement and refused to respond.

They might have been unaware of it entirely.

They might have received some information but not enough to understand what was being asked.

The records could not distinguish between those possibilities.

“We shouldn't assume silence means refusal,” he said.

Victoria nodded.

“Nor should we assume it means agreement.”

Alexander looked at the recovered instruction.

“Find the original appointment record. Until we know who controlled the correspondence, we cannot understand why the principal's acknowledgement disappeared.”

In Singapore, Elena had begun preparing the written report for her project.

She wanted the final version to explain not only how the platform worked, but why each permission existed.

Her team had initially treated access control as a technical feature.

Elena had insisted it was also a responsibility.

A reviewer could assess an application.

An administrator could manage the system.

A committee could approve funding.

But none of those roles automatically allowed one person to perform the others.

She had added a section titled:

**Authority Is Not Transferred by Convenience.**

Daniel read it over her shoulder.

“That's a strong heading.”

“It needs to be.”

“Are you planning to include an example?”

“An ordinary one. Someone receiving a document doesn't mean they can approve it.”

Daniel nodded.

“That's probably the most important part of the system.”

Elena saved the report.

She had no idea how closely the principle resembled a problem unfolding thousands of kilometres away.

The next morning, Jonathan found a second archive reference.

It was not in the Family Office system.

It came from a private correspondence register maintained by Vale Private Counsel.

**PR-06 — PERSONAL REPRESENTATION MATERIAL**

**Custody transfer:** Day 34.

**Temporary custodian:** Charles Vale.

**Original personal response:** Not received.

**Communication representative:** Gabriel Vale.

**Personal representation custodian:** Pending.

There was also a note dated day 39.

*Separate preservation required. Do not permit administrative continuity to be recorded as principal acknowledgement.*

Edward read it.

“Who wrote this?”

Jonathan enlarged the handwriting.

“It resembles Eleanor Vale's.”

“Can we confirm?”

“Not yet.”

The note was followed by another entry, dated day 43.

*Original personal response remains outstanding. Representative's conditional response retained separately.*

This one carried Eleanor's initials.

E.V.

Edward leaned back.

Eleanor had repeatedly tried to keep the principal's personal response separate from administrative records.

She had also objected when Gabriel's conditional response was treated as consent.

But she had not opened the protected instruction.

She had not contacted P-04.

And she had not been appointed as personal representation custodian.

“Ask Eleanor whether she knew who held temporary custody,” Edward said.

Eleanor Vale received them in her office.

She read the day-34 request and immediately recognised it.

“I remember this.”

“Did you write the note on day 39?” Edward asked.

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“Because the Family Office was beginning to treat Gabriel's response as sufficient for administrative continuity.”

“Did you believe that was improper?”

“I believed it was incomplete.”

“Did you know Charles had accepted temporary custody?”

“Yes.”

“Did you believe he had authority to control P-04's communication?”

“No.”

“Did you tell him that?”

“I did.”

“What did he say?”

Eleanor folded her hands.

“He said the documents had to remain in one place until the receiving authority was appointed. He believed separate custody arrangements would create confusion.”

“Did he say he could decide whether P-04 was contacted?”

“No.”

“Did he ever claim to have personal authority from P-04?”

Eleanor paused.

“Not to me.”

Edward placed the day-46 instruction before her.

“Do you recognise this?”

She examined the wording.

“I have seen the reference.”

“Did you author it?”

“No.”

“Did you approve it?”

“No.”

“Did you know Susan Bell's nomination had been withdrawn?”

“I knew she had not been appointed. I did not know a formal nomination had been withdrawn.”

“Did you know the substitute request existed?”

Eleanor looked up.

“No.”

Edward watched her expression.

“You didn't know someone had proposed an alternate participant?”

“No. The first time I saw the reference was in the archive extract you sent yesterday.”

“Then who could have prepared the instruction?”

Eleanor returned her attention to the page.

“Someone with access to the personal representation file.”

“Who had that access?”

“Charles, the Personal Instructions Division, and the legal custodian. Gabriel had limited access to correspondence relating to his representative role.”

“Could Gabriel issue a control instruction?”

“No.”

“Could Charles?”

“Not without a separate appointment.”

Edward placed the day-34 request beside the day-46 instruction.

Eleanor studied the two documents.

“The wording has changed.”

“How?”

“The original request says the temporary custodian is responsible for preserving material. This one says the originating office controls personal representation.”

“Could that have been an administrative interpretation?”

“Possibly. But it is not what the original request authorised.”

She looked at Edward.

“Someone converted custody of documents into control over a person's communication.”

Edward was about to leave when Eleanor spoke again.

“There is something I should tell you.”

He turned.

“Go ahead.”

“On day 52, I requested a complete inventory of the personal representation file.”

“Why?”

“Because I had discovered that the original acknowledgement form was being held separately from Gabriel's response. I wanted to confirm that no personal communication had been lost.”

“Did you receive the inventory?”

“Yes.”

“Was it complete?”

“No.”

“What was missing?”

Eleanor opened a locked cabinet and removed a thin folder.

“I retained the request and the response.”

She placed two sheets on the table.

The first was her day-52 inventory request.

The second was a reply from the Personal Instructions Division.

It listed the contents of the file:

- PR-06 appointment extract.
- Gabriel Vale communication authority.
- Conditional response.
- Principal acknowledgement form.
- PI-07 instruction.
- Day-46 joint custody review record.

Edward scanned the list.

“That's everything we've already found.”

“Not quite.”

Eleanor pointed to the final line.

**Personal representation correspondence — held separately under sealed custody.**

Edward looked at her.

“Was there a separate correspondence file?”

“Yes.”

“Where was it kept?”

“The inventory did not say.”

“Did you ask?”

“I did.”

“What happened?”

Eleanor placed another document before him.

It was a reply dated day 54.

*The correspondence file remains under the originating office's personal custody instruction. No access permitted pending completion of the receiving authority appointment.*

Edward read the sentence.

“Did you challenge it?”

“I asked who had appointed the originating office.”

“And?”

“There was no response.”

Edward examined the bottom of the page.

A small reference appeared beneath the signature block.

**PO-04 / PERSONAL CORRESPONDENCE — UNIT V-17.**

He looked up.

“V-17.”

Eleanor nodded.

“The correspondence was held separately from the identity file.”

“Did you ever inspect it?”

“No. I did not have the authority to open it.”

Edward gathered the documents.

The original personal response might not be missing.

It might have been stored separately all along.

But if it had been preserved, someone knew where it was.

And someone had repeatedly refused to release it.

At the Aurelius Estate that evening, Victoria received Edward's message.

**We have located a separate personal correspondence file associated with P-04. It was held under temporary custody at V-17. We are seeking the original inventory and transfer records.**

She read it twice.

Then she turned to Alexander.

“They may have the person's actual words.”

“Possibly.”

“Not Gabriel's response. Not an administrative summary. The principal's own correspondence.”

“Yes.”

Victoria held the phone in both hands.

For eighteen years, she had searched for a child whose identity remained uncertain.

She had learned to distinguish hope from evidence.

But this was different.

Somewhere in the records was a person whose own answer had never reached the people making decisions.

She did not know who that person was.

She did not know whether the person was a child, a parent, or someone else with a protected role in the arrangement.

She only knew that the answer mattered.

“Alexander,” she said quietly, “if that correspondence still exists, I want it preserved exactly as it is.”

“It will be.”

Edward and Jonathan returned to V-17 the following morning.

The west-corridor room had been cleared years earlier, but the property archive retained its original inventory.

The personal correspondence file appeared under a separate storage code.

**PO-04.**

The transfer history showed three movements.

Day 34 — received from Vale Private Counsel.

Day 61 — transferred to restricted west-corridor storage.

Day 175 — collected under Private Instruction 07 preservation authority.

The last entry was unusual.

The collection receipt did not name the person who took it.

Instead, it recorded:

**Collection authorised by C.V.**

**Physical recipient: Personal correspondence custodian.**

**Identity: Sealed.**

Edward read the final line.

“Who was the personal correspondence custodian?”

Jonathan checked the linked appointment register.

“There isn't one.”

“Then who collected the file?”

“Someone signed for it under the sealed identity procedure.”

“Can we identify the signature?”

“Not from this copy.”

Edward examined the receipt.

A small note had been added in the margin.

*Do not return to general custody. The principal's original communication must remain separate from all receiving identity material.*

The handwriting was familiar.

Eleanor's.

But the receipt had been issued after her day-61 preservation review.

Edward turned the page.

Behind the collection receipt was a second document.

It was a storage instruction dated day 175.

**PO-04 / PERSONAL CORRESPONDENCE**

**Contents:** Original principal communication, if received; subsequent correspondence; delivery acknowledgements.

**Access:** Personal correspondence custodian only.

**Release condition:** Verified identity of principal and independent confirmation of authority.

At the bottom, someone had written:

*The original response has not been delivered.*

Edward looked at Jonathan.

“Read that again.”

Jonathan did.

The original response had not been delivered.

Not lost.

Not destroyed.

Not necessarily never written.

It had not been delivered.

Edward examined the handwriting.

The note was unsigned.

But its date was clear.

Day 175.

The same day the file had been removed from V-17.

He looked towards the empty west-corridor room.

For months, they had been searching for the authority that allowed someone to speak for P-04.

Now they had found a record suggesting that the principal's own communication might have existed separately.

And someone had moved it under sealed custody.

The final line of the storage instruction was typed in a different format from the rest.

**If the principal's response is recovered, do not assume that it refers to the proposed receiving arrangement.**

Edward read it slowly.

The instruction suggested that the response had not merely been withheld.

Someone had been concerned that its contents might be misunderstood.

He turned the page over.

On the reverse was a reference to a separate document:

**PO-04 / ORIGINAL DELIVERY ATTEMPT — DAY 43.**

Jonathan checked the archive index.

“There's a record for it.”

“Where?”

“Meridian.”

Edward closed the file.

“Then we're going to Meridian.”

For the first time, they had a specific date associated with an attempted delivery of P-04's own communication.

And whatever had happened on day 43 might finally explain why the principal's voice had never entered the official record.
