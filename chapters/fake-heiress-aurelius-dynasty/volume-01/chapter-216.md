# **Chapter 216 — The Initials Beneath the Restriction**

The first response arrived at 8:03 a.m.

It was not from St Catherine's.

It was from Meridian's archive governance officer, confirming that the original FAW/06 appointment page could be examined under a restricted disclosure procedure.

The approval contained three conditions.

The examiner could identify the author of the handwritten restriction.

The examiner could confirm when it was added and whether it formed part of the original appointment.

The examiner could not disclose unrelated personal information or use the handwriting comparison to establish the identity of any person connected to the historical maternity records.

Edward read the conditions.

“Reasonable.”

Eleanor glanced at the initials on the scanned page.

“E.S.”

“Possibly Evelyn Shaw.”

“Possibly someone else.”

“Exactly.”

Dr. Evelyn Shaw had already appeared in the investigation. She had declined the OR-28 identity review and separately declined Adrian Holt as an independent verifier for OC-19 because of his prior involvement in receiving-route administration.

But those facts did not prove that she had written the restriction.

Nor did they prove that she had any role in the family communication request.

Edward sent the approval to the independent examiner.

Then he waited.

At 9:30, Elena was preparing for her second-stage pilot meeting.

She had divided her presentation into four sections.

**What the platform records.**

**What the platform does not decide.**

**Who can access each stage.**

**What happens when the evidence is incomplete.**

She had also added a page titled *Responsibilities Outside the Software*.

It listed the institution's obligations.

The platform could record that a document had been received.

It could preserve the file's submission history.

It could record who reviewed the document and what conclusion they entered.

It could not establish that a person's identity was genuine simply because a document carried a name.

It could not grant authority to a reviewer.

It could not turn an unanswered request into consent.

She read the last line and paused.

Then she saved the presentation.

Her phone vibrated.

A message from Isabelle.

**Isabelle:** I have spoken to the Valmont Foundation secretary. They can make time for a technical discussion next week. They want to understand the platform before deciding whether to participate.

Elena replied.

**Elena:** That's fine. I can explain the system and its limitations. I won't be able to promise that it can solve every problem in their current process.

The response came quickly.

**Isabelle:** I don't think anyone expects that.

Elena smiled.

**Elena:** Good. Because I don't want to be responsible for fixing problems the software cannot control.

**Isabelle:** That's probably the first thing you should tell them.

Elena looked at the message.

It was the first time Isabelle had offered advice about her work without suggesting how it should be presented to the Valmont family.

Elena appreciated that.

She put the phone aside and returned to her slides.

At 11:16, Dr. Cole submitted her preliminary examination note.

The initials on FAW/06 were not Evelyn Shaw's.

They belonged to the person who had issued the temporary appointment.

The examiner could not identify that person from the initials alone, but the appointment's issuing record contained a corresponding authorisation entry.

The name was visible.

**Dr. Samuel Hale.**

Edward sat very still.

Eleanor read the report over his shoulder.

“Samuel Hale wrote the restriction?”

“The examiner says the initials correspond to his authorised appointment notation.”

“Does that mean he knew the witness might be asked to verify infant identities?”

“It means he expressly prohibited the witness from doing so.”

Eleanor opened the original appointment record.

Dr. Hale had signed the issuing section on Day 24.

The handwritten restriction had been added before the witness signed.

The examiner confirmed that the ink and writing sequence were consistent with the restriction being part of the original execution process.

There was no indication that the note had been added later.

Edward read the final sentence.

**The restriction was not included in the Day-47 administrative summary.**

He leaned back.

“Now we need to understand why.”

Dr. Samuel Hale was available for a recorded interview the following afternoon.

He was no longer involved in the day-to-day administration of the preservation trust, but he remained its original administrator and settlor.

He agreed to discuss FAW/06.

The meeting took place in a small conference room at the trust's legal office.

Dr. Hale arrived carrying a thin folder.

He looked older than Edward remembered, though his voice remained firm.

“You have the appointment record?”

“Yes,” Edward replied.

“And the original restriction?”

“Yes.”

Dr. Hale nodded.

“Then I assume you have already reached the correct conclusion.”

“That the witness was authorised to witness execution of a family declaration, not verify the declaration's contents.”

“Correct.”

“Why did you add the handwritten restriction?”

Dr. Hale opened his folder.

“Because the role was narrow, and the surrounding circumstances were not.”

“What circumstances?”

“Several family authorities were preparing declarations for personal communication requests. The declarations were being submitted through a restricted process. The individuals named as recipients were not necessarily available to the people submitting the requests.”

Edward waited.

Dr. Hale continued.

“I wanted to prevent an administrative witness from being mistaken for an identity authority.”

“Was that a general concern?”

“It was a specific concern arising from the way the process was being organised.”

“Had someone already attempted to use the witness role to verify an infant's identity?”

Dr. Hale paused.

“I cannot say that.”

“Why not?”

“Because I was not reviewing infant identity records. I was establishing the scope of a temporary appointment.”

Edward looked at him.

“Did you know the identity of the protected recipient in F-02?”

“No.”

“Did you know the originating family?”

Dr. Hale considered the question.

“I knew that a recognised family authority had submitted a declaration. I did not personally verify the family relationship.”

“Did you know who signed it?”

“I knew the signatory's identity was held in the restricted source material. I was not authorised to disclose it, and I did not need it to issue the witness appointment.”

Eleanor made a note.

Edward asked, “Why was the witness appointed at all?”

“To ensure that the family authority's declaration had been executed properly. The witness could confirm that the signatory had signed the declaration in their presence. That was all.”

“Who selected the witness?”

“The hospital's temporary liaison office proposed the person.”

“Who approved the appointment?”

“I did.”

“Did the witness have a separate records role?”

“Yes. A temporary administrative liaison role.”

“Was that role connected to the maternity and neonatal records?”

“Not in the sense of authorising identity decisions. The person could coordinate records requests and administrative correspondence. They could not change clinical entries or certify a receiving identity.”

Edward looked down at his notes.

“Was the same person involved in the day-six amendment to SC-MAT/02-17?”

Dr. Hale did not answer immediately.

“I cannot confirm that from memory.”

“Would the appointment records establish it?”

“They might establish the administrative role. They would not prove that the person personally performed a particular task.”

Edward nodded.

That distinction was important.

“Did you later review the Day-47 summary?”

“Yes.”

“Did you authorise the broader wording?”

“No.”

“Did you approve the description of the witness as having verified family-originating documentation?”

“No.”

“Did you object?”

“I raised a concern.”

“What happened?”

Dr. Hale opened his folder and removed a short letter.

“I requested that the original appointment be used as the controlling document. I also requested that the summary be corrected.”

Edward took the letter.

It was dated Day 48.

The wording was restrained.

It did not accuse anyone of deliberate misconduct.

It stated that the later description exceeded the appointment's scope and that the original restriction had to remain visible in any future summary.

At the bottom was a receipt stamp.

**Received — Governance Secretariat.**

There was no correction record attached.

Eleanor looked up.

“Was the summary corrected?”

“I have not seen evidence that it was.”

“Did you follow up?”

“I sent one further request. I received an acknowledgement, but no substantive response.”

Edward examined the letter.

“Who acknowledged it?”

Dr. Hale turned to the final page.

The acknowledgement carried a shared governance account.

No individual name.

Edward looked at Eleanor.

Another shared account.

Another administrative response without an identifiable author.

The interview ended after forty minutes.

Dr. Hale made one final observation before leaving.

“Mr. Valmont, I want to be clear about something.”

Edward looked up.

“The family communication request was legitimate as a request. That does not mean the process that followed it was legitimate.”

“I understand.”

“I am not defending what happened afterward. But do not turn my preservation work into proof of a family relationship.”

“We won't.”

“And do not assume that because I established the limits of one person's authority, I knew who was responsible for every later decision.”

Edward nodded.

“That is not our conclusion.”

Dr. Hale closed his folder.

“Good. Then perhaps this investigation will finally distinguish what people did from what later records claim they did.”

He left.

Eleanor remained seated.

“He has given us something important.”

“The Day-48 objection?”

“Yes. He challenged the expanded description.”

“And the governance office acknowledged it without producing a correction.”

She looked at the letter.

“Which means someone received a clear warning.”

Edward gathered the documents.

“Now we need to find out what happened to it.”

At the Aurelius residence, Victoria was reviewing a proposed update to the family's independent search protocol.

Adrian had added a new paragraph.

**No historical administrative designation shall be treated as proof of biological identity, family membership, or personal consent without independent supporting evidence.**

Victoria read it twice.

“Good.”

Alexander looked up from his papers.

“Anything else?”

“An explicit statement that the search team must not approach a person merely because their name appears in a historical record.”

Adrian added it.

Damien looked at the draft.

“Will this slow the search?”

“Yes,” Alexander said.

Julian frowned.

“Is that bad?”

“No. Not if it prevents us from hurting someone.”

Julian opened his notebook.

He had added a new line beneath his fiftieth item.

**Finding someone is not the same as being ready to meet them.**

Lucien read it.

“That's actually good.”

Julian closed the notebook.

“Don't make a big deal out of it.”

“I wouldn't dream of it.”

For once, the room remained quiet.

The next morning, Edward received the Day-48 follow-up record.

It contained two entries.

The first was Dr. Hale's request for correction.

The second was a governance response.

**The existing administrative description has been retained pending review of related receiving-route records. No further action is required from the issuing administrator at this stage.**

Edward read it slowly.

Pending review of related receiving-route records.

The witness appointment concerned family authority declarations.

The receiving-route records concerned the proposed destination and handling of protected individuals.

They were separate functions.

The response had linked them.

Eleanor looked over his shoulder.

“Why would they retain an inaccurate description while waiting for a receiving-route review?”

“Perhaps they thought the broader wording was useful.”

“Useful to whom?”

Edward did not answer.

He opened the document's metadata.

The response had been generated by a shared governance account, but its routing history contained one additional field.

**Review coordinator: Restricted Appointments Desk.**

No individual name was displayed.

The same desk had appeared in the earlier review of OC-19.

Edward searched the preserved correspondence.

A note from the Restricted Appointments Desk had been issued on Day 47.

It stated that the family witness appointment could be cited as supporting evidence that the originating family declaration had been properly executed.

That part was accurate.

But the note also described the witness as having independently verified the family-originating documentation.

That part was not.

Edward compared the note with the original appointment.

The difference was unmistakable.

The appointment established that a declaration had been signed in front of a witness.

The later note suggested that the declaration's contents had been independently verified.

Someone had converted a procedural witness into a factual verifier.

Eleanor looked at the two documents.

“Could this be the point where the original authority became distorted?”

“It could be one point.”

“And Dr. Hale objected.”

“Yes.”

“But the description remained.”

Edward opened the Restricted Appointments Desk correspondence register.

A new line appeared beneath the Day-47 entry.

**Related review file: RA-47/09.**

The file had not appeared in the earlier investigation because it was classified as a governance coordination record rather than an identity or receiving-route record.

Edward requested its index.

A few minutes later, the first page arrived.

The subject line read:

**Consolidated Authority Descriptions — Restricted Historical Processes.**

Below it was a list of six administrative references.

FAW/06 was the first.

OC-19 was the second.

OR-28 was the fourth.

The remaining references were unfamiliar.

Edward looked at Eleanor.

“This is not another identity record.”

“No.”

“It is a document about how different authorities were described across separate processes.”

She read the subject line again.

“And someone brought them together.”

Edward opened the final page.

At the bottom was a routing instruction.

**For continuity, use the consolidated descriptions in subsequent administrative summaries unless the issuing authority submits a formal correction.**

The instruction had been issued after Dr. Hale's objection.

Edward stared at the date.

Day 49.

The original appointment had expired on Day 38.

The inaccurate summary had been challenged on Day 48.

And one day later, the broader description had been authorised for continued administrative use.

He closed the document.

“Someone knew the original scope was disputed.”

Eleanor looked at the screen.

“And chose continuity over correction.”

Edward did not reply.

For the first time, the investigation had moved beyond an inaccurate summary.

It had found a decision to keep using it.
