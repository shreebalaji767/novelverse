# **Chapter 118 — The Package That Never Reached Its Recipient**

The payment record was only three lines long.

Edward read it twice.

Then he placed it beside Dr Evelyn Shaw’s report.

**Day 34 — Cross Administrative Services**

- Recipient correspondence package.
- Conditional acknowledgement form.
- Alternate notification materials.

The amount was modest compared with the sums moving through the Northbridge Continuity Trust.

That made it more interesting, not less.

Someone had paid to prepare a communication package for a proposed recipient whose identity had not been independently verified.

And the payment had been made on the same day the alternate recipient schedule was created.

Jonathan studied the record.

“Could this simply have been a routine administrative expense?”

“It could.”

“Then what makes it important?”

Edward tapped the final line.

“Because the payment description says notification materials, while the review authority explicitly prohibited notification.”

“Preparation isn't delivery.”

“Exactly.”

Edward opened his notebook.

“Until we find a dispatch record, we cannot say the recipient was contacted. And until we find the original acknowledgement form, we cannot say anyone consented to receive a child.”

Jonathan nodded.

“Marianne Cross?”

“She administered the company. If anyone knows what happened to the package, she does.”

Marianne Cross received them at a small office in the city.

The company no longer operated commercially. Its remaining records had been transferred into a legal preservation arrangement, but Marianne had retained copies of several administrative registers.

She looked considerably less surprised by Edward’s arrival than she had been during their first meeting.

“You found the payment,” she said.

Edward placed the record before her.

“Day 34. Cross Administrative Services.”

Marianne adjusted her glasses.

“Yes.”

“What exactly did your company prepare?”

She examined the three descriptions.

“A correspondence envelope, a conditional acknowledgement form, and a set of instructions for an alternate notification.”

“Who requested them?”

“Vale Private Counsel.”

“Charles Vale?”

“The request came through his office. I cannot say whether he personally dictated every item.”

Edward waited.

Marianne opened a drawer and removed a thin folder.

“I kept the job register because the invoice was never closed in the usual way.”

She turned several pages.

Then stopped.

“Here.”

The entry was dated day 34.

**Project AV-18/RC — Alternate Recipient Correspondence.**

Below it were three separate tasks.

1. Prepare a sealed recipient correspondence package.
2. Prepare a conditional acknowledgement form.
3. Prepare alternate notification materials for use only after receiving authority confirmation.

Edward read the third line again.

“Was the package completed?”

“Yes.”

“Was it dispatched?”

Marianne did not answer immediately.

She turned another page.

“The preparation was completed on day 36. The package was sealed on day 37.”

“And the dispatch?”

“There isn't one.”

Jonathan leaned forward.

“Are you certain?”

Marianne pointed to the register.

“Cross Administrative Services maintained separate completion fields. Preparation, sealing, dispatch, delivery, and acknowledgement. They were never interchangeable.”

The dispatch field was blank.

So was the delivery field.

Edward looked at her.

“Why was it sealed if it wasn't going to be sent?”

“Because the instructions said to prepare it in advance. The package was supposed to remain in restricted custody until the necessary approvals were obtained.”

“Which approvals?”

Marianne turned to the instruction sheet.

“Receiving authority appointment. Independent recipient verification. Confirmation of the originating identity. And authorisation for notification.”

Four conditions.

Not one had been satisfied.

Edward looked at the date.

“Day 37.”

Marianne nodded.

“Dr Shaw's review had not yet been completed.”

“And when it was completed?”

“Her report did not recommend appointment.”

“So the package should have remained sealed.”

“It did.”

Edward studied the register.

“Should have?”

Marianne looked up.

“I can confirm what my company's records say. I cannot certify that nobody removed a copy or used the information elsewhere.”

It was an important distinction.

Edward did not overlook it.

Marianne brought out another document.

It was a carbon copy of the original job instruction, bearing the reference AV-18/RC.

At the bottom, beneath the administrative requirements, was a handwritten addition.

**No direct contact with proposed recipient until authority is formally established.**

The handwriting appeared familiar.

Edward took a photograph.

“Do you recognise this?”

“Charles's office sent the instruction.”

“That wasn't my question.”

Marianne looked at the handwriting more carefully.

“I believe the addition was made by Eleanor Vale.”

“Did she approve the package?”

“No. She approved the restriction.”

“Did she know who the proposed recipient was?”

Marianne shook her head.

“I never saw a verified identity attached to the instruction. The name was kept in a separate schedule.”

“Where?”

“Northbridge.”

Edward's expression tightened.

“Was the schedule ever sent to your company?”

“No. We were given a reference number and told to prepare the correspondence without entering the recipient's identity into our ordinary register.”

“AV-18.”

“Yes.”

“Who had access to the sealed package?”

Marianne consulted the custody page.

“Initially, me. Then one of my senior clerks, under a two-person preservation rule.”

“Name?”

“Peter Dallow.”

Edward wrote it down.

“Where is he now?”

“He retired. I don't know whether he still lives at the same address.”

“Did he ever sign a dispatch receipt?”

“No.”

“Did he ever report the package missing?”

“No.”

Marianne closed the folder.

“As far as my records show, it remained in our restricted cabinet until the company transferred its preserved files.”

Edward considered the possibilities.

The package might have remained sealed.

It might have been moved.

Or someone might have copied its contents without disturbing the original.

He needed the custody records.

“Can we inspect the original preservation cabinet?”

Marianne hesitated.

“Not without the legal custodian's permission.”

“Then we'll request it.”

She nodded.

Before Edward left, she spoke again.

“There is something else.”

He turned.

“The conditional acknowledgement form wasn't an ordinary acceptance form.”

“What was different about it?”

“It contained a warning.”

“What warning?”

Marianne searched through the folder and found a surviving template.

The wording was printed in small type beneath the signature field.

**Acknowledgement of receipt of proposed information does not constitute acceptance of a child, acceptance of permanent responsibility, confirmation of biological identity, or appointment as receiving family.**

Jonathan read it twice.

“So even if someone signed it…”

“They would only have acknowledged receiving information,” Marianne said. “Nothing more.”

Edward looked at the form.

The wording was unusually careful.

Someone had anticipated that a signature might later be misrepresented.

Or perhaps someone had already seen that happen.

The legal custodian granted access to the preserved Cross Administrative Services records three days later.

Edward and Jonathan arrived with Dr Mira Sen, who had been asked to inspect the package without opening any restricted identity material.

The cabinet stood in a secured storage room.

Its exterior was unremarkable.

Inside were numbered envelopes, sealed folders, and registers dating back nearly two decades.

The clerk checked the inventory.

“AV-18/RC,” she said.

She removed a grey archival box.

The seal number matched the transfer record.

Dr Sen photographed the box before examining it.

“Seal intact,” she said.

Edward checked the label.

**ALTERNATE RECIPIENT CORRESPONDENCE PACKAGE**

**Prepared Day 36. Sealed Day 37.**

Below that:

**Dispatch prohibited pending formal receiving authority appointment.**

The box contained three items.

The first was a correspondence envelope.

The second was the conditional acknowledgement form.

The third was a set of alternate notification instructions.

None bore a recipient's name on the exterior.

Dr Sen examined the seals.

“No indication of opening.”

“Can you establish whether copies were made?” Edward asked.

“Not from the physical condition of this package.”

She inspected the inventory sheet.

“There is a document-copy entry, however.”

Edward looked down.

A small notation appeared beside the second item.

**Duplicate acknowledgement template — administrative archive.**

“Who requested the duplicate?”

The clerk checked the index.

“Vale Private Counsel. Day 38.”

“Why?”

“Preservation of standard wording.”

Edward looked at Jonathan.

The original package had remained sealed.

But a copy of its acknowledgement template had been produced.

“Where was that copy sent?”

The clerk checked the distribution register.

“VPC administrative records. Reference FA-ADMIN.”

“Which department?”

“Family allocation review.”

Edward's eyes narrowed.

“Was it the same department that processed the altered OR-28 status record?”

The clerk checked.

“Yes.”

The answer did not establish misuse.

It did establish access.

Dr Sen carefully compared the original acknowledgement form with the surviving template recovered from the Family Allocation Records Office.

The documents were nearly identical.

There was one difference.

The original included the complete warning.

The administrative copy omitted the final clause.

**...or appointment as receiving family.**

Edward stared at the missing words.

“Who removed them?”

Dr Sen examined the printing marks.

“The copy was generated from a modified electronic template. The original paper form was not altered.”

“Can we identify the operator?”

“The print record may identify the account, not necessarily the person.”

Jonathan opened the archive's technical report.

The print event had been recorded on day 39.

Account:

**FA-ADMIN / REVIEW DESK**

No individual operator was named.

The same shared account appeared repeatedly in the earlier administrative changes.

Edward leaned back.

“Someone copied the acknowledgement form, removed a limitation, and placed the altered version in the family allocation department.”

“Possibly for administrative convenience,” Jonathan said.

“Possibly.”

Edward did not dismiss the explanation.

But the omission mattered.

The original form made clear that acknowledging information did not appoint a receiving family.

The shortened version did not say that.

It was still not an acceptance form.

Yet it could be misunderstood.

Or deliberately presented as something it was not.

Dr Sen placed both copies beside each other.

“There is another difference.”

The administrative version included a new field beneath the signature line.

**Receiving representative's confirmation: __________________**

Edward looked at it.

“That field isn't on the original.”

“No.”

“Who added it?”

“The electronic document history may tell us.”

The clerk checked the archive index again.

“There's a revision record attached to the template.”

She opened the file.

A new line appeared.

**Day 40 — Field added at request of Family Allocation Review.**

No individual author.

No supporting approval.

Edward closed his notebook.

“Find the original request.”

The original request arrived the following morning.

It was a short internal memorandum.

The subject was:

**AV-18 — Conditional Acknowledgement Template**

The memorandum requested a modified version of the standard form for internal administrative use.

It did not authorise notification.

It did not authorise appointment.

It did not approve the proposed recipient.

But the final paragraph was troubling.

*The receiving representative's response should be recorded separately from the acknowledgement of information received, so that any later appointment can be processed without repeating the initial correspondence.*

Edward read it slowly.

The distinction sounded reasonable.

A person might acknowledge receipt of a proposal without accepting it. Their response could then be recorded separately.

But who was supposed to ask for that response?

And under whose authority?

The memorandum had been routed through the Family Allocation Review desk.

Its approval field contained a reference to the day-47 administrative closure.

The closure had been processed through Daniel Mercer and the shared FA-ADMIN account.

Edward called Daniel.

He arrived that afternoon.

After reading the memorandum, he frowned.

“I remember the form.”

“Did you authorise this modification?”

“I processed the document request.”

“Did you understand what it was for?”

“An internal record. We needed to distinguish between someone receiving a proposal and someone agreeing to it.”

“Did you know the receiving authority had not been appointed?”

“Yes.”

“Did you believe the form could be sent?”

Daniel shook his head.

“No. It was supposed to remain an administrative template.”

“Then why was the receiving representative's confirmation field added?”

“Because the department wanted a place to record a response if one eventually arrived.”

“Who requested that?”

“I don't remember.”

Edward placed the original memorandum before him.

“You signed the processing acknowledgement.”

Daniel examined it.

“That is my system acknowledgement. It means I processed the request. It doesn't mean I approved notification.”

“Did anyone ask you to remove the warning from the original template?”

Daniel studied the document comparison.

His expression changed.

“No.”

“Are you certain?”

“I remember the instruction being to retain the original limitations. We were not supposed to turn an acknowledgement into an acceptance.”

“Then why was the warning shortened?”

Daniel did not answer.

For several seconds, he stared at the two versions.

“I didn't make that change,” he said.

Edward believed he might be telling the truth.

But that did not make the document harmless.

“Who had access to the review desk after you?”

Daniel considered.

“Several people. Patricia Moore had supervisory access. Martin Keane had technical access during the migration. Charles's office could submit revised wording.”

“And could you identify who printed the shortened version?”

“No. The account was shared.”

Edward closed the file.

The same problem again.

A decision had been recorded.

An action had occurred.

But the person responsible had disappeared behind an administrative account.

At the Valmont Estate, Elena's suitcase stood open on her bed.

The departure date was approaching quickly.

Her passport lay beside her travel documents. A folder contained her Singapore programme acceptance, accommodation confirmation, insurance papers, and the financial plan she had prepared with Nicholas.

On her laptop, the final version of her digital grant management proposal was open.

She checked the application tracking section again.

Every submission would receive a reference number.

Every eligibility decision would have a recorded reason.

Every reviewer would have a defined role.

Every change would leave an audit trail.

No single administrator would be able to quietly convert a pending review into an approved decision.

She leaned back.

It was not a revolutionary idea.

That was precisely why she liked it.

Institutions did not always need complicated systems.

Sometimes they needed simple procedures that people could not easily misuse.

A knock came at the door.

“Come in.”

Isabelle entered with a small notebook.

“I wanted to ask you something.”

Elena turned her laptop slightly away.

“Is this about the foundation?”

“Yes.”

“Then I charge consultation fees.”

Isabelle raised an eyebrow.

“You're leaving in three days.”

“My prices have increased because I'm internationally mobile.”

Isabelle almost smiled.

She placed the notebook on the desk.

“I revised the grant application process. I included the separate reviewer comments you suggested.”

Elena read the page.

“You've added a reason field for rejected applications.”

“Yes.”

“And a record of who changed the decision?”

“I thought that was important.”

“It is.”

Isabelle watched her.

“Would you actually use this system if we implemented it?”

Elena looked up.

“You mean after I return?”

“If you want to.”

Elena considered the question.

“I'd be interested in testing it. But I don't want to become responsible for the foundation just because I designed a tool.”

“I didn't ask you to.”

“I know.”

A brief silence followed.

Isabelle looked at the suitcase.

“Are you nervous?”

“A little.”

“About Singapore?”

“About getting there and discovering that everyone else understands everything already.”

Isabelle laughed softly.

“That sounds familiar.”

Elena closed the notebook.

“You'll be fine.”

“So will you.”

Neither said anything more for a moment.

Then Isabelle picked up the notebook.

“I'll send you the revised version tomorrow.”

“Send it tonight. I'll review it on the flight.”

“You'll be studying on the flight?”

“I'll also be eating, sleeping, and complaining about the seat.”

“That sounds more realistic.”

Isabelle left.

Elena returned to her laptop.

For the first time in several weeks, the mystery surrounding the old records felt distant.

Not unimportant.

Just not the only thing happening in her life.

At the Aurelius Estate, Victoria received the latest investigation summary.

She read the section concerning the Cross Administrative Services package.

“The package was never dispatched?”

“According to the original custody records, no,” Alexander replied.

“And the acknowledgement template was copied?”

“Yes. A modified version reached the Family Allocation Review department.”

Victoria looked at the comparison.

“They removed the sentence explaining that acknowledgement did not appoint a receiving family.”

“Only from the administrative copy,” Alexander said. “The original remains intact.”

“Do we know why?”

“Not yet.”

Victoria set the report down.

For a moment, she looked toward the garden.

“Every time we find another document, I expect it to tell us where our daughter went.”

Alexander said nothing.

He understood the expectation.

He also understood that it was becoming dangerous.

They had spent eighteen years searching for a child whose identity had been obscured by records that contradicted one another.

They could not afford to turn an administrative reference into a biological conclusion.

“Edward has not established that Child 28-B was ours,” he said quietly.

“I know.”

“Nor has he established that IV-204 was ours.”

“I know that too.”

Victoria folded her hands.

“I want the truth, Alexander. Not another explanation that sounds convincing until the next document arrives.”

He nodded.

“Then we keep asking for originals.”

Victoria looked again at the report.

“Even if the answer takes longer?”

“Especially then.”

That evening, Edward received a message from Dr Mira Sen.

She had completed a preliminary comparison of the original and modified acknowledgement forms.

There was one additional finding.

The shortened template had not merely been printed.

Its electronic revision history showed that the omitted warning had been restored once before.

Then removed again.

The restoration occurred on day 41.

The second removal occurred on day 43.

Both changes had been made through the same shared administrative account.

But the second revision contained a comment.

Edward opened the attached screenshot.

The comment read:

**Do not include the appointment limitation in the response field. The appointment question will be addressed separately.**

There was no author name.

Only a document reference:

**AV-18 / R-12 / CONDITIONAL RESPONSE.**

Edward stared at the final phrase.

R-12.

Dr Evelyn Shaw's review office.

The same office that had refused to recommend appointment of the alternate recipient.

The same review whose conclusion had later been described as administratively complete.

He called Jonathan.

“Get me the complete R-12 correspondence file.”

“Didn't we already obtain Dr Shaw's report?”

“We obtained her report and the day-47 summary.”

“What else is there?”

Edward enlarged the screenshot.

“Apparently, someone kept a separate record of the proposed recipient's response.”

He paused.

“And I want to know whether that response was ever actually received.”

Because if the recipient had responded, the next question would be unavoidable.

Who had contacted them?

And what exactly had they been told?

Edward looked once more at the altered template.

The original form had warned that acknowledgement was not acceptance.

The modified form had created a separate field for confirmation.

And somewhere between those two versions, an administrative record had begun to resemble a decision that nobody had the authority to make.
