# **Chapter 194 — The Custodian Who Could Not Answer Alone**

Dr Eleanor Vale did not refuse the request.

That was the first thing Edward noticed.

Her office acknowledged it at 8:12 the following morning, less than fifteen minutes after the Secretariat opened.

The response contained three paragraphs.

**The OI-07 register remains under joint preservation.**

**The requested material may be considered for limited procedural disclosure, subject to confirmation by the originating office.**

**The preservation custodian cannot independently establish the authorship or authority of an instruction she was not authorised to originate.**

Edward read the final paragraph again.

“She is telling us she cannot answer the question herself,” Sophie said.

“Or that she won't.”

“Those aren't the same thing.”

“No.”

Edward placed the response beside the day-43 governance instruction.

Helen Sloane had drafted GOV/OC19/02. Marianne Vale had completed an administrative review. The document had been circulated after a restricted instruction from an unidentified originating office.

That instruction was OI-07.

And Eleanor Vale held the register.

“Request a meeting,” Edward said.

“With Eleanor?”

“Yes. Limited to the OI-07 instruction and its release conditions.”

Sophie began drafting.

“Should we include the day-46 C-19 classification?”

“Include it as the reason for the request. Don't imply the originating instruction caused the later classification.”

Sophie nodded.

Edward watched her finish the letter.

He had learned to be careful with chronology.

A document created before an event could explain the conditions under which the event occurred.

It could not automatically explain the decisions people made afterward.

At 10:30, the Valmont Estate's smaller records room was prepared for the meeting.

Richard had initially suggested the conference room.

Edward declined.

The request concerned a restricted register, not a family discussion. He wanted a small room, a limited attendance list and a clear record of what was disclosed.

Sophie arrived with the chronology.

Eleanor arrived at 10:42.

She carried a narrow archival case.

“Good morning, Edward.”

“Dr Vale.”

She placed the case on the table.

“I understand you want to examine OI-07.”

“I want to establish who initiated the review that produced GOV/OC19/02.”

“That is not necessarily the same as identifying who created the register entry.”

“I understand.”

Eleanor looked at Sophie.

“Is the requested scope still limited to procedural authorship, approval and distribution?”

“Yes,” Sophie replied. “We are not requesting the protected principal's identity, personal communications, or any receiving identity.”

“Good.”

Eleanor opened the archival case.

Inside was a sealed document envelope, a preservation certificate and a separate release schedule.

She did not remove the envelope immediately.

“Before I release anything, I need to explain the custody arrangement.”

Edward waited.

“The OI-07 register was transferred to my office under a preservation instruction. I was appointed to protect the integrity of the original record and maintain a controlled access log.”

“Were you involved in creating the instruction?”

“No.”

“Did you know its author?”

“Not when I accepted custody.”

“Have you learned since?”

Eleanor paused.

“I have learned who was responsible for transmitting it. That is not the same as knowing who authorised it.”

Edward wrote that down.

“Then what can you release?”

“The register extract, the instruction's routing history, and the custody record. The substantive source material remains restricted until the originating office confirms that the proposed disclosure is within the original instruction's scope.”

“Can the originating office be identified?”

“The register records an office code.”

“Which one?”

“PAO-01.”

Sophie looked up.

“Programme Authority Office?”

“Yes.”

“Under the Chair?”

“Yes.”

Edward looked at Eleanor.

“Does that mean the instruction originated with you?”

“No.”

“Could it have originated from someone acting under your office?”

“Possibly. The code identifies the office responsible for the instruction, not the individual who made it.”

Eleanor removed the sealed envelope.

She checked the seal number against the preservation certificate.

Then she signed the access register.

Only after that did she open it.

The first extract contained six entries.

**OI-07 — Originating Instruction Register**

**Entry date:** Day 42.

**Originating office:** PAO-01.

**Receiving offices:** Governance Secretariat; Programme Administration.

**Subject:** Independent verification and protected-principal communication controls.

**Instruction status:** Issued for procedural review.

**Authorising instrument:** Restricted.

The second entry recorded the preparation of GOV/OC19/02 on day 43.

The third recorded the circulation of the document.

The fourth recorded Helen Sloane's draft completion.

The fifth recorded Marianne Vale's administrative review.

The sixth recorded the instruction's return to the originating office for confirmation of its next stage.

Edward examined the dates.

“Day 42,” he said.

“Yes.”

“The day before GOV/OC19/02.”

“Yes.”

“Was the instruction issued before Adrian Holt was proposed?”

“By three days.”

“Was it issued before the C-19 query?”

“By two days.”

Edward looked at the subject.

Independent verification and protected-principal communication controls.

The investigation had previously treated OC-19 as a specific proposal arising from the need to verify PR-06.

But OI-07 suggested that the review of independence and communication controls had been initiated earlier.

“Why was this review started?” Edward asked.

Eleanor folded her hands.

“The register does not state the reason.”

“Does the original instruction?”

“It may.”

“Then we need it.”

“The originating office must confirm its release.”

“Can you request that confirmation?”

“I already have.”

“When?”

“Yesterday, after your request arrived.”

Edward looked at her.

“Who received it?”

“The Programme Authority Office.”

“Did they respond?”

“Not yet.”

Sophie examined the distribution record.

“There's an additional recipient here.”

She pointed to the fourth entry.

**External preservation liaison — restricted copy.**

The name field was blank.

Edward leaned forward.

“Was this Charles Vale?”

“I cannot say.”

“Was it Vale Private Counsel?”

“The office code is not included in this extract.”

“Can you establish whether the external liaison received the instruction?”

“Not from the register alone.”

Edward made another note.

The pattern was becoming familiar.

A record established that something had been sent.

It did not establish who received it.

A routing code identified a destination.

It did not identify the person behind it.

And an administrative review did not create substantive authority.

He looked at Eleanor.

“Did you know the review concerned the C-19 correspondence route?”

“I knew it concerned the separation of independent verification from personal representation. I did not know the specific day-46 classification until your request arrived.”

“Did you authorise C-19 to transmit the query?”

“No.”

“Did you authorise anyone to treat C-19 as a nomination source?”

“No.”

“Did you appoint an OC-19 verifier?”

“No.”

“Did you approve Adrian Holt?”

“No.”

Eleanor's answers were precise.

Edward closed the register extract.

“Then I need the original instruction.”

“So do I,” she said.

At 12:18, the Programme Authority Office responded.

The response was addressed to Eleanor, with Edward and Sophie copied.

It was signed by the office's current administrative secretary.

**The originating instruction may be disclosed in limited procedural form. The identity of the individual author and the personal authority underlying the instruction remain restricted pending confirmation of the originating authority.**

Edward read the message.

“They're releasing the instruction without its author.”

“That's their current position,” Eleanor said.

“Can we challenge the restriction?”

“We can ask for the basis.”

Sophie drafted a reply.

Eleanor reviewed it.

Then she added a sentence of her own:

**The preservation custodian cannot certify the identity or authority of an originating author from an instruction whose authorisation record remains withheld.**

She sent it.

Edward watched the message leave.

“You've made your position clear.”

“I have to.”

“Does that create a problem for your custody appointment?”

“It creates a problem if someone expects me to certify something I cannot verify.”

She closed the archival case.

“Custody is not authorship, Edward. It is not approval. And it is not a substitute for the authority that created the record.”

Edward nodded.

He had heard similar language from her before.

But today it sounded less like a general principle and more like a warning.

At 14:05, Eleanor asked to speak with Edward privately.

Sophie remained in the records room, reviewing the disclosed entries.

Eleanor led Edward to the adjoining study.

A small folder lay on her desk.

It was not part of OI-07.

“Before you leave,” she said, “I want to show you something from the preservation history.”

Edward sat down.

Eleanor opened the folder.

Inside was a certificate dated day 49.

**Preservation instruction — OI-07 supporting material.**

The certificate stated that the original authorisation record had been separated from the procedural instruction and retained under restricted custody.

The reason was listed as:

**Protection of originating personal authority and prevention of administrative substitution.**

Edward read the sentence carefully.

“Personal authority?”

“Yes.”

“Does that mean the instruction was issued by an individual acting in a personal capacity?”

“Not necessarily. The wording distinguishes the underlying authority from the administrative record. It does not identify the person.”

“Was the authorisation record sealed at the same time as the instruction?”

“No. It was separated later.”

“When?”

“Day 49.”

“Two days after Adrian Holt's proposal was rejected.”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“The preservation certificate does not explain.”

Edward turned the page.

A second certificate was attached.

It recorded the transfer of the authorisation record into a sealed package.

The receiving custodian was Dr Samuel Hale.

Edward recognised the name.

Dr Hale had administered the medical-record preservation arrangements and the Northbridge Continuity Trust.

He had also acted as settlor and administrator of certain preservation structures.

But he was not the identity custodian.

Nor was he the appointment authority.

“Why was Dr Hale given custody of the authorisation record?”

“His preservation appointment covered sealed supporting material connected to the protected-principal communication procedure.”

“Did he have authority to inspect the contents?”

“Only to preserve them and release them under the stated conditions.”

“Did he know the author?”

“I don't know.”

Edward looked at the transfer certificate.

“Did he ever release the package?”

“No.”

“Was it transferred after his death?”

Eleanor nodded.

“The package remained sealed. Its custody was transferred to the current preservation arrangement.”

“Who has it now?”

Eleanor did not answer immediately.

Then she said, “I do.”

Edward looked at her.

“You have the original authorisation record?”

“Yes.”

“Why wasn't that included in the OI-07 release?”

“Because it is not part of the procedural instruction. It is the separate authority supporting it.”

“Can we request it?”

“You can.”

“Will you release it?”

“Not without the originating authority's confirmation.”

Edward stood.

“Then we need to know why the authorisation record was separated.”

“Yes.”

“And why it was placed under Dr Hale's preservation custody.”

“Yes.”

He looked at the folder again.

“Do you know whether the authorisation record identifies a person?”

Eleanor's expression changed slightly.

“I have never opened it.”

Edward believed she was telling the truth.

But he also understood what that meant.

They had found a sealed document that might explain who initiated the investigation of OC-19.

And it was now in the custody of someone who could not release it without permission from the very office whose authority they were trying to establish.

At 16:30, Edward returned to the records room.

Sophie had finished reviewing the extract.

“I found a discrepancy.”

He sat down.

“Where?”

“The day-42 register says the instruction was issued for procedural review. But the day-49 preservation certificate says the supporting authorisation was separated to protect the originating personal authority.”

Edward placed Eleanor's certificate beside her notes.

“I've seen it.”

Sophie read the transfer record.

“Dr Samuel Hale.”

“Yes.”

“Wasn't he also responsible for the Northbridge preservation trust?”

“He administered it. That doesn't make him the author of this instruction.”

“Could the trust have funded the review?”

“Not established.”

Sophie nodded.

“Then we should check the expenditure records.”

“Not yet.”

She looked at him.

“Why?”

“Because we need the authorisation record first. If we start treating every payment connected to Northbridge as evidence of this instruction, we'll repeat the same mistake we've been trying to avoid.”

Sophie closed the file.

“Fair.”

Edward turned to the day-42 entry.

There was another field.

**Review requested by:** PAO-01.

**Review purpose:** Confirm independent verification requirements before any further candidate proposal.

The wording was important.

The review had not been initiated to appoint someone.

It had been initiated to determine what would be required before a candidate could be proposed.

That meant the later sequence had two distinct stages.

First, the originating office asked for criteria.

Then someone transmitted a query through C-19.

Then Adrian Holt was proposed.

Then Dr Shaw rejected him.

Then an alternative possibility was recorded without a name.

The procedural review had started before any of those events.

But the authorisation record remained sealed.

At 18:12, Edward received a call from Dr Eleanor Vale.

“The Programme Authority Office has responded.”

“What did they say?”

“They confirm that the original instruction was issued under a valid originating authority.”

“Have they identified the authority?”

“No.”

“Have they confirmed the author?”

“No.”

“Have they authorised release of the supporting record?”

“No.”

Edward closed his eyes briefly.

“What reason did they give?”

“The supporting record contains a personal authority that remains subject to separate preservation conditions.”

“Can they confirm whether it was issued by P-04?”

“No.”

“Can they confirm whether P-04 personally authorised the review?”

“No.”

“Can they confirm whether the instruction was issued by a representative?”

“No.”

“Then what exactly have they confirmed?”

“That OI-07 was validly issued under an authority recognised by the originating office.”

Edward wrote the sentence down.

It was useful.

But it was not enough.

“Did they say when the restriction will be reviewed?”

“They did.”

“When?”

“After the originating office completes a separate authority reconciliation.”

Edward looked at the clock.

“Is that a new process?”

“It appears to be.”

“Was it already underway?”

“The office says it was initiated on day 164.”

Edward froze.

Day 164.

The same day Marianne Vale had written to Peter Langford asking him to nominate a successor or confirm that the vacant control function should remain suspended.

The same day the administrative continuity problem had been formally raised.

“Did the authority reconciliation involve the original PR-06 procedure?”

“I don't know.”

“Can you send the exact response?”

“I'll send it.”

The call ended.

A minute later, the document arrived.

Edward opened it.

The final paragraph contained a reference.

**Authority reconciliation file: AR-164/09.**

He searched the closure index.

The result appeared immediately.

**AR-164/09 — Reconciliation of Originating Authority and Restricted Instruction Custody.**

**Initiated:** Day 164.

**Responsible office:** Programme Authority Office.

**Preservation custodian:** Dr Eleanor Vale.

**Supporting material:** OI-07; PR-06; OC-19; restricted authorisation package.

Edward read the listed references again.

PR-06.

OC-19.

OI-07.

The three procedures had now been brought together in one reconciliation file.

He looked at Sophie.

“Day 164.”

She opened the chronology.

“That's the day Marianne asked Peter to nominate a successor or suspend the control function.”

“Yes.”

“And the authority reconciliation started that same day.”

Edward nodded.

“Which means the originating office was reviewing the authority behind OI-07 while the administrative control appointment remained vacant.”

“Could that explain why no successor was appointed?”

“Possibly. But we don't know.”

He opened the document's final page.

The file contained a note from the Programme Authority Office.

**No substantive conclusion may be drawn from administrative continuity while the originating authority remains under reconciliation.**

Edward stared at the sentence.

It was almost identical to the rule that had been ignored in the closure process.

Routine work could continue.

Authority could not be created by continuity.

And the person responsible for the original instruction still had not been identified.

He saved the document.

Then he noticed a field beneath the preservation conditions.

**Next review date: Day 190.**

Edward checked the calendar.

Today was day 193.

The review date had passed three days ago.

He looked at Sophie.

“Request the day-190 review outcome.”

She began typing.

Edward added one more requirement.

“Ask for every version of the reconciliation file, not just the current summary.”

Sophie sent the request.

At 18:47, the acknowledgement arrived.

**Request received.**

**Current review status: Completed.**

**Outcome document: Restricted.**

**Release authority: Pending confirmation.**

Edward leaned back.

The review had been completed.

Someone had reached a conclusion.

But the conclusion was still sealed.

And for the first time, the investigation was no longer waiting for someone to create a record.

It was waiting for someone to explain a decision that had already been made.
