# **Chapter 77 — The Envelope Victoria Never Opened**

Victoria Aurelius remembered signing for the envelope.

She did not remember what had been inside it.

That distinction became increasingly important as she stood in the restricted archive room, looking at the old receipt on Helena Brooks's screen.

The date was unmistakable.

The seventeenth day after the births.

Her signature appeared beneath the receiving officer's declaration, written in the careful, slightly slanted handwriting she had used before the disappearance of her daughter.

Alexander stood beside her.

"Are you certain you received it?" he asked.

Victoria examined the digital record.

"Yes."

"Do you remember who delivered it?"

"No."

"Did you open it?"

She looked at the receipt again.

"I don't think so."

Edward remained quiet.

He had learned that pressing someone to remember something from eighteen years earlier rarely produced reliable answers. A confident recollection could be less useful than an honest admission of uncertainty.

Victoria touched the screen.

"What's the description?"

Helena enlarged the entry.

**Additional evidence received.**

**Origin: Private maternity clinic.**

**Description: Original maternal identification material.**

**Reference: IV-204.**

**Condition: Sealed.**

**Receiving authority: Victoria Aurelius.**

**Storage instruction: Restricted evidence custody.**

Victoria read it twice.

"Why would an item from Isabella Vale's maternity clinic be sent directly to me?"

Nobody answered.

Alexander looked at the reference.

"Was Isabella already known to us at that time?"

Edward opened his notebook.

"According to the surviving correspondence, the Aurelius Family Office knew that a separate maternity record existed under Isabella Vale's name. However, we haven't established how much information reached you personally."

Victoria shook her head.

"I remember receiving documents about the missing child. I remember the hospital refusing to provide complete records. But I don't remember anyone telling me that another woman had given birth at nearly the same time."

"Could the envelope have been delivered without an explanation?" Jonathan asked.

"Possibly."

"Did you sign for restricted documents regularly?"

"Yes. Especially during the first few months after the disappearance."

Victoria's voice remained steady, but her expression had changed.

"At the time, I was receiving so many reports that I relied on the Family Office to tell me which ones required my attention."

Alexander turned towards Helena.

"Find the physical envelope."

Helena checked the archive register.

"There is a storage location."

"Where?"

"Cabinet Seven. Secondary evidence collection."

The archivist left to retrieve it.

Victoria remained beside the table.

She looked at the old receipt again.

"I signed for something connected to another mother's child," she said quietly. "And I don't remember ever seeing it."

Alexander placed a hand on the back of her chair.

"We'll find out what happened."

Edward did not interrupt.

He was already wondering whether the envelope had ever been intended for Victoria to open.

The envelope was brought into the examination room twenty minutes later.

It was larger than the first one, made of thick cream-coloured paper, and bore no hospital logo.

A red security seal crossed the flap.

The reference IV-204 had been written in black ink.

Unlike the earlier envelope, this one had no St Catherine's identification number.

The archivist checked the seal against the receiving register.

"It appears intact."

Edward examined the exterior.

"Any evidence of previous opening?"

"Not visible."

Victoria stepped closer.

"Can I open it?"

The archivist looked towards Helena.

Helena reviewed the authorisation.

"You are the recorded recipient. We can open it under witnessed evidence procedure."

Victoria nodded.

The seal was cut.

Inside lay a folded sheet of paper, a small transparent evidence pouch, and a second envelope containing a typed letter.

The pouch was empty.

Its label read:

**ORIGINAL MATERNAL IDENTIFICATION BAND — IV-204**

**Transferred for independent preservation.**

**Not to be attached to any receiving infant record without verification.**

Edward looked at the pouch.

"Where is the band?"

Holt leaned forward.

"That should be the same band I received from Meridian."

"Possibly," Edward replied. "But we need to establish whether these are two separate records of the same item or two separate items."

The letter might answer that question.

Victoria unfolded it.

The first paragraph was addressed to her.

*Mrs. Aurelius,*

*The enclosed identification material originated from the private maternity admission of Isabella Vale. The infant associated with this material was recorded as a live female birth at 2:31 a.m.*

Victoria stopped reading.

Alexander looked at her.

"Continue."

She swallowed and read the next paragraph.

*The material has been retained separately because the receiving infant's identity has not been independently established. A cross-reference to St Catherine's Infant A appears in the transfer paperwork, but that reference must not be interpreted as proof of biological identity.*

Edward's attention sharpened.

The wording was almost identical to the caution in Gabriel's transfer instruction.

Victoria continued.

*No statement should be made to Isabella Vale concerning the identity or location of the child until the comparison has been completed and independently verified.*

The final paragraph was shorter.

*Please preserve the enclosed material. Do not permit its use as proof of family relationship.*

There was no signature.

Only the initials:

**M.C.**

Jonathan looked at Edward.

"Marianne Cross."

Edward nodded.

But the letter had a date.

He checked it.

The seventeenth day.

The same day Victoria had signed for the envelope.

"Who delivered it?" Edward asked.

Helena examined the receipt.

"According to the archive record, the item was delivered through the Family Office's external correspondence desk."

"Was Marianne Cross listed as the sender?"

"No. The sender field is blank."

"Then the initials are not enough to establish that she personally wrote it."

"Agreed."

Edward looked at the empty evidence pouch.

"Where did the original band go?"

Holt answered.

"I received it from Eleanor Vale later. The date in my own inventory was the twenty-eighth day."

Victoria turned towards him.

"Then why was this pouch received on the seventeenth day?"

Holt considered the question.

"Perhaps the pouch was sent ahead of the band."

"Empty?" Alexander asked.

"Or the band was removed before the envelope reached us."

Edward studied the label.

There was a small handwritten note beneath the reference.

**Contents held separately by originating representative.**

He looked at Victoria.

"Do you remember anyone explaining that to you?"

"No."

"Do you remember being told the envelope was empty?"

"No."

Edward looked at the pouch again.

Someone had sent Victoria a letter warning her not to treat the band as proof of identity.

But the band itself had not accompanied the letter.

It had arrived later through a different route.

And the person who had sent the letter had known enough to insist that the two records remain separate.

The question was no longer simply who had possessed the evidence.

It was who had known what the evidence could establish.

By late afternoon, Edward had returned to his office with copies of both archive receipts.

He placed them beside the comparison dispatch records.

The sequence was now clearer.

On the fourteenth day, the comparison materials were returned as unsuitable for identity determination.

On the seventeenth day, two separate evidence deliveries were recorded at the Aurelius Family Office.

One contained the St Catherine's identification card.

The other contained a letter and an empty pouch referring to Isabella's original maternity band.

Both carried references connected to the same unresolved identity question.

Jonathan sat opposite him.

"Someone was trying to preserve the evidence."

"Yes."

"That sounds positive."

"Only if preservation was the objective."

Jonathan frowned.

"What else could it have been?"

"Control."

Edward arranged the documents into two columns.

"One item remained sealed. The other was described but physically absent. Both were kept away from the people who needed answers."

"Victoria and Alexander."

"And Isabella."

Jonathan studied the letter.

"Yet the letter was addressed to Victoria."

"Which means someone wanted her to know that Isabella's maternity record existed."

"Why?"

Edward tapped the final paragraph.

"Because someone wanted to prevent the Aureliuses from treating the band as proof."

"That would have been reasonable."

"Yes. But if the concern was accuracy, why was the comparison never completed?"

Jonathan had no answer.

Edward continued.

"Someone was careful enough to warn against a false conclusion, but not careful enough to ensure the investigation reached a true one."

He picked up the letter.

"That contradiction matters."

The next morning, Edward contacted the private maternity clinic.

Its original name had been Vale Women's Medical Centre, although it had since been acquired by a larger healthcare group.

The current management team arranged access to the surviving archival material after reviewing the relevant authorisations.

The clinic's records officer, Ms. Priya D'Souza, met them in a small conference room.

"We have preserved the original maternity admission register," she explained. "Some administrative correspondence was transferred to the acquiring institution. Other records were retained in off-site storage."

Edward showed her the letter.

"Do you recognise this reference?"

D'Souza examined it.

"IV-204 was our temporary maternity identification number."

"Was an original identification band issued under that number?"

"Yes."

"Do your records show who collected it?"

She opened the digital archive.

"Let me check."

After several minutes, she found the relevant entry.

**Patient: Isabella Vale.**

**Delivery: 2:31 a.m.**

**Infant: Female.**

**Identification band: IV-204.**

**Transfer: Sixth day after birth.**

The next entry concerned the identification band.

**Original band retained pending external identity review.**

**Collection authority: Eleanor Vale.**

**Date: Twenty-eighth day.**

Edward nodded.

"That matches the Meridian record."

D'Souza opened the attached correspondence.

"There is something else."

She turned the monitor towards them.

A request had been submitted on the fifteenth day.

It asked the clinic to provide the original maternity identification material for an external comparison.

The sender was not a hospital.

It was Vale Private Counsel.

The request carried Charles Vale's office reference.

But the receiving authority was listed as:

**Dr. Peter Lang — Northbridge Clinical Review Unit.**

Edward leaned closer.

"Was the request fulfilled?"

"Not immediately."

"Why?"

"The clinic requested written consent from the mother before releasing the original identification materials."

"Did Isabella give consent?"

D'Souza searched further.

"Yes. There's a signed consent form dated the sixteenth day."

Jonathan looked at Edward.

"Then why did Northbridge report that the original material was missing?"

D'Souza continued reading.

"The clinic received a second instruction on the seventeenth day."

"What did it say?"

She opened the document.

**Previous request suspended. Original identification material to remain in clinic custody until receiving authority is confirmed.**

The signature was difficult to read.

But the typed name beneath it was clear.

**Eleanor Vale.**

Edward stared at the screen.

"Did Eleanor have authority to suspend Isabella's consent?"

D'Souza checked the attached documents.

"No. The clinic did not consider her the patient's legal representative."

"Then why was the instruction accepted?"

"It wasn't accepted as a cancellation. It was placed on the file as a dispute notice."

"Who submitted it?"

"Vale Private Counsel."

"Under whose authority?"

D'Souza opened the submission record.

The document had been delivered by a courier.

The sender's identity field was blank.

The courier's signature was available.

Edward read the name.

**Marianne Cross.**

Jonathan sat back.

"She submitted the instruction."

"Or delivered it," Edward said.

"That's the third time her name has appeared."

"Yes."

Edward examined the timestamp.

The instruction had been submitted at 11:42 a.m.

Victoria's receipt for the empty evidence pouch was recorded at 2:15 p.m.

Three hours and thirty-three minutes later.

He looked at the date.

The seventeenth day.

The same day both evidence deliveries had reached the Aurelius Family Office.

Edward felt the pieces shifting.

"Did the clinic ever release the original band?"

D'Souza nodded.

"On the twenty-eighth day, Eleanor Vale collected it."

"Was Isabella informed?"

"According to the correspondence, yes. She was told the band would be preserved pending verification."

"Was she told the comparison had been suspended?"

D'Souza searched.

"No."

Edward closed his notebook.

That was the problem.

Isabella had consented to the comparison.

The clinic had received her consent.

Someone had then submitted an instruction to delay the release of the material.

But the instruction had not cancelled her consent.

It had merely created a dispute that prevented the process from continuing.

And when the original band was finally collected, it went to Eleanor Vale rather than the comparison office.

Isabella was reluctant to meet Edward again.

Not because she had changed her mind about cooperating, but because each new discovery seemed to uncover another person who had handled her daughter's records without giving her a complete explanation.

She arrived at Edward's office with a small folder.

"I brought the original consent form," she said.

Edward stood.

"Thank you."

She sat down.

Jonathan placed a glass of water before her.

Edward showed her the clinic's archived request.

"Did you sign this?"

Isabella studied it.

"Yes."

"Do you remember signing it?"

"Of course."

"What were you told?"

"That the clinic needed my permission to release the original identification materials for comparison."

"Who asked you?"

"Charles."

Edward waited.

"He said the hospital had finally agreed to compare my maternity record with the St Catherine's records. He told me it was necessary because the documents had become confused."

"Did he explain what would be compared?"

"My maternity identification number and the infant record."

"Did he tell you the result might not establish biological identity?"

"He said it was a records comparison. I understood that."

Edward showed her the cancellation notice.

Isabella read it slowly.

Her expression changed when she reached the signature.

"Eleanor."

"You recognise it?"

"Yes."

"Did she speak to you about suspending the comparison?"

"No."

"Did she explain why she wanted the original band retained?"

Isabella looked up.

"She told me it was safer to keep the band separate."

"Did she explain that the clinic had already received your consent?"

"No."

Isabella placed the document on the table.

"Why does everyone keep deciding what I should be allowed to know?"

Nobody answered immediately.

She continued, her voice quieter.

"I was told my daughter had died. Then I was shown a photograph of a different child. Then I was told the photograph might not be mine. I was allowed to look at a bracelet, but not the baby. And every time I ask for an answer, someone tells me the records are complicated."

Edward understood that she was not asking him to promise a result.

She wanted the investigation to stop treating her as someone who needed to be protected from information.

"We found evidence that you consented to the comparison," he said. "We also found evidence that the original materials were not sent to the comparison office as planned."

"Because Eleanor stopped it?"

"We don't know whether she made that decision independently."

"But she signed the instruction."

"Yes."

Isabella looked away.

"She once told me she was trying to protect the child."

Edward remained still.

"Did she say which child?"

"No."

"Did she ever say she knew where your daughter was?"

Isabella shook her head.

"She said the records had to remain separate until someone could verify them."

Edward showed her the seventeenth-day letter addressed to Victoria.

Isabella read it.

Then she read the sentence about not informing her until the comparison had been completed.

A faint, bitter smile appeared on her face.

"They couldn't complete the comparison because they kept stopping it."

Jonathan looked down.

Isabella folded her hands.

"Did the Aurelius family know about my daughter?"

"Victoria received this letter. We cannot yet establish when she read it or what she understood."

"And Charles knew."

"Yes."

"Gabriel knew."

"He handled the transfer paperwork."

"And Eleanor knew."

"She handled the identification materials."

Isabella looked at Edward.

"Then who didn't know?"

Edward did not answer.

Because the question had become more difficult than it first appeared.

Perhaps the people whose names appeared on the documents knew more than they admitted.

Perhaps some of them knew less than the records suggested.

And perhaps the person who had organised the entire process had deliberately ensured that nobody possessed the complete picture.

That evening, Elena received an email from the Singapore International Academic Programme.

She read it twice.

Then she opened the attached document.

**Application received.**

**Academic review: Pending.**

**Additional supporting documents required.**

She immediately opened her laptop and began checking the list.

Academic transcripts.

Statement of purpose.

Teacher recommendation.

Proof of English proficiency.

Financial declaration.

She had expected most of it.

The only unexpected requirement was an interview.

Elena leaned back.

An interview was manageable.

She opened a new document and began preparing possible answers.

Why did she want to attend the programme?

What did she hope to learn?

How would the experience contribute to her future academic plans?

She typed a first draft.

Then deleted it.

It sounded too formal.

She tried again.

This time, she wrote honestly.

She wanted to study in an environment where she would have to solve unfamiliar problems. She wanted to understand how people from different academic backgrounds approached the same challenges. She wanted to explore business, public policy and technology without committing herself to a single career too early.

And she wanted to know what she could accomplish without relying on her family name.

She paused over the final sentence.

Then she deleted it.

It was true, but it sounded like an accusation against people who had not necessarily deserved one.

Instead, she wrote:

*I want to develop the ability to make independent decisions and take responsibility for the work I choose to pursue.*

Elena read the paragraph.

It sounded more like her.

A knock came at the door.

Catherine entered.

"Are you busy?"

"Just finishing an application."

Catherine noticed the programme name on the screen.

"Singapore?"

Elena nodded.

"I received a request for additional documents."

Catherine approached the desk.

"Have you decided to apply?"

"Yes."

"Have you considered how long you would be away?"

"Six months initially."

Catherine looked at the application.

She was quiet for a moment.

Then she said, "If you want to go, we can discuss the arrangements."

Elena looked at her.

"You aren't going to tell me to stay?"

Catherine's expression softened.

"No."

"Even with everything happening here?"

Catherine lowered her eyes.

"Especially with everything happening here. You shouldn't have to put your life on hold because the adults around you haven't resolved their mistakes."

Elena did not know what to say.

Catherine reached out and gently straightened the papers.

"Just make sure you understand the programme before accepting anything."

"I have."

"Then finish your application."

She turned towards the door.

"Mother?"

Catherine stopped.

Elena hesitated.

"Thank you."

Catherine nodded.

She did not try to hug her.

She did not ask for forgiveness.

She simply left the room.

Elena returned to the application.

For the first time in several days, she felt that a decision about her future was being treated as her own.

Edward was still at his office when Helena called.

"We found the second evidence register."

He sat upright.

"What second register?"

"The external correspondence archive. It was stored separately from the Family Office's main evidence inventory."

"Does it contain the original envelope records?"

"Yes. And something else."

"What?"

Helena paused.

"There are three entries connected to IV-204. Not two."

Edward opened his notebook.

"Read them."

"The first is the seventeenth-day delivery to Victoria Aurelius. The second is the twenty-first-day receipt of an additional document from Northbridge Clinical Review Unit."

"And the third?"

"The twenty-eighth-day transfer of the original identification band to Eleanor Vale."

Edward waited.

"What was the twenty-first-day document?"

"A comparison status notice."

"Who signed it?"

"Dr. Peter Lang."

"What did it say?"

Helena read from the register.

"Comparison materials received. Original neonatal identification card unavailable. Comparison suspended. No result issued."

Edward closed his eyes briefly.

That confirmed what they had already suspected.

But Helena was not finished.

"The document also lists the party responsible for supplying the missing identification card."

Edward opened his eyes.

"Who?"

"St Catherine's Records Department."

"That's impossible. The card was delivered to Holt on the seventeenth day."

"Yes."

"Then why was it recorded as unavailable?"

Helena was silent for a moment.

"Because the comparison office was not referring to the card Holt received."

Edward slowly sat back.

"What was it referring to?"

"The original neonatal identification card required for the laboratory comparison."

He looked at the photograph of the card on his desk.

The one bearing the STC-A reference and the handwritten IV-204 notation.

The card in the Aurelius archive might have been an administrative identification card.

But the comparison office had requested something else.

Something that had never arrived.

Edward looked at the date on the status notice.

The twenty-first day.

Then he read the final line.

**Original sample card retained at St Catherine's under restricted neonatal custody. Release authority: originating clinician only.**

He understood.

The card in the archive was not the missing evidence.

It was a record of the missing evidence.

And somewhere inside St Catherine's, eighteen years earlier, a second identification card had remained under restricted custody.

The comparison had never failed because the samples were proven incompatible.

It had failed because the essential original material had never reached the people responsible for examining it.

Edward picked up his telephone.

"Jonathan."

"Yes?"

"Cancel tomorrow's appointment with Dr. Lang."

"Why?"

"We're going back to St Catherine's."

He looked once more at the archive photograph.

"Ask them to locate the restricted neonatal custody register."

"And if they can't?"

Edward's expression hardened.

"Then we'll find out who signed it out."

Because the next question was no longer whether the comparison had been completed.

It was who had taken away the one piece of evidence that could have allowed it to begin.
