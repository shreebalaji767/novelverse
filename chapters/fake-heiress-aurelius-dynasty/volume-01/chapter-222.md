# **Chapter 222 — The Two Originals**

The appointment was approved at 9:12 a.m.

Dr. Miriam Cole received separate written authorisations from St Catherine's and Meridian.

Neither institution surrendered custody of its original material.

St Catherine's would retain the maternity identification slip.

Meridian would retain the neonatal band material.

Both would make the originals available for examination under controlled conditions.

The authorisations were deliberately separate.

St Catherine's approved examination of its maternity source.

Meridian approved examination of the band and its documented custody history.

Neither institution authorised a conclusion about biological parentage.

Edward read both letters before signing the investigation's receipt.

“Now we have an examination,” Eleanor said.

“We have permission to examine two items.”

“That's what I meant.”

“It's important that we keep the wording precise.”

She gave him a tired look.

“You've started sounding like Dr. Hale.”

Edward almost smiled.

“Perhaps I've finally learned something.”

The examination took place at Meridian three days later.

Dr. Cole arrived with a small team.

The room had been prepared in advance. The two original items would be brought in separately, their seals checked, and their condition photographed before any comparison began.

Edward and Eleanor were permitted to observe from the adjoining room.

They were not permitted to handle the materials.

Dr. Cole began with the maternity identification slip.

The original register reference was recorded.

The paper was examined under magnification.

The visible writing, ink characteristics, physical condition, and attachment to the original register were documented.

She then examined the neonatal band material.

The partial reference remained visible.

**M-204.**

The Meridian Receiving Unit stamp was incomplete.

The material showed signs of cutting and later handling.

Dr. Cole photographed both sides.

She did not attempt to reconstruct missing portions.

After the initial examination, she placed the images side by side.

A short pause followed.

Edward leaned closer to the observation window.

“What is she looking at?” Eleanor asked.

“The reference markings.”

Dr. Cole returned to the original maternity slip.

Then she examined the band again.

She made a note.

Another photograph was taken.

The first comparison was complete.

The originals were resealed separately.

No sample had been removed.

No alteration had been made to either item.

The preliminary report arrived two days later.

Edward opened it in Eleanor's office.

**Examination reference:** IC-222/01.

**Examiner:** Dr. Miriam Cole.

**Materials examined:**

- Original St Catherine's maternity identification slip, HIST-27/06-A.
- Preserved neonatal band material, HIST-27/06-C, Meridian archive reference R-17/01.

**Findings:**

The maternity slip contained an original entry identifying a female infant and a recorded maternity reference.

The neonatal band contained a partial reference, M-204, and a partial Meridian Receiving Unit stamp.

The examiner identified no complete matching identifier visible on both items.

The band material was consistent with historical neonatal identification material.

Its surviving markings were insufficient to establish that it belonged to the infant described in the maternity slip.

**Conclusion: Association between the two original materials is not established.**

Edward read the conclusion twice.

Eleanor sat opposite him.

“So they don't match?”

“That's not what it says.”

“Then what does it say?”

“We cannot establish that they match. We also cannot establish that they don't.”

She looked at the report.

“The band is incomplete.”

“Yes.”

“And the maternity slip has its own reference.”

“Yes.”

“Then we still don't know whether they concern the same infant.”

“Correct.”

Edward turned to the limitations section.

The report explained that the missing portion of the band prevented a reliable comparison of the complete reference.

The custody gap between Day 11 and Day 14 also limited the examiner's ability to establish that the band had remained continuously under controlled access.

The Day-12 shared account entry remained unresolved.

No individual operator had been identified.

Dr. Cole had not attributed the band to a particular infant.

She had not connected it to STC-A.

She had not connected it to IV-204.

She had not connected it to Elena.

Eleanor closed the report.

“We've spent months getting permission to examine two original objects, and we still have no identity.”

“We have something else.”

“What?”

“We now know exactly what the available originals can and cannot establish.”

Edward placed the preliminary report beside the original Day-6 amendment response.

The receiving desk had said the identity was unconfirmed.

Dr. Cole's examination had not changed that.

The later administrative summaries had claimed much more than the original evidence supported.

At 11:30, Dr. Hale joined them.

He read the preliminary report without interruption.

When he reached the conclusion, he nodded.

“That is a proper finding.”

“It's inconclusive,” Eleanor said.

“It is properly inconclusive.”

“Could further examination help?”

“Possibly. But we cannot manufacture missing information.”

Edward showed him the Day-12 access record.

“Could the shared account entry explain the missing portion?”

“It could explain an undocumented handling event. It does not prove that the band was altered or that anyone removed information deliberately.”

“Can the operator be identified?”

“Not from this record alone.”

“Who controlled the archive at the time?”

“Meridian's Restricted Records Custodianship Unit supervised access. The unit can tell you which accounts were active. It may not be able to identify the individual who used the terminal.”

Edward wrote that down.

“Did the custodianship unit have authority to determine identity?”

“No. It had authority over storage and access.”

“Could it authorise the band comparison?”

“It could authorise access to the stored item within the preservation framework. The source authority still had to approve examination of its own records.”

Dr. Hale paused.

“You should also examine the original receiving response.”

“The response to the Day-6 amendment?”

“Yes. It matters because it shows what the receiving office understood the proposed reference to mean.”

Edward already had the document.

He placed it on the table.

The response was short.

**The proposed receiving reference is acknowledged for reconciliation purposes only. The receiving identity remains unconfirmed. Original identification material is required before any identity conclusion can be considered.**

Dr. Hale tapped the final sentence.

“That was the position.”

“And the Day-28 summary changed it,” Edward said.

“Yes.”

“Did you approve that summary?”

“No.”

“Did anyone ask you to?”

“Not that I recall.”

Edward placed the response beside the Day-28 summary.

The contrast was now documented.

The receiving office had expressly refused to confirm the identity.

The later summary had described the reference as established.

No new original evidence had been produced between the two documents.

The next question was the original receiving desk.

Its records had been transferred to an archive managed by the Meridian administrative division.

Edward requested the complete reconciliation file.

The response arrived late that afternoon.

It contained a cover sheet, the original receiving response, two internal routing notes, and a later migration extract.

The first routing note was dated Day 8.

It instructed the receiving desk to retain the proposed reference but prohibited any permanent identity entry.

The second was dated Day 14.

It stated:

**Original identification material remains outstanding. Do not convert the proposed reference into a confirmed receiving identity.**

Edward read the line aloud.

Eleanor stared at the page.

“That's explicit.”

“Yes.”

“Someone knew the distinction.”

“The receiving desk did.”

The Day-14 note had been signed by a records officer.

It had not been included in the later Day-28 summary.

Edward checked the migration extract.

The extract cited the Day-6 amendment and the Day-8 routing note.

It did not cite the Day-14 restriction.

The authoring account was again a shared administrative terminal.

The document did not identify the operator.

Eleanor pointed to the routing field.

“Who received the Day-14 note?”

Edward checked.

“Migration coordination.”

“Was it acknowledged?”

“Yes.”

“By whom?”

“Shared terminal MC-MIG/02.”

“Is that a person?”

“No. It's an account.”

Edward leaned back.

The original receiving office had repeated its restriction.

The migration system had received it.

But the later summary still claimed an established identity.

He wrote a new request.

**Identify the Day-14 restriction's handling history, including receipt, classification, onward routing, and any exclusion from subsequent summary preparation.**

At the Aurelius estate, Julian was sitting at the dining table with his notebook open.

Victoria placed a glass of water beside him.

“You've been writing for an hour.”

“I'm updating the list.”

“Again?”

“Some of the ideas are bad.”

Victoria sat opposite him.

“Which ones?”

Julian turned the notebook around.

Several entries had been crossed out.

One said:

*Take her to every place in the city.*

Another:

*Buy her everything she missed.*

Victoria read them.

“Why did you remove those?”

“Because I don't know whether she'd want any of that.”

He turned to the final page.

Item 50 remained.

**Ask her what she wants to do, instead of deciding everything myself.**

Below it, he had written:

**Finding someone is not the same as being ready to meet them.**

Victoria touched the edge of the notebook.

“You're learning to be patient.”

“I'm trying.”

“That's enough for now.”

Julian closed the notebook.

“Do we know anything new?”

“Not anything that would justify making promises.”

“Does that mean we're further away?”

Victoria considered the question.

“No. It means we are refusing to pretend that uncertainty is an answer.”

Julian nodded.

He did not look happy.

But he did not argue.

The next morning, Elena's pilot preparation meeting began at 9:00.

Mr. Lim had prepared six sample applications using fictional data.

The administrator, two reviewers, and delivery officer were present.

Elena demonstrated the workflow.

The administrator uploaded an application.

The system recorded receipt.

The first reviewer opened the supporting documents and marked one as requiring further verification.

The second reviewer independently assessed the application.

Neither reviewer could see the other's preliminary assessment until both had submitted.

The committee's decision field remained unavailable.

Elena showed them why.

“The system records evidence and actions. It doesn't decide who deserves the grant.”

Mr. Lim nodded.

“And if one reviewer leaves?”

“The replacement must receive a new authorisation. Their access begins from the recorded effective time. They don't inherit the previous reviewer's decisions or identity.”

One of the reviewers asked, “What if we need to correct a submitted assessment?”

“The original remains preserved. A correction is recorded separately, with the reason, author, and time.”

“Can the correction replace the original?”

“No.”

The reviewer considered this.

“That will make the audit trail longer.”

“Yes,” Elena said. “It will also make it more honest.”

Ms. Tan smiled.

The pilot would begin the following month.

Elena had not yet been asked to make any final implementation commitment.

She was relieved.

She wanted the system tested before anyone relied on it.

At 4:20 p.m., Edward received the handling history for the Day-14 restriction.

It had been received by migration coordination.

The account log showed that it had been opened.

But the system did not preserve the name of the individual operator.

A classification entry had been added.

**Administrative clarification — non-blocking.**

The instruction was then stored in a migration reference folder.

It was not attached to the Day-28 summary preparation pack.

Edward examined the classification rule.

The rule had been introduced under the same continuity migration framework.

It allowed receiving-route documents to be treated as non-blocking when they did not contain a formal withdrawal or direct challenge to the migration authority.

The rule had been applied to the Day-14 restriction.

But the restriction was not a request to withdraw the migration.

It was a direct statement from the receiving authority that identity remained unconfirmed.

Eleanor read the classification.

“So it was treated as non-blocking because it didn't challenge the migration.”

“Yes.”

“But it challenged the identity description.”

“Exactly.”

“Who created the rule?”

Edward opened the rule's authorisation page.

The instruction had been prepared by Daniel Mercer.

It had been approved under the temporary continuity framework.

The approving officer was Margaret Ellison.

Margaret Voss's desk had implemented the classification procedure.

Three distinct roles.

One increasingly distorted result.

Edward looked at the Day-14 restriction again.

The receiving office had not merely failed to provide confirmation.

It had explicitly refused to provide one.

And the system had classified that refusal as something that did not need to interrupt the migration.

Eleanor stood.

“Then the next interview is with Mercer.”

“Yes.”

“Not Voss?”

“We'll interview both again if necessary. But first I want to know why he designed a rule that treated an identity restriction as non-blocking.”

Edward prepared the request.

Before sending it, he added one final question:

**Did you understand that the Day-14 receiving authority restriction would be excluded from the evidence pack used to prepare the Day-28 identity summary?**

He pressed send.

The investigation had finally reached the point where the original evidence and the later administrative claims could be compared directly.

The two originals had not established an identity.

The receiving desk had said so.

And someone had built a process in which that answer could disappear without the record ever being formally changed.
