# **Chapter 35 — The Mother Who Was Told Her Child Was Dead**

For several seconds, nobody spoke.

The elderly woman sat beside the window, holding the photograph between both hands.

Edward had seen many documents during the investigation.

Birth records.

Transfer registers.

Legal authorisations.

Handwritten instructions.

Photographs of hospital employees who had died years ago.

But this was different.

It was the first time someone had placed a living memory in front of him.

A woman who had received a newborn child.

A woman who had been told that the child's mother believed her baby was dead.

And a woman who had apparently spent eighteen years keeping that secret.

Edward lowered his voice.

"May I ask your name?"

The elderly woman looked at him.

"Which one?"

"Your real name."

She considered the question.

"Margaret is my name."

"Is it your full name?"

"No."

She looked at the photograph again.

"My full name was Margaret Ellison."

Edward made a note.

"Mrs. Ellison, I want to understand what happened. But you don't have to answer everything today."

The woman gave a faint, humourless smile.

"Today?"

She looked toward the window.

"I've been waiting eighteen years for someone to ask."

The administrator had arranged for the conversation to take place in a private sitting room.

She remained nearby, aware that Margaret's memory sometimes became confused when she was tired.

Edward and Pierce did not press her.

They began with simple questions.

The date.

The hospital.

The man who had brought the baby.

Margaret answered some easily.

Others required long pauses.

"I wasn't supposed to go to the hospital," she said eventually.

"Why?"

"Because the arrangement was private."

"What arrangement?"

Margaret looked at him.

"I had applied to care for a child."

"Through a legal office?"

"Yes."

"Was it an adoption?"

She shook her head.

"Not at first. They said it was temporary."

"Who said that?"

"A lawyer. Charles."

"Charles Vale?"

"Yes."

Edward waited.

"Did you know him before the ninth day?"

"I met him twice. The first time was in his office. The second time was at the hospital."

"What did he tell you?"

"That a child needed somewhere safe to stay."

"Did he tell you why?"

Margaret looked down at the photograph.

"He said the family situation was complicated."

"Which family?"

"I didn't know."

"Did he mention the Aurelius family?"

She shook her head.

"The Valmont family?"

Again, she shook her head.

"Then what did he tell you about the child's parents?"

Margaret took a moment.

"Nothing."

"Nothing?"

"He said the child was not to be discussed as someone's daughter. Not yet."

Edward exchanged a glance with Pierce.

"Did he tell you the child's name?"

"No."

"Did you give her one?"

Margaret's expression changed.

"Yes."

"What did you call her?"

She looked down at the photograph.

"Anna."

Edward wrote it down.

"Was that the name used on her hospital records?"

"No. They told me not to use a name from the hospital."

"Why?"

"Because it might cause confusion later."

Edward looked at the photograph.

The infant's hospital band was partially visible.

There was no name.

Only the letter A and the birth time.

"Mrs. Ellison, did you understand that the child might be returned to her biological mother?"

"Yes."

"Did you agree to that?"

"Of course."

Her answer came immediately.

"She wasn't mine."

She paused.

"But I cared for her."

Edward nodded.

"How long did you expect to keep her?"

"A few days. Perhaps a few weeks."

"And how long did you actually keep her?"

Margaret looked at him.

"Eighteen years."

Edward did not ask the next question immediately.

He knew what it was.

He also knew that asking it too quickly might cause her to withdraw.

Instead, he asked about the first day.

"Who brought Anna to you?"

"Charles."

"Was anyone else present?"

"A man from the hospital."

"Marcus Morelli?"

Margaret thought for a moment.

"Yes."

"Did he speak to you?"

"He checked the papers."

"Did he explain the arrangement?"

"No. Charles did that."

"Did Marcus seem comfortable with it?"

Margaret looked toward the window.

"No."

Edward waited.

"He looked at the baby for a long time," she continued. "Then he asked Charles whether the mother had been informed."

"What did Charles say?"

"That everything had been arranged."

"Did Marcus believe him?"

"I don't know."

"Did he say anything else?"

Margaret's fingers moved across the photograph.

"He told me to keep the baby safe."

Edward leaned forward.

"Did he tell you who she was?"

"No."

"Did he tell you who her mother was?"

"No."

"Did he tell you why she needed protection?"

Margaret shook her head.

"He only said that the child must not be taken somewhere else until the proper people came."

Edward wrote the sentence down.

"Did those people ever come?"

Margaret was silent.

Then she shook her head.

"No."

The conversation continued for another twenty minutes.

Margaret described the first weeks.

A small rented house.

A cot borrowed from a neighbour.

Formula, blankets, medical appointments, and the endless uncertainty of waiting for someone to contact her.

Charles Vale had visited twice.

The first time, he brought a new set of documents.

The second time, he told her that the arrangement would continue.

"Did he explain why?" Edward asked.

"He said the original family could not receive the child yet."

"Which family?"

"He wouldn't tell me."

"Did he say the child's mother had agreed?"

Margaret frowned.

"He said the mother had been informed."

"Did he say she had agreed?"

"No."

"Did you ask?"

"Yes."

"What did he tell you?"

Margaret's expression became troubled.

"He said the mother had received the information and had chosen not to come."

Edward felt something tighten in his chest.

"Did you believe him?"

"At first."

"And later?"

She looked at the photograph.

"Later, I began to wonder."

"Why?"

"Because the baby was healthy. She was growing. There was no reason for someone not to come if they knew where she was."

Edward looked at Pierce.

"Did you ever contact the hospital yourself?"

"No."

"Why not?"

"Charles told me that doing so would expose the child to the people who had arranged the confusion."

"Which people?"

"I didn't know."

Margaret's voice became softer.

"I was frightened."

Edward nodded.

He did not blame her for that.

She had been given a child with no established identity, incomplete documents, and a warning that someone might come looking for her.

She had been told the mother knew.

She had been told the mother had chosen not to come.

And she had been asked to keep the child safe.

Whatever the truth, Margaret had been placed in an impossible position.

When Edward finally asked about the photograph, Margaret held it out.

He examined it carefully.

The infant was wrapped in a pale blanket.

The identification band was visible.

**A — 2:17.**

"Was this taken at the hospital?"

"Yes."

"On the ninth day?"

"I think so."

"Who took it?"

"Marcus."

Edward looked up.

"Marcus Morelli?"

"Yes."

"Why?"

"He said he wanted a record of the child before she left."

Edward examined the photograph again.

"Did he take a photograph of the other infant?"

Margaret looked confused.

"What other infant?"

Edward did not answer immediately.

He turned the photograph around.

On the back was a handwritten date.

And a short sentence.

**One child delivered. One child still awaiting confirmation.**

The handwriting resembled Morelli's.

Edward showed it to Pierce.

Pierce examined it.

"This may be significant."

Margaret looked between them.

"What does it mean?"

Edward chose his words carefully.

"It may mean that another child was involved in the same arrangement."

Margaret stared at him.

"Another?"

"Yes."

She shook her head slowly.

"No. Charles told me there was only one."

Edward looked at her.

"Did you ever see another baby?"

"No."

"Did anyone tell you that the hospital had two infants in private care?"

"No."

Margaret's expression began to change.

"Why are you asking me that?"

Edward hesitated.

"Because there were two girls."

Margaret looked at the photograph.

Then she looked at him.

"Two girls?"

"Yes."

"Both?"

"Both were born at the same hospital on the same night."

Margaret's hands began to tremble.

She placed the photograph on the table.

"Then..."

She stopped.

Edward waited.

"Then what happened to the other one?"

The question remained unanswered.

Edward did not tell Margaret that the other infant had also been released on the ninth day.

He did not tell her about the unidentified woman who had received that child.

He did not tell her that one family had been searching for a missing daughter for eighteen years.

There was too much uncertainty.

Instead, he asked one more question.

"Mrs. Ellison, do you remember whether anyone contacted you after the first year?"

"Yes."

"Who?"

"A woman."

"Do you remember her name?"

Margaret shook her head.

"She didn't give one."

"What did she want?"

"To see the child."

Edward leaned forward.

"Did she say she was the mother?"

"No."

"Did she identify herself?"

"No."

"Did you let her see Anna?"

Margaret hesitated.

"Yes."

Edward looked at Pierce.

"Where?"

"At the house."

"Did she meet the child?"

"Only for a few minutes."

"What did she say?"

Margaret looked down.

"She asked whether the child was happy."

Edward felt the significance of the question immediately.

"Did she say anything about the child's mother?"

Margaret nodded.

"She asked whether the mother had ever come."

"What did you tell her?"

"I told her what Charles had told me."

"That the mother had been informed and had chosen not to come?"

"Yes."

"How did the woman react?"

Margaret's eyes filled.

"She cried."

Edward waited.

"Then she asked me to keep the child safe."

"Did she give you any explanation?"

"No."

"Did she leave anything?"

Margaret slowly reached toward the small drawer beside her chair.

She opened it and removed a folded piece of paper.

It had been kept carefully inside a transparent sleeve.

She handed it to Edward.

The paper was old.

The handwriting was delicate.

There was no name at the bottom.

Only one sentence.

**If she ever asks why nobody came for her, tell her that someone did.**

Edward read it twice.

Then he looked at Margaret.

"Who gave you this?"

"The woman."

"Do you remember what she looked like?"

"Yes."

"Can you describe her?"

Margaret looked toward the window.

"She was young."

"How young?"

"Perhaps thirty. Maybe a little older."

"Was she connected to the hospital?"

"I don't know."

"Did she look familiar?"

Margaret hesitated.

Then she shook her head.

"No. But she looked as though she had been crying for a long time."

The interview ended shortly afterward.

Margaret was tired.

The administrator suggested that she rest.

Edward and Pierce stepped outside.

For several moments, neither spoke.

Then Pierce said, "We need to identify the woman who visited her."

"Yes."

"She may have been the mother."

"Or someone acting on the mother's behalf."

"She asked whether the mother had come."

"Which suggests she knew about the arrangement."

Pierce considered the note.

"If she knew the child was alive, why didn't she tell the mother?"

Edward looked down at the folded paper.

*If she ever asks why nobody came for her, tell her that someone did.*

"Perhaps she wasn't allowed to."

"By whom?"

Edward looked back toward the care facility.

"That's what we need to find out."

The first person Edward contacted was Marcus Morelli.

Morelli answered after the second ring.

"I met Margaret Ellison today."

There was silence.

Then Morelli asked, "How is she?"

"She remembers the arrangement."

"That is not what I asked."

Edward looked at the facility.

"She's alive. Her memory is uneven, but she remembers the child."

Morelli exhaled.

"Good."

"She told us that Charles brought the baby."

"Yes."

"She said you were present."

"Yes."

"She also said a woman visited her approximately a year later."

Morelli did not answer.

"Do you know who that woman was?"

"No."

"Did you arrange the visit?"

"No."

"Did Eleanor?"

Morelli's silence was longer this time.

"I don't know."

Edward looked at the note.

"She left a message."

"What message?"

Edward read it aloud.

There was a faint sound at the other end of the call.

A chair moving.

Then Morelli spoke.

"I remember that sentence."

Edward straightened.

"You remember it?"

"Yes."

"Who wrote it?"

"I don't know."

"Did you see the woman?"

"No."

"Then how do you know the sentence?"

"Because I received a copy."

"From whom?"

"Charles."

Edward's grip tightened.

"Why?"

"He said the woman had visited the child and left the message."

"Did he identify her?"

"No."

"Did he tell you why she had visited?"

Morelli was quiet.

Then he said something Edward had not expected.

"She was looking for the other child."

Edward stared at the phone.

"The other child?"

"Yes."

"She visited Margaret to look for Infant B?"

"No."

Morelli's voice became strained.

"She was looking for the child who had not been delivered."

Edward looked at Pierce.

"Where was that child?"

Morelli did not answer immediately.

"That is what I have been trying to find out."

At the Aurelius residence, Alexander received Edward's report that evening.

Victoria listened without interrupting.

When Edward finished, she looked at the photograph of the infant.

"She was called Anna?"

"By Margaret Ellison."

"Did Margaret know the child's biological identity?"

"No."

"Did she know who the mother was?"

"No. She believed the mother had been informed and had chosen not to come."

Victoria looked at the note.

*If she ever asks why nobody came for her, tell her that someone did.*

"Who was the woman who visited her?"

"We don't know."

Alexander looked toward Edward.

"But Morelli believes she was looking for the other infant."

"Yes."

"Not the child she had visited."

"Correct."

Adrian frowned.

"Why would someone looking for one child visit the guardian of the other?"

Edward placed the two birth records side by side.

"Because the children were connected."

Lucien leaned forward.

"Perhaps the woman knew both children had been separated."

"Possibly."

Damien looked at the note.

"Or perhaps she believed the other child was being kept there."

Victoria's eyes remained on the photograph.

"Did Margaret say whether the woman looked like either of the mothers?"

"No. She had never met either mother."

Victoria lowered her gaze.

"Then we have to identify her before we decide what she knew."

Alexander nodded.

"Adrian, contact the old legal office and check every visitor record connected to Margaret's address."

"Understood."

"Also examine the correspondence sent to Charles Vale after the first year."

Adrian wrote it down.

Julian had been sitting quietly.

Now he looked at the photograph.

"Father?"

"Yes?"

"Could Anna be our sister?"

Alexander paused.

"She could be."

"Could she be someone else's daughter?"

"Yes."

Julian looked down.

He was beginning to understand how little they knew.

He had spent weeks imagining that the investigation would eventually produce a name, a location, and a person.

Instead, every answer seemed to reveal another person who had been left out of the story.

He closed his notebook.

"Then we need to find out who she is before we call her our sister."

Victoria looked at him.

"Yes."

At the Valmont estate, Elena was reading a message from the university.

Her interview had been successful.

She had been invited to the next stage of the selection process.

She read the message twice before calling Clara.

"Guess what?"

"Did you get selected?"

"Not yet. I've been invited to the next stage."

Clara shouted loudly enough that Elena moved the phone away from her ear.

"THAT COUNTS."

Elena laughed.

"It does not count as final selection."

"It counts as progress."

"Yes. Progress."

"Emergency biscuits are officially approved."

Elena looked toward the garden.

For the first time in several days, she felt genuinely excited about something that had nothing to do with her birth.

She had a future to prepare for.

A programme she wanted to join.

A career she had not yet chosen.

A life that was still taking shape.

She did not know that an elderly woman in a care facility had given investigators a photograph of an infant who might have been her.

She did not know that someone had once visited that infant and left a message saying that someone had come for her.

And she did not know that another child had remained undelivered.

At 9:16 p.m., Edward received the results of a search through the old correspondence archive.

The investigation team had found a letter sent to Charles Vale eleven months after the births.

It had no sender's name.

The envelope was addressed to Vale's private office.

The letter contained only three lines.

**You promised that both children would remain safe.**

**One has been placed. The other has not.**

**Do not let the mother discover which one is missing.**

Edward read it again.

One child had been placed.

The other had not.

That meant the ninth-day release had not completed the arrangement.

At least one infant had remained outside the receiving guardian's care.

And someone had known.

He turned the page.

There was a small handwritten notation at the bottom.

**Received personally by Charles Vale. Forwarded to M. Morelli.**

Edward picked up his phone.

He called Morelli.

No answer.

He tried again.

Still nothing.

Then a message arrived from an unknown number.

It contained no greeting.

Only a photograph of a handwritten page.

At the top was a date.

**TENTH DAY AFTER BIRTHS.**

Below it was a single line.

**The second child has been located. Do not tell either family.**

Edward stared at the message.

For eighteen years, everyone had assumed that both children had left the hospital on the ninth day.

But the new document suggested something else.

One child had been placed.

The other had remained missing for at least another day.

And whoever located her on the tenth day had ordered that neither family be informed.

Edward slowly lowered the phone.

The ninth-day release had not been the end of the arrangement.

It had been the beginning of another one.
