# **Chapter 18 — What the Records Could Not Prove**

The folder remained open on the conference table.

Nobody had touched it for several minutes.

The recovered names were still visible beneath the transparent protective sheet.

**VALMONT — ELENA**

**AURELIUS — ISABELLE**

Two names.

Two families.

Two children whose lives had apparently been assigned to the wrong destinations before either of them was old enough to open her eyes.

Alexander Aurelius stood at the far end of the table, reading the same lines for the third time.

Adrian watched him.

"Father."

Alexander did not look up.

"These are provisional placements," Adrian said. "The report explicitly says they cannot establish biological identity."

"I can read."

"I know."

"Then don't repeat it."

Adrian fell silent.

Across the table, Edward Valmont turned another page.

"There is something else."

Richard looked at him. "What?"

"The handwriting on the placement section doesn't match the handwriting on the original report."

Sebastian leaned forward.

"You're certain?"

"Not completely. The document has been examined under magnification, but the ink and writing pressure suggest at least two different people handled this section."

Edward placed two photographs beside the folder.

One showed the original entry.

The other showed the recovered writing beneath the adhesive strip.

"The placement names were added later," he explained. "We don't know when. We don't know who wrote them. And we don't know whether the person who wrote them had accurate information."

Damien, who had remained near the door, finally spoke.

"Could someone have deliberately written the opposite placements?"

"Yes," Edward answered. "Or they could have recorded an arrangement that was never completed. Or they could have been trying to correct an earlier mistake."

Lucien glanced at the photographs.

"So the document tells us that somebody wrote two names on a page eighteen years ago."

"Essentially," Sebastian said.

Lucien sighed.

"An impressive amount of paper for something that tells us almost nothing."

"Not nothing," Jonathan Pierce corrected.

Everyone turned towards him.

The elderly lawyer had been examining the folder's binding.

"It tells us that someone was concerned enough about the two children's placements to record them separately. It also tells us that the person who wrote them did not consider those placements final."

He tapped the words *Provisional placement only*.

"Someone expected the situation to change."

Victoria was not present.

She had remained at the Aurelius Estate, waiting for the first verified account of what had happened to the child she had lost eighteen years ago.

Alexander looked at the photograph again.

"Find out who wrote it."

Pierce nodded.

"And find out who removed the strip."

"Yes."

Richard closed the folder.

"Before we proceed any further, I want one thing made clear."

His voice was controlled, but there was no mistaking the firmness beneath it.

"Neither girl is to be treated as a conclusion waiting to happen. We have documents, incomplete records and a laboratory report that has not been finalised. We do not make decisions about their lives based on a provisional note."

Alexander regarded him for a moment.

"Agreed."

It was the first thing they had agreed upon without argument that morning.

Unfortunately, agreement did not make the truth any easier to find.

At the Valmont Estate, Elena was having a much more ordinary problem.

Her laptop had stopped responding.

She stared at the frozen screen.

Then she pressed a key.

Nothing.

She pressed another.

Still nothing.

"Don't do this to me."

The laptop remained silent.

Elena leaned back in her chair.

She had a university assignment due in three days, an examination approaching, and a growing collection of documents she had been meaning to organise.

Now her computer had apparently decided that none of those things mattered.

A knock sounded at the door.

"Come in."

Nicholas entered carrying two cups of tea.

"Why are you glaring at your laptop?"

"It has betrayed me."

"Have you tried apologising?"

"I've tried threatening it."

"Ah. A reasonable escalation."

He placed one cup beside her.

Elena pointed at the screen.

"It froze while I was saving my assignment."

Nicholas leaned over.

"Did you save it?"

"I was trying to."

"That's unfortunate."

"Thank you for your sympathy."

"You're welcome."

He pulled out the chair beside her and examined the screen.

After a few seconds, he reached for the power button.

"Wait. I might lose everything."

"Then we won't touch it."

They waited.

The laptop continued displaying the same frozen document.

Nicholas took a sip of tea.

Elena looked at him.

"You're enjoying this."

"I'm enjoying the fact that someone else is having a worse morning than I am."

"That's a terrible quality in a brother."

"I have several."

She laughed despite herself.

Nicholas eventually helped her restart the laptop. Fortunately, the assignment had autosaved most of its contents.

Elena checked the document.

Only one paragraph was missing.

She let out a relieved breath.

"Saved."

"Good. Now you can stop looking like you're attending a funeral."

"I was preparing to write a very angry email to the manufacturer."

"You can still do that. It might improve their day."

Elena closed the document and took her tea.

For a while, neither spoke.

Then Nicholas glanced at her.

"Are you still worried?"

She knew what he meant.

The hospital investigation.

The records.

The Aurelius family.

The possibility that everything she understood about her own history might be wrong.

"A little."

"Only a little?"

"Would you prefer a dramatic answer?"

"Naturally."

Elena considered it.

"I'm worried that eighteen years of my life might turn out to be a clerical error."

Nicholas stopped smiling.

She continued before he could respond.

"But I'm also worried that everyone will become so occupied with finding out where I came from that they'll forget I've got an examination next week."

Nicholas looked at her for a moment.

Then he nodded.

"That's fair."

"Thank you."

"I'll remind everyone that your examination is more important than their mysterious paperwork."

"Please don't."

"Too late. I'll make an announcement at dinner."

"You're impossible."

"Yet beloved."

Elena threw a cushion at him.

He caught it.

And for a few minutes, the matter was forgotten.

Isabelle was in the library when Elena came downstairs.

She had a notebook open in front of her and a university prospectus beside it.

Elena stopped at the entrance.

"Research?"

Isabelle looked up.

"Trying to decide between two programmes."

"Which ones?"

"Business administration and economics."

Elena came closer.

"Those are quite different."

"I know. That's why I'm having trouble."

She turned the prospectus towards Elena.

"I've always thought business administration would be more practical. But economics interests me more."

Elena pulled out the chair opposite her.

"What do you want to do after university?"

Isabelle hesitated.

"Something in finance, perhaps. Or management. I'm not entirely sure."

"Then maybe you should think about which subjects you'd enjoy studying for three or four years."

"That's not very helpful."

"It's honest."

Isabelle smiled faintly.

Elena examined the course descriptions.

"Look at the optional modules too. Sometimes the main programme names sound similar, but the actual subjects are quite different."

Isabelle took her notebook.

"You've already decided what you're studying, haven't you?"

"Mostly."

"Economics?"

"Yes. Though I'm still comparing universities."

"Your family could probably arrange a place anywhere."

Elena looked up.

"Probably."

"Doesn't that make the decision easier?"

"Not really."

Isabelle tilted her head.

"Why?"

"Because I don't want to choose a university just because someone can arrange it for me."

Elena returned the prospectus.

"I want to study somewhere I'll actually learn something. And somewhere I can make my own decisions."

Isabelle studied her expression.

"You really mean that."

"Of course."

"I thought being part of a wealthy family would make everything easier."

"It makes some things easier."

Elena shrugged.

"But it also means people sometimes assume you don't need to work for anything."

Isabelle looked down at her notebook.

"I know what you mean."

There was a pause.

Then Isabelle spoke quietly.

"Do you think they'll find out which of us belongs to which family?"

Elena did not answer immediately.

"I think they'll find out the biological facts."

"And after that?"

"I don't know."

Isabelle looked at her.

"Are you frightened?"

"Yes."

The admission seemed to surprise Isabelle.

Elena folded her hands on the table.

"I don't know what the answer will change. I don't know whether people will expect me to behave differently. I don't know what happens if the records say something none of us wants to hear."

She looked towards the tall windows.

"But I don't think worrying about it will make the result arrive faster."

Isabelle let out a quiet laugh.

"You're very good at saying things that sound sensible."

"I'm not always sensible."

"Really?"

"I once spent twenty minutes looking for my university card while it was in my hand."

Isabelle laughed properly this time.

The tension between them loosened.

For the first time that morning, they returned to their prospectuses without thinking about the hospital.

At Aurelius Medical, the laboratory director was having a less comfortable conversation.

Dr. Helen Morris sat opposite two representatives from the independent verification committee.

A sealed report lay between them.

"The biological analysis is complete," she explained. "The results are internally consistent. We have no concerns regarding the sample integrity or chain of custody."

"Then why has the report not been released?"

"Because the historical identity verification remains incomplete."

One of the representatives opened the accompanying documentation.

"The samples were collected from the individuals themselves, correct?"

"Yes. Both young women provided consent. Reference samples were obtained separately from the two parental couples."

"And the genetic comparisons?"

"Completed."

"Does the discrepancy affect the biological findings?"

"No."

Dr. Morris spoke carefully.

"The discrepancy concerns the identity references attached to the historical maternity records. One of the original registration numbers does not correspond with the revised hospital registration system. We cannot attach the final report to a historical identity record until the discrepancy is formally documented."

"Could you release the genetic findings independently?"

"Technically, yes. However, the requesting parties specifically asked for a jointly verified report identifying the tested individuals, the reference samples and the relevant historical records. We will not issue a document that could later be challenged because of an unresolved identity discrepancy."

The representative nodded.

"How much longer?"

"If we receive the original hospital ledger and the independent identity confirmation, we can complete the verification promptly."

"And if we don't?"

"Then the biological findings remain valid, but the historical report cannot be certified in the requested form."

The representative closed the file.

"Continue the verification."

Dr. Morris nodded.

The laboratory meeting ended.

But the director remained seated for a moment, looking at the sealed report.

There were two sets of genetic comparisons.

Two young women.

Two families.

And one question that eighteen years of altered paperwork had failed to answer.

At the Aurelius Estate, Julian had a different concern.

He was trying to reorganise his list.

The original list had contained twenty-seven suggestions.

It now contained thirty-four.

Lucien looked over his shoulder.

"Why are there three different kinds of headphones?"

"Because I don't know what she likes."

"Then ask her."

"I haven't met her yet."

"That is generally how introductions work."

Julian ignored him.

He crossed out one item and wrote another.

Victoria entered the room carrying a small tray.

"What are you doing?"

"Preparing."

"For what?"

"For when my sister comes home."

Victoria placed the tray on the table.

She looked at the list.

"How many things are on there now?"

"Thirty-four."

"Yesterday there were twenty-seven."

"I remembered more."

Lucien leaned back against the sofa.

"He has included a gaming chair, a reading chair, a tablet, a bookshelf, a camera and something called a mechanical keyboard."

Victoria raised an eyebrow.

"Does she play games?"

"I don't know."

"Does she take photographs?"

"I don't know."

"Does she like mechanical keyboards?"

Julian looked offended.

"That's why I'm making the list. So she can choose."

Victoria smiled.

It was an innocent answer.

And perhaps the best one.

She sat beside him.

"Then remember something else."

"What?"

"She may not want all of these things."

Julian considered that.

"Then I'll ask."

"Good."

He looked down at the paper.

"Do you think she'll like us?"

Victoria's smile softened.

"I hope so."

"What if she doesn't?"

"Then we give her time."

Julian frowned.

"How much time?"

"As much as she needs."

He was quiet for a moment.

Then he asked, "What if she already has a family?"

Victoria's fingers rested against the edge of the tray.

The question was not unkind.

It was simply one she had been avoiding.

"She may have people who raised her," she said gently. "People she loves. People who love her."

Julian looked confused.

"But she's our sister."

"She can be both."

He considered this with the seriousness of someone trying to understand a difficult school lesson.

Then he nodded.

"Okay."

Victoria touched his shoulder.

"Don't make plans for her entire life before you meet her."

Julian looked at his list.

"I'll reduce it to thirty."

Lucien laughed.

Victoria shook her head.

For a moment, the room felt almost normal.

Almost.

Edward spent the afternoon reviewing the recovered file.

The original report contained more than the placement page.

There were references to telephone calls, instructions and a series of handwritten annotations.

Most were brief.

Some were incomplete.

One had been partially cut away when the file was rebound.

He read it twice.

Then he called Jonathan Pierce.

"Mr. Pierce, I need you to look at something."

"What have you found?"

"A reference to an external instruction dated three days after the births."

"From whom?"

"That's the problem. The name is missing."

Edward sent him a photograph.

There was a short line beneath the date:

*Instruction received from the originating authority. Previous arrangement suspended pending confirmation.*

Pierce called back less than five minutes later.

"Where did you find this?"

"Inside the original maternity identity report."

"Was there anything beneath it?"

"Only the edge of another page. It appears to have been removed."

Pierce was silent.

Then he said, "Edward, I want you to understand something."

"What?"

"An instruction to suspend an arrangement is not the same as an instruction to cancel it."

Edward looked again at the sentence.

"You're suggesting the original arrangement may have remained active."

"I'm suggesting we don't know what happened to it."

"Who was the originating authority?"

"I don't know."

"Could it have been Alexander Aurelius?"

"It could have been. It could also have been someone acting under his authority."

Edward leaned back.

"Martin Hale said Charles Vale believed the wrong child had been delivered to the wrong family."

"Yes."

"But the records don't show who arranged the transfer."

"Correct."

"And someone suspended the arrangement three days later."

"Apparently."

Edward stared at the file.

"Then what happened during those three days?"

Pierce did not answer.

Because neither of them knew.

That evening, Richard called a family meeting.

Not a formal one.

He simply asked everyone to gather in the sitting room after dinner.

Elena arrived with a notebook.

Nicholas looked at it.

"Are you bringing study materials to a family meeting?"

"I have work to finish."

"Your dedication is frightening."

"Your lack of dedication is concerning."

Isabelle entered behind them, followed by Catherine.

Sebastian and Edward were already seated.

Richard waited until everyone had settled.

"The investigation has recovered another original document," he began.

Elena closed her notebook.

"Does it explain what happened?"

"Partially."

Edward took over.

"The document records two provisional placements. One under the Valmont name and one under the Aurelius name."

Isabelle looked at him.

"Which one is mine?"

Edward shook his head.

"We don't know."

"Then what does it say?"

He explained the two entries.

Elena listened carefully.

"So the paper says I was provisionally placed with the Valmont family, and Isabelle was provisionally placed with the Aurelius family."

"Yes."

"But that doesn't mean those were our biological families."

"Correct."

Isabelle looked down.

"Then it doesn't really answer anything."

"It tells us that someone recorded those placements," Edward said. "And that they were not considered final."

Richard added, "The independent genetic report is still being verified."

Elena looked towards Catherine.

"How long?"

"We don't have a confirmed date yet."

There was a brief silence.

Then Elena asked the question that had been sitting in her mind since the previous evening.

"Does the Aurelius family know about us?"

Richard exchanged a glance with Edward.

"They know about the investigation."

"Do they know our names?"

"Yes."

Elena absorbed that.

Isabelle shifted in her chair.

"Have they asked to meet us?"

Edward answered carefully.

"Not yet."

"Why not?"

"Because they don't want to approach either of you before the facts are established."

Elena nodded.

It was a reasonable answer.

Still, it felt strange to know that somewhere in the world, another family had learned her name.

A family whose history might be connected to hers.

A family she had never met.

Richard looked around the room.

"Until we receive verified findings, nothing changes here. Both of you continue your education, your routines and your lives."

Nicholas leaned towards Elena.

"Especially your examination."

She glanced at him.

"Thank you."

"I have been instructed to protect your academic future."

"By whom?"

"Myself."

Sebastian sighed.

"Can we finish the meeting?"

Nicholas smiled.

"Of course."

The atmosphere eased slightly.

But Elena noticed something.

Edward had been looking at the documents throughout the meeting.

He seemed troubled by something that had not been mentioned.

After everyone began leaving, she remained near the doorway.

"Edward?"

He looked up.

"Yes?"

"Is there something else?"

He hesitated.

For a moment, she thought he might tell her.

Then he closed the folder.

"Nothing that has been verified."

Elena studied him.

"That's not the same as nothing."

"No," he admitted. "It isn't."

She nodded.

"Then tell me when you know."

"I will."

Elena left.

Edward remained in the sitting room.

He looked down at the recovered page again.

The provisional placements were not the only thing that troubled him.

There was a second annotation in the lower margin, almost hidden by the binding.

It had not been included in the first examination.

The writing was faint, but still legible.

*If the original placement cannot be completed, the child must remain where she is until the second instruction is received.*

Edward read it once.

Then again.

There was no name.

No date.

No indication of which child the instruction referred to.

But beneath the sentence was a short reference code.

**EFO-SL/07.**

The same code that had appeared in the Aurelius Family Office archive.

Edward reached for his phone.

He called Adrian.

"I found another reference to the external liaison."

Adrian answered immediately.

"Where?"

"On the original hospital report. The instruction says the child must remain where she is until a second instruction is received."

"Which child?"

"That's not specified."

Adrian was silent.

Then he asked, "Was there a second instruction?"

Edward looked at the torn edge of the page.

"I don't know."

He lowered his voice.

"But I think someone removed it."

And somewhere in the eighteen-year-old history of the case, there was still an instruction that neither family had ever seen.
