# **Chapter 225 — The Identity Behind the Sealed Record**

Dr. Priya Nair arrived at Meridian at 9:05 a.m.

She carried no briefcase, only a document case containing her appointment letter and independence declaration.

Edward met her outside the restricted examination room.

“Have you reviewed the scope?”

“Yes. I will examine the relationship between the protected personal identity record and the original St Catherine's clinical source. I will not determine biological parentage or disclose a living person's identity beyond the authority granted.”

“Do you understand that the records may contain conflicting descriptions?”

“I expect them to.”

“And if they do?”

“I will record the conflict. I will not resolve it by choosing whichever description appears more plausible.”

Edward opened the examination room.

“Then we can begin.”

The two records were brought in separately.

The first was the original St Catherine's clinical source, preserved under HIST-27/06-A.

The second was the protected personal identity record, held under Meridian's restricted archive arrangement.

Dr. Nair checked the seals.

She compared the archive references with the custody registers.

She examined the original pages, the attached schedules, and the recorded amendments.

The first document contained clinical information.

The second contained personal identity information.

They had different custodians.

They had different preservation histories.

And they had never previously been examined together under an approved identity-review appointment.

Dr. Nair began with the clinical source.

She checked the original entry.

The time was 2:17 a.m.

The infant was recorded as female.

A temporary neonatal reference appeared in the margin.

She then examined the personal identity record.

Its identifying details were partly concealed by the restricted-access protocol.

But the record contained a historical reference to the original clinical source.

Dr. Nair stopped.

She checked the reference against the source register.

Then she checked the amendment history.

A second reference appeared.

It had been added later.

She requested the original supporting schedule.

The custodian brought it into the room.

Dr. Nair compared the entries.

For several minutes, she made no comment.

Finally, she called Edward.

“The records have a documented historical relationship.”

Edward looked at her.

“What kind of relationship?”

“The personal identity record cites the original St Catherine's clinical source as one of its originating records. The reference is present in the original schedule, not merely in a later administrative summary.”

“Does that establish that both records concern the same infant?”

“Not yet.”

She showed him the relevant entries.

“The source reference is genuine. The personal identity record was created using information derived from this clinical source. However, the original schedule also contains a second receiving reference that was entered later.”

“STC-A?”

“Yes.”

“Was it treated as confirmed?”

“In a later amendment, yes. But the original schedule identifies it as provisional.”

Edward examined the page.

The original wording was unmistakable.

**Receiving reference: proposed. Identity reconciliation outstanding.**

Dr. Nair continued.

“The personal identity record is therefore connected to the clinical source through an original documentary reference. But the receiving identity was not established by that connection.”

Eleanor, observing from the adjoining room, looked at Edward.

“That's a significant difference.”

“It is,” he replied.

The source records were connected.

The living person's identity remained protected.

And the question of which infant the later receiving reference represented remained unresolved.

The examination continued into the afternoon.

Dr. Nair found another document attached to the personal identity record.

It was a historical identity declaration.

The declaration had been prepared after the original clinical event.

It contained a personal name, a date of registration, and a receiving-family reference.

The name was sealed.

The date of registration was visible.

The receiving-family reference was partially redacted.

Dr. Nair examined the declaration's execution details.

The signature was original.

The witness had signed in the proper place.

But the witness's authority was limited to confirming that the declaration had been executed in their presence.

The witness had not certified the biological truth of the declaration.

Dr. Nair entered that limitation into her notes.

Edward asked, “Does the declaration identify the living subject?”

“It identifies the person whose personal identity record is being preserved.”

“Can you tell us who that is?”

“Not under the current disclosure authority.”

“Can you tell us whether the declaration identifies a receiving family?”

“It contains a receiving-family reference. I cannot confirm that the family accepted the person or that the reference accurately describes a biological relationship.”

Eleanor asked, “Was the declaration altered?”

Dr. Nair examined the amendment history.

“The original declaration is intact. But a later administrative extract changes the description of the receiving-family reference.”

“How?”

“The original says ‘proposed receiving family.’ The extract says ‘confirmed family association.’”

“Who made the change?”

“The extract was prepared under a shared administrative account. The authoring individual is not identified.”

Edward looked at the document.

Another administrative description had become broader than its source.

But this time, the original personal identity record itself remained intact.

The alteration existed in a later extract.

That mattered.

At 4:30 p.m., Dr. Nair completed her preliminary findings.

She did not provide the sealed name.

She did not identify the protected person.

Her report established four points:

1. The protected personal identity record contained an original reference to the St Catherine's clinical source.
2. The original reference was consistent with the preserved source register.
3. The receiving-family reference remained provisional in the original schedule.
4. A later administrative extract described the receiving-family association as confirmed without a corresponding original identity determination.

The report concluded:

**The historical records are demonstrably connected through an original documentary reference. This examination does not establish that the protected person is the infant described in every associated receiving record, nor does it establish biological parentage or current family identity.**

Edward read the conclusion.

“Can we now identify the person?”

“Not through this report,” Dr. Nair replied.

“Can the authority disclose the name?”

“That is a separate decision. The custodian must establish the legal basis and the necessity of disclosure.”

“Would you recommend disclosure?”

“I would recommend that the competent authority review whether a limited identity disclosure is necessary to permit a voluntary biological comparison. I would not recommend general disclosure to the investigation team.”

Edward nodded.

That was enough for now.

The disclosure request went to the RPRA.

Helen Ward convened a restricted review the next morning.

Edward and Eleanor attended.

Dr. Nair joined remotely to explain the findings.

Helen began with the central limitation.

“The examination has established a documentary connection between the protected personal identity record and the St Catherine's clinical source. It has not established that the protected person is Elena Aurelia, Elena Valmont, or any other named individual.”

Edward nodded.

“Understood.”

“The protected name cannot be released merely because the investigation would find it useful.”

“Then what would permit disclosure?”

“A competent authority must establish that disclosure is necessary for a specific lawful purpose, that the recipient is authorised, and that the protected person's rights are preserved.”

Eleanor asked, “Could the identity be disclosed directly to an independent examiner instead of to us?”

Helen considered the proposal.

“That may be possible.”

Dr. Nair spoke.

“If the purpose is to determine whether a living individual is the same person described in the historical record, the examiner could receive the identity under restricted conditions. The investigation team would receive only the information necessary to establish the next lawful step.”

Edward looked at Helen.

“Could that next step be a voluntary biological comparison?”

“Yes, if the relevant participants are independently identified and consent is obtained.”

“Would the protected person have to know why?”

“Absolutely.”

“Would the family authority be permitted to consent on their behalf?”

“No. The family authority's historical appointment does not confer that power.”

Helen paused.

“The living participant must decide for themselves.”

Edward agreed.

The review authorised a limited disclosure route.

The protected identity would be disclosed only to a designated independent examiner.

The examiner would verify whether the person described in the protected record could be independently matched to a living individual through lawful identity records.

No biological sample could be collected without separate consent.

No family member could compel participation.

And no result could be disclosed to the investigation team beyond the scope of the authorisation.

The approval was recorded.

**Restricted Identity Verification Route — RIV/225.**

For the first time, the investigation had a lawful route toward identifying the person behind the sealed record.

It still did not have that person's name.

At the Valmont Foundation, Elena received the draft process-review agreement.

She read it carefully.

The document described her as an external technical adviser.

It also included a sentence stating that she would provide ongoing implementation oversight.

She circled it.

Then she called Isabelle.

“Did you see this?”

“I've just opened it.”

“They've included ongoing implementation oversight.”

“That wasn't discussed.”

“Exactly.”

Isabelle read the clause again.

“I'll ask them to remove it.”

“Please don't simply replace it with different wording. I want the agreement to say that my work ends when the review and recommendations are delivered unless we agree to something else separately.”

“I understand.”

Elena looked at the draft.

“And I don't want access to confidential applicant records unless the review genuinely requires it and the foundation has a lawful basis to share them.”

“I'll add that to the discussion.”

Elena paused.

“Thank you.”

Isabelle sounded more serious now.

“You're right to insist on it.”

The call ended.

Elena returned to her laptop.

She had learned that an unclear permission could become an obligation surprisingly quickly.

She intended to avoid that mistake in her own work.

At 7:15 p.m., Edward received an urgent message from Helen Ward.

The independent examiner had completed the restricted identity verification.

The protected identity could be matched to a living person through original civil registration records.

The match had been verified through two independent sources.

The name was still sealed from Edward.

But the report contained one important statement.

**The living person identified through the protected personal identity record is currently known under a different personal name from the original historical declaration.**

Edward read the sentence.

Eleanor leaned forward.

“Does that mean the person changed their name?”

“Possibly. The report doesn't say why.”

“Could it be Elena?”

“We still don't know.”

The report included a request for further authority.

The examiner had identified a potential route to the living person's current identity.

But before any approach could be made, the person had to be informed through an appropriate channel.

They would need to understand the purpose of the contact.

They would need to decide whether they wanted to learn more.

And they would need to be free to refuse.

Edward closed the report.

The next step would not be another records request.

It would be a carefully authorised approach to a living person.

He looked at Eleanor.

“We have to be certain that the person is contacted as an individual, not treated as a missing family member before the evidence is complete.”

Eleanor nodded.

“And if they don't want to participate?”

“Then we respect that.”

Edward placed the RIV/225 approval beside the independent examiner's report.

The sealed identity had finally been connected to a living person.

But the investigation still had not established a biological relationship.

And it had not yet earned the right to ask that person for a sample.

In Singapore, Elena was preparing for bed when an unfamiliar email appeared.

The sender was a legal office.

The subject line read:

**Request for Voluntary Participation in a Historical Identity Review.**

Elena frowned.

She opened the message.

It contained no family name.

No claim about her birth.

No accusation.

Only a brief explanation that an independent records review had identified a possible connection between a historical clinical record and a living person's registration history.

The sender stated that no conclusion had been reached.

Participation was entirely voluntary.

The email invited her to confirm whether she was willing to receive a confidential explanation from an independent legal representative.

Elena read it again.

Then she closed the email.

She did not reply immediately.

She opened her contacts and called Edward.

He answered on the fourth ring.

“Hello?”

“Edward, I've received something strange.”

“What is it?”

“A legal office wants me to participate in a historical identity review.”

There was a pause.

“What does it say?”

Elena read the subject line aloud.

Edward went very still.

He had not expected the first contact to reach her before the formal disclosure review was completed.

But the request had come through the authorised channel.

He chose his words carefully.

“Don't agree to anything until you understand exactly what they're asking.”

“I wasn't planning to.”

“Good.”

“Do you know what this is about?”

Edward looked at the sealed identity report on his desk.

“I know an independent review is taking place. I don't yet have enough evidence to tell you what it means.”

Elena was quiet.

Then she asked the question he had been dreading.

“Is this about my birth?”

Edward did not lie.

“It may be.”

She took a slow breath.

“Then I want the facts before I decide anything.”

“That's your right.”

Elena looked at the email again.

For years, people had made decisions about her identity without asking her.

This time, she would not let anyone move ahead of her.

“Send me the independent examiner's details,” she said. “I'll decide whether I want to speak to them.”

Edward nodded, although she could not see him.

“I will.”

The call ended.

On Edward's desk, the sealed identity report remained unopened to the investigation team.

On Elena's screen, the invitation remained unanswered.

For the first time, the investigation had reached the person it might concern.

But the next decision belonged to her.
