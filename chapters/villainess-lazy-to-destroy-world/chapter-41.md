# Chapter 41 — The Inventory That Had Two Owners

The Central Stores Division responded with an inventory.

Unfortunately, it was the wrong inventory.

Seraphina discovered this after reading the first page.

The document described equipment transferred from the Western Regional Stores Annex twenty years earlier. It included regulating assemblies, calibration instruments, metal fittings and several boxes of technical components.

It did not identify individual items.

Instead, everything had been grouped under a single category:

*Regional Magical Infrastructure Equipment — Group Seven.*

There were forty-three entries.

The first thirty-seven had descriptions.

The remaining six were listed as miscellaneous.

No individual equipment numbers were provided.

Seraphina placed the document beside the N-4 receiving record.

"Can this establish that the casing was transferred?"

Holt shook his head.

"It establishes that the category was transferred."

"Not the individual equipment."

"Correct."

"Then why did they send it?"

"Because the request asked for the Group Seven inventory."

Seraphina looked at him.

"Did the request not specify the individual casings?"

"It did."

"Then they sent the wrong inventory."

Holt looked uncomfortable.

"Apparently."

Seraphina returned to the document.

"At least they answered."

It was not the answer she wanted, but it was an answer.

She made a note.

**Group Seven transfer confirmed. Individual equipment identity unconfirmed.**

Then she added:

**Request item-level inventory and receiving records.**

Theodore was less patient.

He read the inventory during the next committee meeting.

"How can an equipment division transfer forty-three entries without recording the individual items?"

Mr Bellamy adjusted his spectacles.

"The entries may have been recorded in an accompanying schedule."

"Which has not been found."

"That is correct."

"Then what exactly did the Central Stores Division receive?"

Bellamy looked at the inventory.

"Forty-three grouped entries."

Theodore stared at him.

"That is not an answer."

"It is the answer supported by the surviving document."

Seraphina intervened.

"The annex may have transferred the items as a collection. The detailed schedule could have remained with the receiving storekeeper."

"Then we need the receiving storekeeper."

"Or the storekeeper's register."

Holt opened his notebook.

"The original receiving clerk was Robert Fenwick."

Seraphina looked up.

"Do we know whether he is still employed?"

"He retired fifteen years ago. He lives in the capital."

"Then contact him."

Theodore nodded.

"Do so."

Robert Fenwick arrived three days later.

He was a thin man with carefully combed white hair and a walking stick. His clothes were neat, his shoes polished, and his expression suggested that he had expected a much shorter meeting.

"I understand this concerns an old stores transfer."

"It does," Seraphina said.

"Group Seven?"

"Yes."

Fenwick sighed.

"I wondered when someone would ask about that."

Holt exchanged a glance with Seraphina.

"You remember the transfer?"

"I remember the reorganization. The department was being consolidated, the regional annex was closing, and we received a large quantity of equipment."

"Was there a detailed inventory?"

"Yes."

Seraphina leaned forward.

"Where was it kept?"

"In the receiving office."

"Do you remember the register?"

"Of course. I entered the items myself."

"Was it a separate document from the summary we have?"

"Certainly. The summary was for the department. The receiving register was for the stores."

"Do you know what happened to it?"

Fenwick tapped his walking stick against the floor.

"It should have been transferred to the central archive."

"Should have been?"

He looked at her.

"My lady, I worked in stores administration for thirty-two years. I learned never to confuse what should have happened with what did happen."

Seraphina nodded.

"That is sensible."

Fenwick continued.

"The receiving office was moved twice during the reorganization. The registers were packed separately from the equipment schedules. I remember because the archive staff refused to accept the boxes until the labels had been corrected."

"Why?"

"Someone had written the old regional department name on the new archive labels."

"Who corrected them?"

"I did."

"Do you remember the destination?"

"Central Records Repository. Basement Annex."

Holt wrote it down.

Seraphina asked, "Do you remember whether the casing from N-4 was among the equipment?"

Fenwick thought.

"I remember regulating casings. I cannot say which station they came from."

"Do you remember how many?"

"Several."

"Were any damaged?"

"Some were marked for inspection."

Seraphina exchanged a glance with Lucien.

"Was there a separate inspection register?"

Fenwick nodded.

"There should have been."

The Central Records Repository was an old stone building behind the Treasury complex.

It had originally been constructed as a municipal storehouse before being converted into an administrative archive.

Its corridors were narrow, its windows small and its staircases steep.

The basement annex was colder than the upper floors.

A clerk named Edwin Clarke met the committee at the entrance.

He looked at their request.

"Group Seven?"

"Yes," Holt replied.

Clarke checked the register.

"We have the summary transfer schedule."

"We have that already," Seraphina said.

"Then you are looking for the receiving register."

"And the inspection register."

Clarke turned several pages.

"Those may be in separate collections."

"Can you identify them?"

He studied the entries.

"The receiving register was catalogued under Central Stores, 20 years ago. The inspection register was classified under technical equipment review."

Seraphina nodded.

"Then we would like both."

Clarke looked toward the basement corridor.

"That may take some time."

Seraphina had learned not to ask how much.

The receiving register was found first.

It was a large volume bound in dark green leather.

The pages were thick, and the writing had been made in several different hands.

Fenwick's name appeared on the first page.

He had signed the opening certification.

The individual entries began on page twelve.

There were descriptions, quantities, condition notes and receiving signatures.

Seraphina searched for the regulating casings.

There were four.

Two were marked as serviceable.

One was marked for inspection.

One was marked as damaged.

She compared the descriptions with the N-4 record.

The casing number matched.

**RC-17-044.**

The N-4 receiving record had recorded the same number.

Seraphina looked at Lucien.

"We have a match."

He examined the entry.

"Yes. This is the N-4 casing."

The condition column read:

*Stored pending technical inspection.*

The next column contained a destination.

**Central Stores — Bay 12.**

Seraphina read it again.

The casing had not remained at N-4.

It had been transferred to the Central Stores Division during the reorganization.

The entry had been signed by Robert Fenwick.

She looked at him.

"You remember this?"

Fenwick examined the page.

"I remember receiving it. I cannot remember the individual number, but this is my handwriting."

"Was it inspected?"

"That would have been recorded separately."

Clarke found the second register.

The inspection register was smaller.

It contained a list of equipment sent for technical examination.

The entries were organized by date, not by station.

Seraphina searched for RC-17-044.

It appeared on page twenty-seven.

The inspection had been scheduled.

The assigned technical examiner was a specialist from the Royal Academy.

The result column was blank.

There was no date of completion.

No certificate.

No return-to-stores entry.

Lucien examined the page.

"This is the first time the equipment's inspection status has been documented."

"Can we find the examiner?" Seraphina asked.

Clarke checked the personnel reference.

"Master Oren Whitlock."

Seraphina stopped.

Oren Whitlock.

The retired engineer who had originally supervised N-4.

Lucien looked at her.

"That is the same Oren."

"Yes."

The clerk examined the register.

"Would you like the corresponding technical file?"

"Yes," Seraphina said.

The technical file was stored in another box.

It contained a request for inspection, a brief technical description and a handwritten note.

The note was dated three days after the equipment arrived.

*Component unsuitable for immediate installation. Internal alignment requires correction before use. Retain in stores pending repair authorization.*

It bore Oren Whitlock's signature.

Lucien read it carefully.

"The casing was not ready for use."

"Could it have been repaired?" Seraphina asked.

"Yes. But we need the technical specifications to determine what correction was required."

"Was a repair authorization issued?"

The clerk checked the file.

"No."

"Was the casing returned to the supplier?"

"No record."

"Was it transferred elsewhere?"

"Not in this file."

Seraphina looked at the note.

The casing had arrived at N-4.

It had been stored.

It had been transferred to Central Stores.

It had been inspected.

It had been found unsuitable for immediate installation.

And then it had remained in the records without a recorded repair or further movement.

The casing had not been stolen.

It had not disappeared during the original transfer.

It had been received, inspected and stored.

Its later history was still unknown.

Lucien turned to the technical report.

"Oren was right to reject it."

Seraphina looked at him.

"Why?"

"The internal alignment was outside the permitted tolerance. Installing it without correction could have caused unstable mana distribution."

"Was it dangerous?"

"Not while stored. But it could not safely be used."

"Could it have been repaired?"

"Almost certainly. The question is whether the repair would have been economical."

Seraphina nodded.

"Then the committee should not assume that it was suitable for W-2."

Lucien smiled.

"You are becoming cautious."

"I have discovered that certainty is expensive."

Theodore arrived after receiving Holt's message.

He examined the receiving register and the inspection report.

"So the N-4 casing was transferred to Central Stores and rejected for immediate use."

"Correct," Seraphina said.

"That explains why it could not be used at W-2."

"Not entirely. We still do not know whether W-2 could have used it after repair. The fittings may not have been compatible."

Theodore looked at Lucien.

"Can you establish that?"

"With the original specifications, yes."

"And the other casing?"

"We have not yet identified it."

Theodore examined the register.

"At least one item has a complete movement history."

"Up to the inspection."

"And afterward?"

"Unknown."

Theodore sighed.

"Of course."

There was another discovery in the receiving register.

A second regulating casing had been received under the same equipment category.

Its number was **RC-17-051**.

It was marked as serviceable.

Unlike the N-4 casing, it had not been sent for inspection.

Its destination was listed as Central Stores — Bay 12.

The entry was dated two days after the first casing arrived.

Seraphina compared the equipment number with the original western reserve transfer.

It matched the second spare casing.

The one whose destination had previously been unknown.

She turned the page.

The receiving signature belonged to Robert Fenwick.

But the condition column contained a note.

*Retained under restricted allocation pending authorization.*

Seraphina looked at Fenwick.

"Do you remember why this one was restricted?"

He studied the entry.

"Not specifically."

"Was restricted allocation common?"

"Yes. Some components were reserved for approved projects. They could not be issued without authorization."

"Was there a project reference?"

Fenwick examined the column.

There was a small reference number.

**RA-6/19.**

Holt copied it.

"Do you recognize that?"

Fenwick shook his head.

"No. It may have been a reserve allocation number."

Seraphina looked at the entry.

The casing had been serviceable.

It had arrived at Central Stores.

It had been placed under restricted allocation.

It had not been issued in the surviving register.

She wrote down the reference.

The committee returned to the capital the following morning.

The new findings were added to the W-2 file.

The N-4 casing's history was now clearer.

It had been transferred under an emergency procedure.

It had reached Central Stores during the reorganization.

It had been inspected by Oren Whitlock.

It had failed the immediate-use inspection.

It had remained in stores pending repair authorization.

The second casing had also reached Central Stores.

It was marked serviceable.

It had been retained under restricted allocation.

Its later movement was unknown.

Theodore examined the revised summary.

"That is progress."

"Yes," Seraphina replied.

"Does it explain why W-2 was not commissioned?"

"Partially. The N-4 casing was not suitable for immediate installation. The second casing may have been suitable, but we do not know whether its fittings matched W-2."

"And the restricted allocation?"

"We need to identify RA-6/19."

Theodore turned to Holt.

"Find the allocation reference."

Holt nodded.

That afternoon, Seraphina received a short message from the Royal Academy.

Lucien had completed the first comparison of the technical specifications.

The N-4 casing was incompatible with W-2 without substantial modification.

The second casing, RC-17-051, was compatible.

Seraphina read the message twice.

The serviceable casing could have been used at W-2.

But it had been restricted.

She returned to the committee folder.

The entry had not been marked damaged.

It had not been marked unsuitable.

It had been deliberately held under a restricted allocation.

Someone had reserved it for another purpose.

Seraphina wrote to Holt.

*Please identify the project associated with allocation reference RA-6/19. Obtain the authorization, intended destination, and any subsequent issue or release record.*

She paused.

Then she added:

*Do not assume the restriction was improper. Establish which project had priority and who authorized the allocation.*

That evening, Seraphina sat with her mother in the drawing room.

Evelyne was reviewing invitations.

Seraphina was reviewing the Central Stores register.

"You look tired," Evelyne observed.

"I am."

"Have you eaten?"

"Yes."

"What?"

Seraphina considered.

"Biscuits."

Evelyne put down her papers.

"That is not dinner."

"It was several biscuits."

"That does not improve the argument."

Evelyne signalled to a servant to bring something more substantial.

Then she looked at the documents.

"Another missing record?"

"Not exactly. We found the record."

"That sounds promising."

"We found two spare regulating casings. One was unsuitable for immediate use. The other was serviceable but had been reserved for another project."

"Do you know which project?"

"Not yet."

Evelyne nodded.

"Then at least you have a question with a reference number."

Seraphina looked at her mother.

"That is becoming my standard of happiness."

Evelyne smiled.

The next morning, the Allocation Office sent a reply.

Reference RA-6/19 had been identified.

It was not a maintenance project.

It was a reserve allocation created by the Royal Infrastructure Allocation Office for a separate regional facility.

The intended destination was listed as:

**Ashenford Western Relay Station A-2.**

Seraphina stared at the page.

A-2.

The western facility whose physical existence had not yet been confirmed.

The serviceable casing had been reserved for a station that the committee had not even located.

She looked at the date.

The allocation had been approved before the equipment was transferred to Central Stores.

She read the authorization again.

There was a name beneath the approval.

**Arthur Fenwick.**

The same senior expenditure officer who had reviewed the W-2 structural payment.

Seraphina placed the document beside the other records.

The connection was not proof of wrongdoing.

A-2 might have been a legitimate project.

The casing might have been reserved according to a valid priority schedule.

But the committee now had a reason to investigate the sixth facility more carefully.

She looked at the entry.

A-2 had appeared in an old funding summary.

It had been listed in the original six-facility programme.

No one had yet confirmed its location.

And twenty years ago, a serviceable regulating casing had been reserved for it.

Seraphina closed the folder.

For the first time, the missing equipment was no longer merely missing.

It had been waiting for a destination.

The difficulty was that nobody knew whether that destination had ever existed.
