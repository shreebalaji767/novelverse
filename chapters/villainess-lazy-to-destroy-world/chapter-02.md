# Chapter 2 — I Refuse to Die Dramatically

Seraphina Veyr had slept for exactly seven hours and forty-three minutes.

She knew this because the clock beside her bed had been wound the previous evening, and because she had spent the final seventeen minutes before waking in a state of increasingly irritated consciousness.

The problem was not the bed.

The bed was excellent.

It was wide enough to accommodate three people, though Seraphina sincerely hoped no one would ever test that particular feature. The mattress was soft, the blankets warm, and the curtains thick enough to prevent the morning sun from committing an act of violence against her eyes.

The problem was her future.

More specifically, the problem was that her future appeared to involve a public execution.

Seraphina opened one eye.

The ceiling remained expensive.

She closed it again.

In her previous life, she had been an ordinary office employee. Her responsibilities had included answering emails, correcting spreadsheets, attending meetings that could have been messages, and occasionally pretending to understand why a department had spent three weeks arguing over the placement of a comma.

She had never been particularly ambitious.

She had wanted a comfortable apartment, reliable income, enough money to travel occasionally, and the freedom to ignore work notifications after six in the evening.

Instead, she had awakened in the body of a seventeen-year-old aristocratic girl whose fictional biography ended with a sword, a crowd, and a royal decree.

It was an unreasonable career change.

“Lady Seraphina?”

Mira's voice came from beyond the bedroom door.

Seraphina pulled the blanket over her head.

“I'm dead.”

There was a pause.

“Your breakfast is ready, my lady.”

“Then I have been dead very efficiently.”

Another pause.

Mira opened the door.

The maid entered carrying a folded dress over one arm and a small tray containing a cup of warm water. She stopped beside the bed and looked at the blanket-covered figure.

“Would you like me to summon the physician?”

“No.”

“Are you feeling unwell?”

“Only existentially.”

Mira appeared to consider this.

“I will inform the physician that your fever has returned.”

Seraphina immediately sat up.

“No, don't.”

The maid's expression remained perfectly composed.

“Then perhaps you should get dressed.”

Seraphina looked at her.

Mira looked back.

After several seconds, Seraphina accepted the dress.

She had discovered something important during the previous day.

Being a noblewoman did not mean she could simply remain in bed until the world stopped being inconvenient.

It meant that an alarming number of people were employed to ensure she could not.

The morning dress was dark blue, with modest silver embroidery along the sleeves.

Seraphina approved of it.

She also approved of the fact that Mira did not insist on elaborate jewelry.

After dressing, she sat before the mirror while the maid brushed her hair.

The crimson eyes staring back at her were still unfamiliar.

They were beautiful, certainly.

Unfortunately, beauty had not saved the original Seraphina Veyr.

The original character had possessed excellent hair, impeccable posture, a remarkable wardrobe, and a talent for turning every social gathering into a diplomatic incident.

She had also possessed a spectacularly poor survival strategy.

Seraphina stared at her reflection.

“Do you know what I used to think about aristocratic villains?”

Mira carefully separated a section of hair.

“That they were wicked, my lady?”

“That they had too much free time.”

Mira's brush paused.

Seraphina continued.

“People with nothing useful to do eventually begin making problems for themselves.”

“Is that why you have requested the household accounts?”

Seraphina looked at her.

Mira resumed brushing.

The maid had an irritating habit of noticing things.

“I requested them because I was curious.”

“Of course.”

“And because curiosity is harmless.”

“Usually.”

Seraphina narrowed her eyes.

Mira tied the finished hair with a dark ribbon.

“Your father is expecting you in the eastern office after breakfast. The agricultural reports arrived yesterday evening.”

Seraphina's shoulders stiffened.

There it was.

The first obstacle in her carefully planned retreat from destiny.

Paperwork.

### The Problem With Remembering a Novel

The eastern office smelled of ink, polished wood, and the faint bitterness of old tea.

Duke Alaric Veyr stood beside a large window, examining a map of the northern duchy. His dark hair was beginning to show silver at the temples, and his expression suggested that the map had personally disappointed him.

A stack of reports occupied the desk.

Seraphina stopped at the doorway.

“Good morning, Father.”

Alaric turned.

“Good morning. You look better.”

“I feel better.”

“Your mother says you have been reading.”

Seraphina glanced at the reports.

“I have been trying to become more educated.”

“An admirable ambition.”

The duke gestured toward a chair.

“Sit. We need not discuss the reports in detail. I only want you to understand what concerns the estate.”

Seraphina sat.

A servant poured tea and withdrew.

Alaric placed one document before her.

“Three northern villages have reported difficulties with grain storage. The winter harvest was smaller than expected, and the roads are in poor condition.”

Seraphina looked at the page.

The village names meant nothing to her.

The numbers meant slightly more.

She studied the columns.

“Is this the first year?”

“No. The reports have been inconsistent for several years.”

“Then why are they being presented as a new problem?”

Alaric's gaze sharpened.

“They are not. The previous reports were included in the appendix.”

Seraphina turned the page.

The appendix was a collection of handwritten summaries, each using a different method of recording grain quantities.

She felt a familiar irritation rising.

In her previous life, she had encountered this exact sort of problem.

Not grain.

Not villages.

But the same underlying disaster: people using different systems, nobody agreeing on definitions, and a report that looked authoritative because it had enough pages.

“Who compiled this?” she asked.

“Roland Ashford.”

“The accountant?”

“The estate's senior financial officer.”

Seraphina nodded.

She did not want to criticize a man she had not met.

More importantly, she did not want to become the sort of person who criticized documents without understanding the work behind them.

“Could I see the original village reports?”

Alaric studied her for a moment.

“Certainly.”

He rang a small bell.

A secretary appeared and received the instruction.

While they waited, Seraphina examined the map.

The northern villages were connected by three main roads. One followed the river. Another crossed a low ridge. The third passed through a forested stretch that appeared to be considerably longer than the others.

“Why do they use the forest road?” she asked.

“Because the river road becomes impassable during heavy rain.”

“And the ridge?”

“Steep. Difficult for loaded carts.”

Seraphina looked at the map again.

She was not a military strategist.

She was not a magical engineer.

She was not a genius capable of solving an entire kingdom's problems by looking at a map for five minutes.

But she knew that transportation affected prices.

And prices affected whether people could afford food.

And if food became expensive in a region where harvests were already poor, then the problem was not simply agricultural.

It was logistical.

She leaned back.

“This sounds complicated.”

Alaric gave a quiet laugh.

“It is.”

“Can someone else handle it?”

The duke's laughter stopped.

Seraphina looked at him with genuine hope.

“I mean, I am certain the estate has competent people.”

“It does.”

“Excellent.”

Alaric folded his arms.

“And you are the heir.”

Seraphina's hope died.

The original villainess had been involved in the kingdom's political affairs because she wanted influence.

Seraphina was being involved because her father had discovered that she could read.

It was a much less dramatic reason.

It was also considerably harder to escape.

The original reports arrived before the second cup of tea.

They were tied together with a blue ribbon and carried by a young clerk who looked relieved to have found the correct office.

Seraphina untied them.

The first report concerned the village of Bellmere.

The second concerned Northwick.

The third concerned a settlement whose name she had to read twice before she could pronounce it.

Each report contained figures for harvest yield, storage capacity, and estimated winter consumption.

The numbers did not agree.

Not dramatically.

Not in a way that suggested someone had stolen half the kingdom's grain.

They disagreed in the small, irritating ways that made it impossible to know what was actually happening.

One village reported storage capacity in barrels.

Another used weight.

A third recorded the amount of grain delivered to the warehouse rather than the amount that remained after distribution.

Seraphina placed the papers side by side.

“Father?”

“Yes?”

“Do we know how much grain is physically present in these villages?”

Alaric looked at the reports.

“According to the latest estimates.”

“That isn't what I asked.”

The duke's expression changed.

He looked again.

Then he called for the secretary.

Seraphina immediately regretted speaking.

She had not intended to cause an investigation.

She had intended to ask a question.

There was a difference.

A very important difference.

“Perhaps,” she said carefully, “the figures are simply recorded differently.”

“That is possible.”

“Then we should not assume anything is missing.”

Alaric nodded.

“Agreed.”

The secretary returned with a note from the estate's agricultural supervisor.

The supervisor had been asked to confirm the storage figures.

He had not yet visited the villages.

The reports had been compiled from local records.

Seraphina felt a small, unpleasant sensation in her stomach.

This was not a mystery.

It was not a conspiracy.

It was not a hidden plot involving powerful nobles and forbidden magic.

It was simply a system that had been allowed to become unreliable.

And unreliable systems had consequences.

“Could we ask the villages to use the same measurement?” she suggested.

Alaric considered it.

“That would require a standard form.”

“Then perhaps we should make one.”

The duke looked at his daughter.

Seraphina looked at the reports.

Neither of them spoke for a moment.

Then Alaric said, “I will ask Roland to examine the matter.”

Seraphina nodded.

That was acceptable.

Roland could examine it.

Someone competent could examine it.

She did not need to become involved.

She did not need to become famous for agricultural reform.

She did not need a newspaper to describe her as the mysterious young duchess who had discovered the hidden truth behind the northern grain crisis.

She needed only to survive.

### A Future Best Avoided

After the meeting, Seraphina returned to her bedroom.

She dismissed the attendants and sat at her writing desk.

The desk was made of dark walnut. Its drawers contained neatly arranged stationery, sealing wax, and several notebooks.

She opened a fresh one.

At the top of the page, she wrote:

**Things I Remember About the Original Novel**

Then she stopped.

The problem was that she remembered very little with certainty.

She remembered scenes.

She remembered dramatic speeches.

She remembered the saintess standing beneath a stained-glass window while the crown prince offered her his hand.

She remembered Seraphina Veyr's sharp smile.

She remembered a banquet where the villainess humiliated Liora Everwyn in front of the royal court.

She remembered a magical experiment that went wrong.

She remembered the final execution.

But the details were scattered.

She could not remember the exact sequence of events.

She could not remember whether the magical catastrophe happened because of Seraphina's actions or because she had merely been blamed for it.

She could not remember the names of every noble involved.

And she certainly could not remember the financial arrangements of a kingdom she had read about years ago.

The original novel had been entertaining.

It had not been a textbook.

Seraphina dipped her pen into the ink.

**1. The saintess becomes important to the royal court.**

**2. The crown prince supports her.**

**3. The original Seraphina becomes jealous and antagonistic.**

**4. A political conflict develops around magical research.**

**5. Something catastrophic happens.**

She stared at the list.

It was not useful.

It was a sequence of vague warnings.

She added another line.

**6. I do not know what causes the catastrophe.**

The pen hovered.

Then she wrote:

**7. I do not know whether the original novel was accurate.**

That was the most troubling part.

The story had presented events from a limited perspective.

The villainess had been a villainess because the narrative had called her one.

But now Seraphina was living inside the world.

She had a father who worried about her health.

A mother who had probably spent the previous day quietly asking the physician whether the fever had affected her memory.

A maid who knew her habits well enough to recognize that something had changed.

An estate full of people whose lives depended on decisions made in rooms like the eastern office.

They were not background characters.

They were people.

And if the original novel had simplified them, then it had probably simplified everything else.

Seraphina set down the pen.

“Wonderful,” she murmured.

She had no reliable guide.

Only an incomplete memory of a story that might have been wrong.

A knock came at the door.

Mira entered with a small tray.

“Your mother would like to see you.”

Seraphina looked up.

“Did I do something?”

“No, my lady.”

“Is she angry?”

“Not that I know of.”

“Then why?”

Mira placed the tray on the desk.

“Her Grace wishes to discuss your social calendar.”

Seraphina went still.

The social calendar.

The aristocratic equivalent of a battlefield.

She looked at the notebook.

Then at Mira.

“Can I refuse?”

“You may.”

“Will refusing create consequences?”

Mira considered the question.

“Almost certainly.”

Seraphina closed the notebook.

She had been born into a noble family.

She had inherited a title.

She had a future execution waiting somewhere in the distance.

And now she had a social calendar.

For the first time since waking in this world, she seriously considered whether the original Seraphina's greatest mistake had been political ambition.

Perhaps it had simply been attending too many events.

The Duchess's sitting room overlooked the southern garden.

Evelyne Veyr sat beside a window, reviewing a letter. She wore a pale green gown and had arranged several invitations on a small table.

Seraphina entered and bowed.

“Mother.”

Evelyne looked up.

“Come here, darling.”

Seraphina sat beside her.

The duchess set the letter aside.

“I have been told that you asked your father about the agricultural reports.”

“Yes.”

“Was that interesting?”

“Not particularly.”

Evelyne smiled.

“Then why did you ask?”

Seraphina searched for an answer that would not sound suspicious.

“Because I thought the records were confusing.”

“That is a reasonable explanation.”

The duchess picked up one of the invitations.

“The capital will hold its spring reception in three weeks. Several families have requested your attendance.”

Seraphina's heart sank.

“Three weeks?”

“Yes.”

“Is it necessary?”

Evelyne studied her.

“Not strictly.”

Seraphina relaxed.

“However,” the duchess continued, “your absence would be noticed.”

The relaxation vanished.

“Why?”

“Because you are the only daughter of the Duke of Veyr.”

Seraphina looked at the invitation.

The name of the host was written in elegant gold lettering.

She did not recognize it.

That was not surprising.

She did not recognize most of the aristocratic families.

And that was dangerous.

In the original novel, the villainess had used social gatherings to cultivate alliances, spread rumors, and undermine the saintess.

Seraphina had no intention of doing any of those things.

But she also could not afford to behave carelessly.

A refusal could be interpreted as an insult.

An attendance could expose her to people she remembered only as names in a story.

She looked at her mother.

“Would it be possible to attend briefly?”

Evelyne's expression softened.

“Of course.”

“Without participating in anything complicated?”

“That depends on what you consider complicated.”

“Conversations about marriage.”

The duchess blinked.

Then she laughed.

“Your father would be delighted to hear that you are thinking so far ahead.”

“I am not thinking ahead. I am trying to prevent it.”

Evelyne's laughter became quieter.

She studied Seraphina with the careful attention of a woman who had spent years navigating aristocratic society.

“You have changed since your illness.”

Seraphina's fingers tightened around the invitation.

“I know.”

“Are you frightened by something?”

The question was gentle.

It was also difficult to answer.

Seraphina could not tell her mother that she had read a novel in another life.

She could not explain that the world around them had once existed as a story in which her daughter was destined to become a monster.

She could not explain that she was trying to avoid a future she did not fully understand.

So she chose the truth that was closest to the surface.

“I think I have been careless.”

Evelyne said nothing.

Seraphina continued.

“I used to assume that everyone would forgive me because I was a Veyr.”

The duchess's expression became serious.

“Did someone make you realize otherwise?”

“No.”

Seraphina looked down at the invitation.

“I simply thought about it.”

Evelyne placed a hand over hers.

“Then perhaps your illness has given you something useful.”

Seraphina stared at their joined hands.

She had not expected that.

The original villainess's mother had appeared only briefly in the novel.

She had been described as elegant, politically astute, and emotionally distant.

But the woman sitting beside Seraphina was not distant.

She was attentive.

She was careful.

And she seemed to understand that something had changed without demanding an explanation.

“I will attend,” Seraphina said.

Evelyne smiled.

“Good.”

“Briefly.”

“We shall see.”

Seraphina suspected that phrase was dangerous.

That evening, she returned to her notebook.

The first page contained her rules.

The second contained her uncertain memories.

On the third page, she wrote a new heading.

**Things I Must Not Assume**

1. The original novel told the whole truth.
2. Every person described as an enemy was actually an enemy.
3. Every event will happen in the same order.
4. My knowledge is enough to prevent disaster.
5. Avoiding the plot means avoiding responsibility.
6. A quiet life is guaranteed simply because I want one.

She paused.

Then she added:

**7. Being lazy does not mean being careless.**

The sentence annoyed her.

It sounded like something a responsible adult would say.

She preferred not to be responsible.

Unfortunately, the estate's grain reports had already demonstrated that responsibility did not disappear merely because she ignored it.

Seraphina closed the notebook.

Outside, the evening bells rang across the duchy.

Somewhere in the northern villages, people were counting grain.

Somewhere in the capital, invitations were being sent.

Somewhere in the royal academy, a researcher was probably preparing an experiment that would eventually become a problem.

Seraphina had no intention of finding out which one.

She drew the curtains.

Then she climbed into bed.

“I refuse,” she whispered to the ceiling, “to die dramatically.”

The ceiling remained silent.

For once, it was the only thing in the kingdom that did not expect anything from her.
