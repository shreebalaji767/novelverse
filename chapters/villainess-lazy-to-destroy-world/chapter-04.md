# Chapter 4 — Why Is the Household Spending So Much?

Seraphina Veyr had made a terrible discovery.

Her family's estate was expensive.

This was not, by itself, surprising.

The Veyr family owned a large northern duchy, maintained several residences, employed hundreds of people, operated mines, collected agricultural rents, and maintained roads that occasionally disappeared beneath snow.

Expensive was expected.

What Seraphina had not expected was the number of ways in which money could leave a household without anyone appearing to notice.

The discovery began with a breakfast conversation.

It was, as most unfortunate discoveries were, entirely accidental.

“Could you pass the butter?” Seraphina asked.

Mira, standing behind her chair, handed it over.

Across the table, Duke Alaric was reading a letter. Duchess Evelyne was examining a small list of household purchases.

Seraphina glanced at the list.

Then she glanced again.

“Mother?”

“Yes, darling?”

“Is that the household expenditure report?”

Evelyne looked up.

“Part of it. The steward sent the summary this morning.”

“How much does the estate spend on household supplies?”

The duchess gave her a thoughtful look.

“Would you like the annual figure?”

Seraphina hesitated.

“Perhaps the monthly one.”

Evelyne handed her the paper.

Seraphina read it.

Then she read it again.

She was not shocked by the total.

She was shocked by the number of categories.

Food, clothing, cleaning materials, candles, firewood, furniture, repairs, kitchen equipment, stable supplies, guest accommodations, garden maintenance, linen replacement, household wages, and miscellaneous expenses.

There were also smaller categories with names that seemed designed to conceal their contents.

**Special domestic provisions.**

**Occasional household requirements.**

**Additional administrative expenditure.**

Seraphina looked up.

“What does 'occasional household requirements' mean?”

Evelyne took back the paper.

“It covers expenses that do not fit into the regular categories.”

“Such as?”

“Replacement tableware, additional guest bedding, seasonal decorations, and other things.”

“Could those not be recorded separately?”

“They could.”

“Are they?”

The duchess smiled.

“You have been asking many questions lately.”

Seraphina put down her bread.

“I am trying to understand the household.”

“I know.”

Alaric folded his letter.

“Ask the steward for the detailed accounts if you wish. But remember that a large estate has many expenses that cannot be judged by looking at a single number.”

“I understand.”

“Good.”

Seraphina looked back at the paper.

She had a feeling that she did not understand nearly enough.

### **The Cost of Living Like a Duke's Daughter**

The household accounts arrived shortly before midday.

They came in two leather-bound ledgers and a bundle of supporting documents.

Seraphina stared at them.

“Is this everything?”

Mira glanced at the stack.

“For the household, my lady.”

“And the estate?”

“Those records are kept in the financial office.”

Seraphina nodded.

She had not expected otherwise.

The household ledger was already substantial.

She opened the first volume.

The pages contained neatly written figures, dates, descriptions, and signatures.

At first glance, everything appeared orderly.

The problem emerged when she began comparing entries.

The same kind of cleaning supplies appeared under three different headings.

One was recorded as kitchen materials.

Another was listed under general household provisions.

A third appeared in the maintenance section.

Seraphina frowned.

She checked the dates.

The entries were not necessarily duplicates.

Different departments could have purchased similar items independently.

But the records made it difficult to tell whether the purchases were separate.

She made a note.

**Clarify category definitions.**

On the next page, she found several furniture purchases.

A set of chairs had been ordered for the western reception room.

Another set had been purchased for the same room two months later.

A third entry concerned repairs to existing chairs.

Seraphina examined the supporting documents.

The first order had been signed.

The second had a receipt.

The third had a repair estimate but no final confirmation.

She did not know whether the chairs had actually been delivered.

She wrote another note.

**Confirm delivery and current inventory.**

Then she found a large payment for seasonal decorations.

The amount was not unreasonable for a residence of this size.

But the description was vague.

There was no detailed list of what had been purchased.

Seraphina rubbed her forehead.

She was beginning to understand why her previous life had involved so many spreadsheets.

A number without an explanation was not particularly useful.

She turned another page.

There was a payment for the replacement of several windows.

The work had been completed.

The contractor's name was listed.

The materials were itemized.

The amount matched the invoice.

Seraphina nodded.

That was what a proper entry looked like.

She continued reading.

A few pages later, she found another window repair.

Same contractor.

Same building.

Different dates.

The first repair had been recorded as a completed expense.

The second was listed as an emergency maintenance allocation.

She compared the descriptions.

One concerned the northern gallery.

The other concerned the servants' corridor.

They were different areas.

She relaxed.

Not every repeated word indicated a problem.

That was worth remembering.

She wrote:

**Do not assume repetition means duplication. Check location and work description.**

Then she closed the ledger.

For the first time, she understood how easily someone could make a mistake when examining accounts without context.

And how dangerous it would be to accuse someone before understanding the records.

### **A Conversation With the Steward**

The household steward arrived after lunch.

His name was Mr. Thomas Wren.

He was a broad-shouldered man in his late fifties, with a neatly trimmed grey beard and a habit of holding his hands behind his back.

He had served House Veyr for more than twenty years.

“Lady Seraphina.”

“Mr. Wren. Thank you for coming.”

The steward bowed.

“You requested an explanation of the household accounts.”

“Yes. Please sit.”

He hesitated.

Seraphina gestured toward the chair again.

Mr. Wren sat.

She placed the ledger between them.

“I have been reviewing the expenditure records. I noticed several categories that I do not fully understand.”

“Of course, my lady.”

She pointed to the first entry.

“Cleaning materials appear under several headings. Is there a reason?”

“The kitchen purchases its own supplies, my lady. The laundry has a separate allocation. The general household stores maintain cleaning materials for corridors, guest rooms, and public areas.”

“So they are separate purchases?”

“Yes.”

“Are they stored separately?”

“Not always. Some materials are distributed from the central storeroom.”

Seraphina nodded.

That explained the entries.

It did not explain why the same items were being recorded under different categories, but it was a starting point.

She pointed to the furniture orders.

“Could you explain these?”

Mr. Wren leaned forward.

“The first order was for the western reception room. The second was for the adjoining gallery.”

“The entry says reception room.”

The steward examined the page.

He frowned.

“That appears to be a clerical error.”

“Was the second set delivered?”

“Yes, my lady. I believe so.”

“You believe so?”

“I would need to check the inventory.”

Seraphina nodded.

“Please do.”

She moved to the next entry.

“Several household purchases have been described as occasional requirements. Could those be listed more specifically?”

Mr. Wren considered the question.

“It would require some additional work.”

“I understand.”

“The category was created because the household sometimes needs items that are not part of the regular supply schedule.”

“Could we maintain a separate list of those items?”

“We could.”

“Would that be difficult?”

“Not particularly.”

Seraphina looked at him.

He did not seem offended.

That was encouraging.

She turned to the final page she had marked.

“This maintenance allocation is the one I am least familiar with.”

Mr. Wren read the entry.

His expression changed slightly.

“Ah.”

Seraphina waited.

“This was arranged through the estate's property office. The household steward's department only recorded the payment.”

“Do you know what work was performed?”

“Not precisely.”

“Could you find out?”

“Certainly.”

Seraphina closed the ledger.

“Thank you. I am not suggesting that anything improper has happened. I simply want the records to be easier to understand.”

Mr. Wren bowed.

“I appreciate that, my lady.”

He gathered his notes.

Before leaving, he paused.

“If I may say something?”

“Of course.”

“Your questions are sensible. But some of these systems have been in place for many years.”

Seraphina nodded.

“I assumed they had.”

“Changing them will take time.”

“I am not asking you to change everything today.”

Mr. Wren looked relieved.

“Then I shall begin with the inventory and the category descriptions.”

“Thank you.”

After he left, Seraphina leaned back.

She had survived a conversation about accounting.

More importantly, she had not accidentally accused a loyal servant of stealing furniture.

It was a successful afternoon.

### **The Missing Chairs**

The following morning, Mr. Wren returned with the inventory.

He carried a folder and looked considerably less cheerful than he had the previous day.

Seraphina was reading in the library when Mira informed her of his arrival.

“Should I ask him to wait?”

“No. Please show him in.”

Mr. Wren entered and bowed.

“My lady, I have completed the inventory check.”

Seraphina set down her book.

“And?”

“The second furniture order was delivered. However, the first order included twelve chairs, while the second included eight.”

Seraphina looked at the ledger.

“Then there were twenty chairs?”

“Yes.”

“Were all twenty in the western reception room?”

“No. Eight were moved to the smaller gallery after the first delivery.”

“Why?”

“The reception room had already been furnished sufficiently.”

Seraphina nodded.

That was reasonable.

“What about the repairs?”

“Four of the original chairs required repairs. The repair work was completed.”

“Then the records are correct?”

“Mostly.”

Mr. Wren opened the folder.

“The delivery record for the second order was filed under the general furniture inventory rather than the reception room inventory. The repair receipt was also filed separately.”

Seraphina examined the documents.

The information existed.

It was simply scattered.

“Could the inventory be organized by room?”

“It could.”

“And could the repair records be attached to the relevant inventory entries?”

“Yes.”

“Would that be practical?”

Mr. Wren thought for a moment.

“It would make future checks easier.”

Seraphina nodded.

“Then perhaps we should try it.”

The steward bowed.

“I shall arrange it.”

He then placed another paper before her.

“This concerns the maintenance allocation you asked about.”

Seraphina looked down.

The payment had been made to a contractor responsible for several properties.

The work included repairs to a drainage channel, reinforcement of a storage building, and replacement of damaged roof supports.

The documents included a work order, a contractor's invoice, and confirmation from the property office.

Seraphina read them carefully.

The work was legitimate.

The description in the household ledger had simply been too vague.

She exhaled.

“Thank you.”

Mr. Wren looked at her.

“Is there a problem?”

“No. The supporting records explain the payment.”

“Then everything is in order?”

“As far as these documents show.”

She returned the folder.

“Please keep the documents together in the future.”

“Of course, my lady.”

Mr. Wren left.

Seraphina looked at the ledger again.

She had expected to find some great scandal.

Instead, she had found a filing problem.

She was not disappointed.

Filing problems were survivable.

### **An Unwelcome Visitor**

The household accounts might have remained a quiet administrative exercise if not for the arrival of a visitor that afternoon.

The visitor was Lady Amelia Rosenthal.

Seraphina recognized the name from one of the invitations.

She was a senior aristocrat who maintained a residence in the capital and frequently visited the northern duchy.

Her family had longstanding ties with House Veyr.

She arrived with two attendants and a small carriage full of gifts.

The duchess received her in the southern drawing room.

Seraphina was summoned shortly afterward.

She entered, bowed, and offered a polite greeting.

“Lady Rosenthal.”

“Lady Seraphina.”

Amelia was a woman in her late fifties, with silver-blonde hair and an expression that seemed permanently amused.

She looked Seraphina over.

“I heard you were unwell.”

“I have recovered, thank you.”

“And I hear you have been studying the estate accounts.”

Seraphina glanced at her mother.

Evelyne's expression was neutral.

“Yes,” Seraphina replied.

“How industrious.”

“I am trying to understand the household.”

Amelia smiled.

“An admirable pursuit. Though I hope you are not finding it too dull.”

“I find it quite informative.”

“Do you?”

The older woman took a sip of tea.

“I remember when your mother first began managing her own household. She was much the same. Always asking questions, always examining every expense.”

Evelyne smiled faintly.

“And you always told me I was wasting my time.”

“I told you that some matters were better left to experienced people.”

Seraphina listened.

There was no obvious hostility in their voices.

But there was history.

Amelia turned back to her.

“Do you intend to continue?”

“With the accounts?”

“With administration.”

Seraphina considered the question.

“I do not know yet.”

“Your father must be pleased. A daughter who takes an interest in the estate can be very useful.”

Seraphina nearly choked on her tea.

Useful.

There was that word again.

Amelia noticed.

“Is something wrong?”

“No, my lady.”

“Perhaps I have said something amusing.”

“Not at all.”

Amelia leaned back.

“Still, you must be careful. A young lady can become so involved in household matters that she forgets the importance of society.”

Seraphina looked at her.

“Can one not do both?”

“Certainly. But society has its own rules.”

“I am learning them.”

“Good.”

Amelia smiled.

“Then I shall expect to see you at the spring reception.”

Seraphina smiled politely.

“I have received the invitation.”

“Excellent. It will be a useful opportunity for you to meet several families.”

Seraphina had a sudden suspicion that the reception would be much more complicated than she had hoped.

### **The Duchess's Advice**

After Lady Rosenthal departed, Evelyne asked Seraphina to remain.

The duchess waited until the door closed.

“Lady Rosenthal is an old friend.”

Seraphina nodded.

“I noticed.”

“She has a habit of giving advice even when nobody requests it.”

“That seems common among aristocrats.”

Evelyne laughed.

“Perhaps.”

Seraphina looked at the empty tea cup.

“Was she concerned about my involvement in the household?”

“Not particularly. She was testing your response.”

“Why?”

“Because people are curious when someone changes.”

Seraphina felt a small tension in her shoulders.

“Do they think I am behaving strangely?”

“Some do.”

“Have they said anything?”

“Nothing serious.”

Evelyne reached for the invitation tray.

“You must remember that people often interpret unfamiliar behavior according to their own expectations.”

Seraphina thought about the previous days.

Her questions about the grain reports.

Her interest in household accounts.

Her refusal to attend several social gatherings.

Her sudden desire to understand how the estate worked.

None of it had been part of the original Seraphina's reputation.

People would notice.

They would invent explanations.

And she could not control all of them.

“Should I stop asking questions?”

“No.”

Evelyne's answer was immediate.

“Then what should I do?”

“Ask them carefully. Listen to the answers. And understand that people may disagree with you.”

Seraphina nodded.

The duchess continued.

“You are allowed to learn, darling. But you are not required to prove yourself to every person who notices.”

Seraphina looked at her mother.

That was useful advice.

She wished she had heard it sooner.

### **A Small Change**

The next day, Seraphina returned to the household office.

Mr. Wren had prepared a revised inventory sheet.

The new format listed each room separately.

Furniture.

Linen.

Lighting.

Decorative objects.

Maintenance history.

The document was easier to read.

It was also shorter.

Seraphina examined it.

“This is much clearer.”

“I am glad you approve, my lady.”

“Will the other departments use the same format?”

“Not immediately. We shall begin with the household and review the results.”

Seraphina nodded.

That was sensible.

A small trial was better than forcing a new system onto everyone at once.

She signed the approval.

Mr. Wren gathered the papers.

Then he hesitated.

“There is one other matter.”

Seraphina looked up.

“Yes?”

“The household has received several requests from the servants' quarters regarding winter clothing allowances.”

“Are they insufficient?”

“Some employees believe the current allowance does not reflect the rising cost of materials.”

Seraphina considered this.

“Has the matter been reviewed?”

“Not recently.”

“Then it should be.”

Mr. Wren nodded.

“I shall prepare the relevant figures.”

“Please include the number of employees affected and the current allowance.”

“Certainly.”

He left.

Seraphina looked at the new inventory sheet.

She had intended to spend the afternoon reading.

Instead, she had helped improve a household record, confirmed a maintenance payment, and requested a review of employee allowances.

It was not a great achievement.

No one would write a song about it.

No noble family would tremble at the news.

No royal minister would summon her to the capital.

And that was precisely how she wanted it.

She closed the folder.

“Small changes,” she murmured.

Mira, who had been standing beside the desk, tilted her head.

“My lady?”

“Nothing.”

Seraphina rose.

“I am finished for today.”

Mira looked at the remaining documents.

“There are still several letters.”

Seraphina stopped.

She looked at the letters.

Then at the door.

Then back at the letters.

“Tomorrow.”

“Very well.”

She walked out before anyone could find another responsibility for her.

That evening, the duke received a brief report from Mr. Wren.

The household inventory had been reorganized.

Several ambiguous categories were being clarified.

The maintenance documents had been properly filed.

The servants' clothing allowances were under review.

Alaric read the report twice.

Then he placed it on his desk.

His daughter had not demanded authority.

She had not criticized the household staff.

She had not attempted to impose sweeping changes.

She had asked questions, listened to explanations, and suggested small improvements.

It was a sensible beginning.

He glanced at the second report waiting on his desk.

The northern agricultural supervisor had requested permission to visit three villages and verify their grain records.

Alaric approved it.

Then he leaned back.

For years, Seraphina had shown little interest in the administrative responsibilities of House Veyr.

Now she appeared to be approaching them with unusual seriousness.

He wondered what had changed.

Across the estate, Seraphina was already asleep.

She had no idea that her father was beginning to take her questions seriously.

She also had no idea that the next set of household accounts would contain a discrepancy that could not be explained by misplaced furniture, inconsistent labels, or missing paperwork.

For now, she had achieved her goal.

She had spent an entire day working without causing a scandal.

It was a victory.

A modest one.

But Seraphina had decided that modest victories were considerably preferable to dramatic deaths.
