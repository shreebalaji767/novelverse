# Chapter 16 — The Saintess Arrives Two Days Early

The first problem with the spring reception was that it had become an event.

Seraphina had originally understood it to be a gathering of aristocrats, a few formal greetings, an unreasonable quantity of food, and several hours of pretending to enjoy conversations about things nobody particularly cared about.

She had accepted the invitation on that basis.

Unfortunately, the household had other ideas.

By the morning before the reception, the eastern wing had been transformed.

Fresh flowers stood in the entrance hall. The reception rooms had been polished. The musicians had arrived for rehearsals. The kitchens were preparing enough food to feed a small military company.

And Margaret Ellison, head housekeeper, had developed the expression of a woman who would personally challenge anyone who moved a chair without permission.

Seraphina stood in the entrance hall, watching two servants carry a decorative arrangement past her.

“Why are there so many flowers?”

Margaret, standing beside the staircase with a list in hand, did not look up.

“Because it is a spring reception, my lady.”

“I understand the seasonal theme.”

“Then I believe the flowers are doing their job.”

Seraphina looked at the arrangement.

It contained white lilies, pale roses, small blue flowers, and several branches of something green that appeared to have been selected because it had not objected to being cut.

“Are those expensive?”

“Not particularly.”

“Are they difficult to maintain?”

“They are cut flowers.”

“That wasn't my question.”

Margaret finally looked at her.

“The arrangements will be replaced once during the reception. They are supplied by a local florist under the household's seasonal contract.”

Seraphina nodded.

“Fine. I was only asking.”

“Of course, my lady.”

Margaret returned to her list.

Seraphina watched her for a moment.

The head housekeeper had become very good at identifying when Seraphina was about to suggest something unnecessary.

It was an ability Seraphina respected.

“Is everything ready?”

“Almost.”

“What remains?”

“The final seating arrangement, the musicians' payment confirmation, the kitchen delivery, and the guest rooms for the Royal Academy delegation.”

Seraphina looked toward the staircase.

“Guest rooms?”

“Yes.”

“I thought the delegation was arriving tomorrow.”

“They are.”

“Then why are we preparing guest rooms today?”

Margaret gave her a patient look.

“Because they will need somewhere to sleep when they arrive.”

Seraphina considered this.

“An excellent point.”

Margaret resumed writing.

Seraphina decided that the best contribution she could make to the preparations was to leave.

At breakfast, Duchess Evelyne was reviewing the guest list.

She had changed into a pale blue morning dress and had placed three sheets of paper beside her plate.

Duke Alaric was reading correspondence.

Seraphina entered, sat down, and immediately noticed the additional place setting.

“Who is joining us?”

Evelyne looked up.

“Lady Amelia Rosenthal.”

“Today?”

“She requested a private visit before the reception.”

Seraphina picked up her tea.

“Is she bringing advice?”

“Almost certainly.”

“Can we decline?”

“No.”

Seraphina sighed.

Evelyne smiled.

“You will survive.”

“That is what people keep saying.”

Alaric lowered his letter.

“The Royal Academy delegation has also changed its arrival schedule.”

Seraphina stopped drinking.

“What?”

“They will arrive this afternoon rather than tomorrow.”

“Why?”

“Their carriage route was adjusted because of a road inspection near the western bridge.”

Seraphina stared at him.

“Are they coming here because of the road?”

“No. They are coming here because they are attending the reception. The road adjustment simply changed their arrival time.”

“That is a relief.”

Evelyne examined the guest list.

“Saintess Liora Everwyn will accompany them.”

Seraphina slowly lowered her cup.

“Today?”

“Yes.”

“Not tomorrow?”

“Today.”

“Why?”

“The delegation is travelling together.”

Seraphina looked down at her breakfast.

She had been prepared to meet Liora at the reception.

She had even rehearsed a greeting.

It had been a perfectly ordinary greeting.

*It is a pleasure to meet you, Lady Everwyn.*

Nothing excessive.

Nothing insulting.

Nothing that could be interpreted as a challenge.

Now she would have to meet her before the reception, without the distraction of a crowded room.

This was unacceptable.

“Father.”

Alaric looked up.

“Yes?”

“Could I be ill?”

“No.”

“Could I have an urgent appointment?”

“You have none.”

“Could I be somewhere else?”

“You are here.”

Seraphina looked at Evelyne.

“Mother?”

Evelyne folded the guest list.

“You will greet our guests politely. You will not treat the saintess as an enemy. You will not interrogate her about the Church. And you will not spend the afternoon attempting to avoid her.”

Seraphina stared at her.

“I wasn't going to interrogate anyone.”

“Good.”

“I was going to avoid her.”

“I know.”

“Then why did you say that?”

“Because I know you.”

Alaric returned to his correspondence.

Seraphina took another sip of tea.

Her peaceful life had been under attack for several weeks.

Today, the attack had acquired a name.

Liora Everwyn.

The Royal Academy delegation arrived shortly after noon.

Seraphina stood beside her parents in the main reception hall.

She wore a dark green dress with a modest silver pattern along the sleeves. Her hair had been arranged neatly, though Mira had spent several minutes persuading her not to remove the decorative hairpins.

“Your Grace,” announced the butler.

The doors opened.

The first to enter was a tall man in his late forties, dressed in the dark blue formal robes of the Royal Academy.

Professor Edmund Vale.

Seraphina recognized his name from the guest list and from a brief mention in the novel.

She did not remember much about him.

The novel had focused far more on the royal court and the saintess than on the people who maintained the Academy's institutions.

Professor Vale bowed.

“Your Grace. Duchess Veyr. Lady Seraphina. Thank you for receiving us.”

Duke Alaric greeted him.

“We are pleased to welcome the Academy's representatives.”

A second man followed.

He was younger, perhaps in his mid-twenties, with slightly disordered brown hair and a travelling coat that had clearly seen better days.

Lucien Arkwright.

The royal magician.

Seraphina recognized him immediately.

He was an important character in the original novel.

A brilliant researcher.

A man whose work had been connected to the magical catastrophe.

And, according to the story, someone who had trusted the original Seraphina Veyr.

Seraphina felt her stomach tighten.

Lucien bowed.

“Your Grace.”

“Master Arkwright,” Alaric replied.

Lucien's gaze moved briefly toward Seraphina.

“My lady.”

“Master Arkwright.”

He looked tired.

Not merely tired from travel.

He had the expression of someone who had recently discovered that the world contained more problems than he had originally believed.

Behind him came two other Academy representatives, followed by a young woman in a simple white-and-gold travelling dress.

Liora Everwyn.

The saintess.

She was not surrounded by a magical glow.

There were no dramatic bells.

No heavenly light entered the hall.

She looked like a young woman who had travelled for several hours and would probably appreciate a comfortable chair.

Her light brown hair was tied back. Her expression was calm, though Seraphina noticed the faint shadows beneath her eyes.

Liora stepped forward and bowed.

“Your Grace. Duchess Veyr. Lady Seraphina. Thank you for welcoming us.”

Her voice was gentle but steady.

Duchess Evelyne greeted her warmly.

“Lady Everwyn. We are delighted you could join us.”

Liora smiled.

“It is an honour to visit House Veyr.”

Seraphina bowed.

“It is a pleasure to meet you, Lady Everwyn.”

Liora returned the greeting.

“And you, Lady Seraphina.”

That was it.

No hostility.

No accusation.

No dramatic recognition of an enemy.

The saintess was simply standing in front of her.

Seraphina felt strangely disappointed.

She had spent so much time worrying about the encounter that she had expected something more difficult.

Perhaps that was unfair.

She reminded herself that the original novel had not described Liora's entire personality.

It had described her role in a story.

Those were not necessarily the same thing.

The delegation was shown to their rooms.

The arrangements were simple. Professor Vale and the senior Academy officials were given rooms in the eastern guest wing. Lucien was placed in a room overlooking the gardens.

Liora was given a room near the Duchess's private sitting room.

Seraphina watched the servants carry their luggage upstairs.

Liora had brought only two travel cases.

One was small.

The other appeared to contain books.

Seraphina noticed that Mira was looking at the luggage.

“What?” Seraphina whispered.

“Nothing, my lady.”

“You are staring.”

“I was wondering whether the saintess travels with fewer belongings than expected.”

“Perhaps she is practical.”

“Perhaps.”

Mira watched as one of the porters carried the smaller case upstairs.

“Her luggage is lighter than yours.”

Seraphina looked at her.

“That is not a fair comparison.”

“You have four cases for a three-day visit.”

“I need options.”

“You brought three dresses.”

“Exactly.”

“And six pairs of shoes.”

Seraphina frowned.

“Different shoes serve different purposes.”

Mira gave her a look.

Seraphina decided to end the conversation.

The first private conversation with Liora took place in the west garden.

It was not planned.

Seraphina had gone there to escape the preparations.

Liora had apparently gone there for the same reason.

They encountered each other near the stone fountain.

Liora was standing beside a flowerbed, looking at a small wooden sign placed in the soil.

Seraphina stopped.

“Lady Everwyn.”

Liora turned.

“Lady Seraphina.”

“Are you enjoying the garden?”

“It is beautiful.”

Seraphina looked at the flowerbed.

The sign read:

**Newly planted. Please do not step on the soil.**

“That is a recent addition.”

“I noticed.”

“The gardeners had trouble with visitors walking across the flowerbeds.”

Liora looked at the sign again.

“Was it because people did not notice the flowers?”

“Mostly because they did not notice the path.”

Liora smiled.

“That sounds familiar.”

Seraphina gestured toward the stone bench.

“Would you like to sit?”

“Thank you.”

They sat with a polite distance between them.

For several moments, neither spoke.

Seraphina was grateful.

She had not prepared a conversation.

She had prepared a greeting.

There was a difference.

Liora looked toward the garden entrance.

“Your mother is very kind.”

“She is also very difficult to persuade when she has already made a decision.”

“Is that so?”

“Especially when the decision concerns my schedule.”

Liora laughed softly.

Seraphina glanced at her.

It was an ordinary laugh.

Not a saintly one.

Not a magical one.

Just a laugh.

“I understand,” Liora said. “The Church has a similar relationship with my schedule.”

Seraphina looked at her.

“Do they?”

Liora nodded.

“There are charitable visits, healing consultations, ceremonies, meetings with local officials, and requests from people who have heard that I might be able to help.”

“Can you?”

“Sometimes.”

The answer was immediate.

Seraphina appreciated that.

Liora folded her hands.

“Healing magic can help with certain injuries and illnesses, but it cannot replace physicians, medicine, surgery, clean water, or proper care. People sometimes expect it to do more than it can.”

“That must be difficult.”

“It is.”

Liora looked down at her hands.

“I used to think that if I worked harder, I could help more people. But there are only so many hours in a day.”

Seraphina considered this.

“You cannot solve a shortage of doctors by working longer.”

Liora looked at her.

“No.”

“Nor can you solve a shortage of medicine by healing everyone individually.”

“Exactly.”

There was a brief silence.

Then Liora smiled.

“I am glad you understand.”

Seraphina did not know what to say.

She had expected the saintess to be kind.

The original novel had described her as kind, patient, and selfless.

But the novel had rarely allowed her to be tired.

It had rarely shown her limits.

It had rarely shown her doing anything other than helping someone.

Seraphina realized that she had been thinking about Liora as a character in a story again.

She looked away.

“Does the Church have enough physicians?”

“Not everywhere. Some provinces have trained healers, but the smaller villages often depend on travelling practitioners.”

“Are there records of where they are needed most?”

Liora nodded.

“There are regional reports, though they are not always current.”

“Why not?”

“Some villages are difficult to reach. Others have no permanent official to submit reports. And sometimes the reports are written by people who do not know what information the Church needs.”

Seraphina leaned back.

“That sounds familiar.”

Liora turned toward her.

“Does House Veyr have similar problems?”

“Unfortunately.”

Seraphina briefly explained the delayed village requests and the difficulty of identifying which storage facilities were still usable.

She did not mention the old emergency investigation in detail.

Liora listened carefully.

When Seraphina finished, Liora said, “It must be frustrating.”

“It is.”

“Especially when the records suggest that something has been handled, but the people affected say otherwise.”

Seraphina looked at her.

“Yes.”

Liora's expression became thoughtful.

“I have seen that with charitable supplies. A report might say that blankets were distributed to a village, but the village may have received them several weeks after the coldest weather.”

“Then the report is technically correct.”

“But not useful enough.”

Seraphina nodded.

That was a distinction she understood.

A record could be accurate and still fail to answer the question being asked.

She had written that in her notebook only recently.

“Do you think the Church could improve its reporting?” Seraphina asked.

“I think it should. But I do not have authority over every local office.”

“Then perhaps the people who manage those offices should be involved in changing the system.”

Liora smiled.

“That is what I have been trying to persuade them to do.”

Seraphina studied her.

“You are not as different from the reports as I expected.”

Liora blinked.

“I'm sorry?”

Seraphina realized her mistake.

“That was not an insult.”

“I did not think it was.”

“I meant—”

She stopped.

There was no good way to explain that she had previously known Liora through a fictional narrative.

“I suppose I had formed an impression without meeting you.”

Liora nodded slowly.

“That happens often.”

Seraphina glanced at her.

“Does it?”

“People hear that I am the saintess and expect me to be something specific. Sometimes they expect me to be endlessly patient. Sometimes they expect me to know the right answer to every problem. Sometimes they expect me to agree with them.”

“And what do you do?”

“I disappoint them.”

Seraphina stared.

Then she laughed.

It was not particularly elegant.

Liora smiled.

“I think that is a very healthy approach,” Seraphina said.

“I am not sure the Church agrees.”

“Then perhaps the Church needs to be disappointed occasionally.”

Liora looked at her for a moment.

Then she laughed again.

The sound carried across the garden.

They were interrupted by Lucien Arkwright.

He approached from the garden path with a folded document in his hand.

“Lady Everwyn. Lady Seraphina.”

Both women turned.

Lucien bowed.

“My apologies for interrupting.”

Seraphina immediately became suspicious.

“What is it?”

Lucien looked at her.

“I wanted to ask whether the western irrigation channel near Westmere belongs to the ducal agricultural administration.”

Seraphina blinked.

“Why?”

“The Academy received a report about irregular mana flow in a nearby boundary conduit.”

She stared at him.

There it was.

Magic.

She had successfully avoided the subject for nearly half a day.

“Is the conduit part of the irrigation system?”

“Not directly. It runs beneath the old road.”

“Does it affect the channel?”

“We do not know yet.”

Seraphina looked at Liora.

Liora appeared equally uncertain.

Lucien unfolded the document.

“The report mentions a fluctuation in the local mana distribution. It is not severe, but the reading is inconsistent with the previous inspection.”

Seraphina took a breath.

“Has anyone checked the conduit?”

“Not yet.”

“Why?”

Lucien hesitated.

“The previous inspection was recorded as satisfactory.”

Seraphina looked at him.

“Was the inspection actually carried out?”

Lucien's expression changed.

“That is what we are trying to establish.”

Seraphina closed her eyes.

She had heard this before.

A record said something had been checked.

Nobody could establish who had checked it.

The report had been filed.

The problem remained.

She opened her eyes.

“Master Arkwright, I am not a magical engineer.”

“I am aware.”

“I cannot tell you whether the conduit is functioning correctly.”

“I am aware of that as well.”

“Then why are you asking me?”

“Because the conduit crosses land managed by House Veyr, and the Academy requires permission to inspect it.”

Seraphina looked at the document.

That was reasonable.

It was also extremely inconvenient.

“Can the inspection wait until after the reception?”

Lucien considered the question.

“It is not an emergency. We can wait a few days.”

“Then please do.”

“Of course.”

Seraphina looked relieved.

Lucien folded the document again.

“There is one other matter.”

She stared at him.

“Of course.”

“The Academy delegation would like to discuss the maintenance of regional magical infrastructure during tomorrow's reception.”

Seraphina felt her relief disappear.

“Why?”

“Because the Academy is reviewing the condition of several older mana conduits across the kingdom. Your recent work on estate maintenance and regional reporting may provide useful administrative insight.”

Seraphina looked at him.

“My recent work involved grain measurements.”

“Among other things.”

“And household inventory.”

“Indeed.”

“And making sure a village's irrigation request was not forgotten.”

Lucien nodded.

“Exactly.”

Seraphina turned toward Liora.

“Do you see what happens when one asks practical questions?”

Liora smiled.

“I believe so.”

Lucien seemed confused.

Seraphina handed the document back.

“I will listen tomorrow. I will not promise to supervise the inspection, fund the project, or design anything.”

“Understood.”

“And I will not become responsible for the Academy's records.”

Lucien looked at her.

“That would be helpful.”

Seraphina narrowed her eyes.

He seemed entirely sincere.

That made the situation worse.

Later that afternoon, Seraphina attended a private tea with Duchess Evelyne, Lady Amelia, and Liora.

It was supposed to be a simple social meeting.

It became an informal discussion about charitable administration.

Lady Amelia was particularly interested in the Saintess's work.

“Surely the Church has made arrangements to prevent you from being overwhelmed,” she said.

Liora smiled politely.

“They are trying.”

“Trying?”

“There are many people who want assistance, Lady Amelia. The Church has limited resources, and not every request can be answered immediately.”

“Then surely the royal treasury should provide more funding.”

Liora considered the question.

“Additional funding would help in some areas, but it would not solve every difficulty.”

Lady Amelia raised an eyebrow.

“Why not?”

“Because some villages have no reliable road access. Others lack trained staff. Some charitable supplies arrive without accurate records of what was needed. A larger budget does not automatically correct those problems.”

Evelyne nodded.

“An important point.”

Lady Amelia looked at Seraphina.

“You seem to agree.”

Seraphina put down her teacup.

“I do.”

“Why?”

“Because giving an office more money without understanding its work can make its existing problems more expensive.”

Lady Amelia smiled.

“That sounds rather like something a future minister would say.”

Seraphina nearly choked on her tea.

“I sincerely hope not.”

Evelyne looked amused.

“Seraphina has no interest in royal administration.”

“Then she is fortunate,” Lady Amelia said. “It has a habit of developing an interest in people.”

Seraphina decided not to answer.

Liora glanced toward her.

“Do you dislike politics?”

“I dislike being asked to solve problems without being given enough information.”

“That must make court meetings difficult.”

“I imagine it does.”

Lady Amelia laughed.

The conversation moved on to the reception arrangements, the spring weather, and the difficulties of transporting fresh produce from the southern provinces.

For the first time that day, Seraphina felt comfortable.

Liora did not dominate the conversation.

She listened, asked questions, and disagreed when necessary.

Lady Amelia treated her with respect but did not flatter her.

Evelyne guided the discussion without making it feel like an interview.

It was an ordinary afternoon.

Seraphina found that she liked ordinary afternoons.

That evening, she returned to her room and found a small note on her desk.

It was from Lucien.

The note contained a brief description of the western boundary conduit and a request for permission to inspect it after the reception.

At the bottom, he had written:

*The inspection will require one Academy engineer, one local administrator, and two maintenance workers familiar with the conduit route.*

Seraphina read it twice.

It was a sensible plan.

It also meant that the Academy had already begun considering the practical requirements.

She placed the note beside her notebook.

Then she wrote:

**New observations:**

1. Lady Liora Everwyn is not an endlessly cheerful saintly figure. She is tired, practical, and aware of the Church's limitations.
2. Master Lucien Arkwright is intelligent but seems to assume that administrative cooperation can be arranged once he has identified a problem.
3. Magical infrastructure requires maintenance. This is apparently true of everything.
4. The Academy is interested in regional records.
5. I should not assume that refusing involvement is the same as refusing to listen.

She paused.

Then she added:

**6. Do not allow a pleasant conversation to become a new responsibility.**

She put down her pen.

The reception was tomorrow.

The Academy would be present.

The saintess would be present.

The crown prince had not yet confirmed whether he would attend.

Seraphina looked at the ceiling.

She had spent weeks trying to avoid the original novel's central characters.

Now she had met the saintess, spoken with a royal magician, and agreed to listen to a discussion about magical infrastructure.

She was not sure whether she was making progress.

Perhaps she was simply discovering that the world contained people outside the story she remembered.

That was an uncomfortable thought.

It meant she could not predict everything.

It also meant that she did not have to treat every person as a danger simply because the novel had given them an important role.

Seraphina closed her notebook.

Tomorrow, she would attend the reception.

She would be polite.

She would eat something.

She would avoid making promises.

And if anyone asked her to save the kingdom, she would politely recommend finding someone more qualified.

That seemed like a reasonable plan.

For once, she hoped it would survive the evening.
