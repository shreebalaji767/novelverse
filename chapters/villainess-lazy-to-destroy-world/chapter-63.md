# Chapter 63 — The Witness Who Had Not Witnessed Anything

The capital was unusually crowded when Seraphina arrived.

It was not a festival day.

There were no royal celebrations, military parades, or public ceremonies.

Nevertheless, the streets around the Royal Agricultural Council were filled with carriages, messengers, clerks, and people who appeared to have discovered that an ordinary administrative meeting was an excellent reason to travel.

Seraphina looked out of the carriage window.

"How many people are attending?"

Cedric checked the invitation.

"Twenty-three official participants."

"And unofficial?"

"Unknown."

"That is an unfortunate number."

Cedric glanced at her.

"Why?"

"Because it means we have more people than chairs, or more chairs than useful questions."

He smiled.

The carriage stopped outside the council building.

A clerk approached to assist them.

Seraphina stepped down and adjusted her gloves.

She had chosen a dark blue dress without elaborate decoration. The occasion did not require a formal court appearance, and she had no desire to spend the day carrying a train through administrative corridors.

Cedric followed with the document case.

They entered through the main doors.

Inside, a reception clerk examined their invitation.

"Lady Seraphina Veyr and Master Cedric Veyr?"

"Yes."

"Please proceed to the eastern conference room. The secretary will meet you there."

The clerk handed them two identification slips.

Seraphina examined hers.

It read:

*Lady Seraphina Veyr — Estate Administration Representative.*

She turned it over.

Nothing.

She looked at Cedric.

"Representative of what?"

Cedric read his own.

*House Veyr — Supporting Estate Administrator.*

He sighed.

"I suspect the labels were prepared before the council clarified the agenda."

Seraphina handed the slip back to the clerk.

"I am attending as a landowner's representative, not as an officer of the council."

The clerk blinked.

"I can amend that."

"Please do."

It took four minutes.

Seraphina considered the delay acceptable.

The eastern conference room contained a long table, several smaller desks, and a large map of the kingdom.

A number of officials had already arrived.

Minister Garrick, the Royal Finance Minister, was seated near the head of the table.

Beside him sat Director Harold Fenwick from the regional agricultural office.

The Royal Infrastructure Department was represented by Adrian Holt.

A woman from the Ministry of Lands was arranging documents into neat piles.

The council secretary, a narrow-faced man named Master Philip Arden, stood near the window.

And at the far end of the room sat Edmund Vale.

Seraphina recognized him from the newspaper articles.

He was older than she had expected, perhaps in his late thirties, with a narrow beard and a habit of watching people without appearing to do so.

He stood when she entered.

"Lady Veyr."

"Mr Vale."

"I am pleased we have the opportunity to meet."

"Likewise."

He smiled.

Seraphina sat beside Cedric.

She placed her document case on the table.

A moment later, Master Arden approached.

"Lady Veyr, the council appreciates your attendance."

"Thank you."

"We have circulated the revised agenda."

"I have read it."

"Excellent. The council hopes to establish a useful basis for further discussion."

Seraphina looked at the papers.

"Then I hope we can begin by distinguishing the existing regional guidance document from the proposed central index."

Master Arden nodded.

"That will be the first item."

She was relieved.

At least someone had read the objections.

The meeting began shortly afterward.

Master Arden explained the purpose.

The council was not seeking to impose a new system immediately.

It wanted to examine whether a central index could help preserve information about shared-property agreements, maintenance responsibilities, and access arrangements.

The proposed system would not automatically alter legal ownership.

It would not replace private agreements.

It would not authorize regional offices to enter private property without existing legal permission.

Those points were clearly stated.

Seraphina listened.

The first presentation concerned lost and incomplete records.

A representative from the Ministry of Lands described several cases in which property agreements had become difficult to locate.

In one case, two estates had spent nearly a year disputing access to a road because the original agreement had been stored in a private solicitor's office that had closed decades earlier.

In another, a river-maintenance obligation had been recorded in one estate's register but not the neighboring estate's.

The representative explained that a central index might allow officials to identify the existence and location of agreements without necessarily holding every original document.

Seraphina made a note.

That was a reasonable approach.

An index did not need to contain the entire document.

It could record the parties, date, property, document location, and known amendments.

The next presentation concerned confidentiality.

The Treasury representative warned that some private agreements included financial terms, compensation arrangements, or obligations that should not be publicly accessible.

A central index would therefore require different levels of access.

The Ministry of Lands agreed.

The council secretary wrote several notes.

For the first twenty minutes, the meeting was productive.

Then Minister Garrick asked a question.

"Would the participating estates be willing to provide copies of their existing records for a pilot programme?"

The room became quiet.

Seraphina looked at the minister.

"May I ask what the pilot programme would test?"

"Whether the records can be standardized and indexed efficiently."

"Would the originals remain with the estates?"

"Yes."

"Who would hold the copies?"

"The council's records office."

"Who would be permitted to access them?"

"That would be determined by the final rules."

"Then the rules should be established before the records are submitted."

Minister Garrick nodded.

"That is reasonable."

The Ministry of Lands representative added that the pilot could begin with a limited set of non-sensitive documents.

Seraphina considered this.

"Would the estates choose which documents to submit?"

"Yes."

"Would they be able to withdraw their participation?"

The council secretary checked his notes.

"That has not yet been addressed."

"Then it should be."

Master Arden wrote it down.

Seraphina continued.

"Would the index distinguish between the original agreement and later amendments?"

"Yes," the Ministry representative said.

"Would it distinguish between an agreement that is still in force and one that has expired or been replaced?"

"That should be possible."

"Would it record uncertainty?"

The representative paused.

"Uncertainty?"

"For example, if an estate has a copy of an agreement but cannot locate the original. Or if a later amendment is mentioned in a register but the amendment itself is missing."

The representative nodded slowly.

"Yes. That would be important."

Seraphina leaned back.

"Then the index may be useful."

Minister Garrick smiled.

"That is encouraging."

"Provided it is properly designed."

The minister's smile became smaller.

Cedric looked down at his notes.

He knew that expression.

Seraphina had not rejected the proposal.

She had simply refused to approve something that did not yet exist in a complete form.

During the short break, Cedric went to speak with Director Fenwick.

Seraphina remained near the window.

Edmund Vale approached.

"Lady Veyr."

"Mr Vale."

"I found your comments very interesting."

"Thank you."

"You seem particularly concerned with the distinction between records and authority."

"I am concerned with the distinction between what a document says and what people assume it says."

Vale smiled.

"An important distinction."

Seraphina waited.

He continued.

"Your work in the north has attracted considerable attention."

"It has attracted newspaper articles."

"Which is another form of attention."

"Not always a useful one."

Vale laughed softly.

"I imagine not."

He glanced toward the conference table.

"Do you believe a central index would be beneficial?"

"Potentially."

"Then you support the proposal."

"I support examining the proposal."

"That sounds like a careful answer."

"It is an accurate one."

Vale nodded.

"Some people in the capital believe House Veyr has become unusually influential in regional administration."

Seraphina looked at him.

"Who?"

"Several members of the aristocracy."

"Which members?"

He hesitated.

"I cannot disclose private conversations."

"Then I cannot evaluate the claim."

Vale smiled again.

"Fair enough."

He shifted his attention toward the documents on the table.

"May I ask how the northern inspection programme began?"

"An old orchard agreement had stopped functioning."

"And that led to the other reviews?"

"Yes."

"Was it your intention to establish a model for other estates?"

"No."

"Yet the regional office has adopted the format."

"The regional office chose to use it."

Vale nodded thoughtfully.

"Perhaps that is precisely why people are interested. You did not seek authority, but your work has influenced others."

Seraphina studied him.

"Mr Vale, may I ask you something?"

"Certainly."

"Did you write the first newspaper article about House Veyr?"

"Yes."

"Did you write the second?"

"Yes."

"And the third?"

"That one was written by another correspondent."

Seraphina nodded.

"Did you provide the Royal Agricultural Council with the memorandum proposing a centralized property-record system?"

Vale's expression changed slightly.

It was not a dramatic change.

His smile remained.

But he took a moment before answering.

"No."

"Do you know who submitted it?"

"No."

"Did you know that your articles were being used as supporting material for the proposal?"

"I knew the council was considering a review of regional administrative practices."

"That was not my question."

Vale looked at her.

"No. I did not know that my articles would be cited in the preliminary report."

Seraphina nodded.

"Thank you."

He seemed slightly disappointed that she had not pressed further.

Instead, she asked:

"Why did you describe House Veyr as developing a new administrative movement?"

Vale considered his answer.

"Because several estates had begun adopting similar practices."

"They had not."

"They had requested copies of the format."

"That is not the same thing."

"No, perhaps not."

"Did any estate transfer its records to House Veyr?"

"No."

"Did any estate grant House Veyr authority over its properties?"

"No."

"Then what was the basis for the phrase?"

Vale looked toward the window.

"It was an interpretation."

Seraphina nodded.

That was the first honest answer he had given her.

"Then it should have been described as an interpretation."

Vale looked back at her.

"Perhaps."

The conversation ended when Master Arden called the participants back to their seats.

Seraphina returned to her chair.

She had learned something.

Not enough to accuse anyone.

But enough to understand that the newspaper articles were not all part of a single coordinated effort.

One writer had exaggerated.

Another had interpreted.

A council secretary had incorporated the articles into a preliminary report.

An unknown correspondent had submitted a memorandum.

The council had begun considering a proposal.

The events were connected.

The intentions were not established.

That distinction mattered.

The afternoon session concerned the proposed pilot programme.

The council secretary distributed a draft outline.

It suggested that three regional offices participate.

Each office would invite a small number of estates to submit selected shared-property agreements.

The central index would record the document's location, parties, date, property, and status.

Original documents would remain with the estates.

The council would not hold copies of financial terms unless necessary for the index.

The participating estates could withdraw.

The pilot would last six months.

Seraphina read the outline.

It was considerably better than the earlier report.

There were still problems.

The proposed index did not clearly distinguish between an agreement's current status and its historical status.

It also lacked a process for correcting inaccurate entries.

Seraphina raised both points.

The Ministry of Lands representative agreed that the draft needed revision.

A Treasury official suggested that the index should include a field for the source of each entry.

Cedric added that every amendment should be linked to the original agreement rather than replacing it.

The council secretary wrote down the suggestions.

Minister Garrick listened.

Then he asked:

"Would House Veyr be willing to participate in the pilot?"

Seraphina looked at the draft.

"Not yet."

The minister raised an eyebrow.

"Why?"

"Because the proposed correction procedure is incomplete, and the index does not yet distinguish between historical and current obligations."

"Those are matters that can be resolved."

"Then resolve them first."

The minister smiled.

"Would you be willing to participate once they are resolved?"

"I would consider it."

"That is not a refusal."

"No."

"It is also not an agreement."

"Correct."

Cedric looked as though he wanted to laugh.

Minister Garrick nodded.

"Very well."

The meeting continued.

By the time it ended, the council had agreed to revise the draft.

The three regional offices would not begin collecting records until the rules were approved.

The pilot would remain voluntary.

The Ministry of Lands would prepare a revised index format.

The Treasury would propose confidentiality classifications.

The council secretary would prepare a procedure for corrections and withdrawals.

No one had been appointed to supervise the project.

Seraphina was particularly pleased about that last point.

After the meeting, Master Arden approached her.

"Lady Veyr, the council appreciates your comments."

"Thank you."

"Would you be willing to review the revised format when it is prepared?"

"Yes, if the request is limited to reviewing the format."

"Of course."

"And if the council requires legal advice, it should consult the appropriate legal office."

"Certainly."

"And I will not be responsible for collecting records from other estates."

Master Arden paused.

"That is understood."

Seraphina nodded.

She was beginning to suspect that people found it useful to agree with her boundaries while leaving them out of the official documents.

She intended to keep stating them anyway.

Outside the conference room, Cedric was waiting.

"How did it go?"

"Better than expected."

"Will House Veyr participate?"

"Not yet."

He nodded.

"That is probably wise."

They walked toward the entrance.

Near the main staircase, they encountered Edmund Vale.

He was speaking to a clerk.

When he saw them, he ended the conversation and approached.

"Lady Veyr."

"Mr Vale."

"I hope the meeting was productive."

"It was."

"Do you believe the council will adopt the proposal?"

"They will revise it."

Vale smiled.

"Your influence continues."

Seraphina stopped.

"Mr Vale, the council has not adopted anything."

"Not yet."

"Then perhaps you should wait until it does."

Vale inclined his head.

"Of course."

He turned away.

Cedric watched him go.

"Do you think he is deliberately provoking you?"

"I do not know."

"His articles have caused confusion."

"Yes."

"His comments continue to suggest that you are leading the process."

"Yes."

Cedric frowned.

"Then why does he keep doing it?"

Seraphina thought about it.

"Perhaps he believes it makes a better story."

"That is a rather uncharitable explanation."

"It is also a possible one."

They continued toward the carriage.

The journey back to the estate was quiet.

Cedric reviewed the meeting notes.

Seraphina watched the countryside pass outside the window.

She was not disappointed with the day.

The proposed central index might eventually be useful.

The council had agreed to revise the draft.

The regional offices had not been granted new authority.

No estate had been required to submit its records.

The matter was proceeding cautiously.

That was good.

But she could not ignore the fact that her family's name had become attached to a proposal they had never endorsed.

It was a small thing.

It could become a larger one.

When they arrived at the estate, a messenger was waiting at the entrance.

He carried a sealed letter from Lord Benedict Marlowe.

Cedric accepted it.

"Is this urgent?"

The messenger bowed.

"My lord requested that it be delivered today."

Cedric opened the envelope.

He read the letter.

Then he handed it to Seraphina.

The letter was brief.

Lord Marlowe had received a formal inquiry from another landowner concerning the southern drainage channel.

The inquiry asked whether House Veyr and the Marlowe estate had established a new standard for shared watercourse responsibilities.

It also asked whether neighboring landowners could be required to grant access for overflow channels.

Seraphina read the letter.

She looked at Cedric.

"Someone is already treating the agreement as a precedent."

"Yes."

"Who sent the inquiry?"

"Lord Marlowe has not identified the sender in this letter."

Seraphina handed it back.

"Ask him to forward the original."

Cedric nodded.

"And send a copy of our agreement to the regional office."

"With an explanation?"

"Yes. The agreement applies to the three properties involved. It does not establish a general right of access."

Cedric made a note.

Seraphina continued toward the house.

The newspaper articles were no longer merely irritating.

People were beginning to act on them.

That was the point at which a careless story became an actual problem.

The following morning, the original inquiry arrived.

It came from a landowner named Lord Percival Harth.

His estate lay east of the southern provinces, near a small tributary that crossed three private properties.

He had written to Lord Marlowe because he wanted to know whether the recent Veyr agreement could be used as a model for a similar arrangement.

His concern was not entirely unreasonable.

The tributary regularly flooded a lower field.

A neighboring estate had refused access for a drainage survey.

Lord Harth wanted to know whether the regional office could require access.

The letter did not accuse House Veyr of taking authority.

It asked a question.

Seraphina read it carefully.

Then she placed it on the table.

"That is different."

Cedric nodded.

"He wants to understand whether the arrangement can help with his own problem."

"Yes."

"Should we respond?"

"We can explain what our agreement does and does not establish."

"Would you advise him?"

"No. He should consult the regional office and his legal adviser."

Cedric smiled.

"I thought so."

Seraphina looked at the letter again.

The newspaper had distorted the purpose of the regional inspections.

The council was considering a broader system.

Some landowners were beginning to interpret local agreements as general policy.

But not every inquiry was hostile.

Some people simply had real problems and wanted to know whether the work in the north offered a useful example.

She wrote a short response.

It explained that the Veyr drainage agreement had been negotiated between three estates after a joint survey.

It did not create a general right of access.

It did not authorize compulsory inspections.

Any similar arrangement would require examination of the specific properties, existing rights, and applicable law.

She signed it.

"Send this to Lord Marlowe. He can forward it."

Cedric took the letter.

"Will that be all?"

"For now."

That afternoon, Seraphina received the latest edition of the *Aurelion Morning Chronicle*.

The investigative series had begun.

The headline occupied the entire upper half of the page.

**THE NORTHERN REFORMS: WHO REALLY BENEFITS?**

She read the first paragraph.

It described the shared-property register.

It mentioned the Royal Agricultural Council's proposed central index.

It referred to the recent meeting in the capital.

Then came the first claim.

*Sources close to the discussions suggest that several powerful northern families have already begun preparing their records for submission.*

Seraphina stopped.

That was false.

The pilot programme had not been approved.

No estate had agreed to submit records.

She continued.

*One official, speaking on condition of anonymity, suggested that the Veyr estate's recent administrative work had provided the foundation for the proposal.*

The article then described the three regional offices.

It mentioned the Treasury.

It quoted a phrase from the preliminary report.

And it ended with a question.

*If the system is truly voluntary, why have so many estates already begun changing their practices?*

Seraphina put the paper down.

The question was misleading.

The estates had been reviewing their own agreements.

They had not changed them because of the proposed central index.

They had done so because old arrangements needed attention.

The article had taken two separate developments and presented them as one.

She called Cedric.

He arrived within minutes.

He read the article.

Then he looked at her.

"This is more serious."

"Yes."

"Should we issue a public statement?"

Seraphina considered it.

The article contained a specific false claim.

Several estates had supposedly begun preparing their records for submission.

That could be checked.

It also implied that the Veyr estate had initiated the central proposal.

That could be corrected.

A public statement might help.

But it should not become an argument about motives.

"Ask the regional office for confirmation that no estate has been asked to submit records under an approved pilot programme."

Cedric nodded.

"And the council?"

"Request a written clarification of the proposal's current status."

"Then?"

"Then we can issue a factual correction."

Cedric looked at the newspaper again.

"Do you think the writer knows the claim is false?"

"I do not know."

"Should we ask?"

"We can ask where the information came from."

He nodded.

Seraphina picked up her pen.

"Do not accuse him of inventing it. Ask him to identify the source."

Cedric began writing.

The distinction was important.

If the source was mistaken, the correction should say so.

If the source was deliberately misleading, that would be a different matter.

But they did not know which it was.

And Seraphina had no intention of turning an unanswered question into a public accusation.

That evening, Evelyne read the article.

She was less patient than Seraphina.

"Someone is presenting an unapproved proposal as though it has already begun."

"Yes."

"That could damage the participating estates."

"Yes."

"And the council's credibility."

"Yes."

Evelyne placed the paper down.

"Then we should correct it promptly."

Seraphina nodded.

"Once the council confirms the position."

Alaric entered the room.

He had already received a copy.

"Is this the beginning of a campaign against House Veyr?"

Seraphina looked at him.

"It may be."

"Do you believe someone is coordinating the articles?"

"I do not know."

Alaric frowned.

"Three articles, an anonymous memorandum, and now this."

"Those events are connected. That does not establish that they were coordinated."

Her father studied her.

"You are being cautious."

"I would rather be accurate."

Evelyne nodded.

"That is the right approach."

Alaric looked at the article again.

"Still, we should prepare for the possibility that this continues."

"Agreed."

He turned to Seraphina.

"Would you like me to speak with the council?"

"No."

"Why?"

"Because House Veyr should not appear to be pressuring the council. We should send our clarification through the ordinary process."

Alaric nodded.

"Very well."

He was quiet for a moment.

Then he said, "You have become rather good at refusing invitations."

Seraphina looked at him.

"I have had considerable practice."

Evelyne laughed.

For a moment, the tension eased.

Then Cedric entered with a new letter.

It was from Director Harold Fenwick.

He had confirmed that no estate had submitted records for the proposed pilot programme.

The regional office had not requested any.

The central index remained a proposal under review.

The letter also contained a note.

The regional office had received a request from the *Aurelion Morning Chronicle* for copies of its correspondence concerning House Veyr.

The request had been refused because the correspondence contained private estate information.

Seraphina read the note.

"At least they asked."

Cedric nodded.

"They may have received information elsewhere."

"Possibly."

"Should we ask the newspaper for its source?"

"Yes."

She placed the letter beside the article.

The first correction would be straightforward.

The larger question would take longer.

Who had submitted the anonymous memorandum?

Why had the newspaper articles been used as evidence?

And why was the public being told that a voluntary proposal had already begun?

Seraphina looked at the stack of papers.

She had wanted to spend the evening reading.

Instead, she was collecting newspapers.

It was an unpleasant development.

But at least the facts were still available.

For now, that would have to be enough.
