# Chapter 10 — The Duke's Daughter Has Changed

The first sign that something was wrong was the breakfast menu.

Seraphina Veyr noticed it immediately.

There were fewer dishes than usual.

Not dramatically fewer. Nobody had removed the silverware or replaced the fresh bread with stale biscuits. The breakfast table remained perfectly respectable.

But the usual selection of fruit, pastries, eggs, preserves, and several dishes whose names Seraphina had never bothered to learn had been reduced.

She examined the table.

Then she looked at Mira.

“Is something happening?”

Mira adjusted the teapot.

“Your mother has requested a smaller breakfast service.”

“Why?”

“She said the household should avoid unnecessary waste.”

Seraphina considered the arrangement.

“That seems reasonable.”

“I thought you might say that.”

Seraphina looked at the bread.

“Did she also reduce the number of dishes because she expects me to spend the morning reading reports?”

Mira paused.

“I believe she mentioned that you have been skipping breakfast while working.”

Seraphina looked offended.

“I have not been skipping breakfast.”

“You ate half a pear yesterday.”

“That is fruit.”

“It was also your entire breakfast.”

Seraphina picked up a piece of bread.

“I am eating now.”

Mira smiled.

“Your mother will be pleased.”

Seraphina had begun to suspect that her mother and Mira had formed an alliance.

It was becoming increasingly difficult to conduct her private life without supervision.

She took a bite of bread.

Then she noticed the empty chair at the far end of the table.

“Is Father not joining us?”

“His Grace has an early meeting with the regional administrators.”

“About the northern storehouses?”

“I believe so.”

Seraphina lowered her bread.

There it was.

Even breakfast had become vulnerable to agricultural administration.

The second sign that something was wrong came from the servants.

They were behaving differently.

Not dramatically.

No one had started bowing lower or addressing her with exaggerated titles.

But the staff seemed more willing to approach her directly.

A kitchen attendant asked whether the new supply receipt system would apply to food deliveries.

A footman asked whether he should report a damaged corridor lamp through the household office or the maintenance department.

The receiving clerk sent a short note asking whether the trial forms could include a column for delivery time.

Seraphina answered each question as best she could.

By midmorning, she had answered six questions.

She had also been informed that the kitchen, laundry, and household stores departments had begun using the new delivery records.

The system appeared to be working.

This was unfortunate.

Seraphina had hoped it would fail quietly, allowing everyone to return to the previous arrangement.

Instead, Margaret Ellison sent a note stating that the new process had reduced disagreements about deliveries.

Seraphina read it twice.

Then she placed it in a drawer.

She did not want to encourage anyone.

At eleven, Cedric arrived.

He carried a folder.

Seraphina looked at it with suspicion.

“Is that about the storehouses?”

“No.”

She relaxed.

“Good.”

“It concerns the northern property office.”

She stopped relaxing.

“Is that different?”

“Technically.”

Cedric sat down.

“Your father has asked me to explain the current situation before the next administrative meeting.”

Seraphina looked at the folder.

“What situation?”

“The disputed maintenance authorization.”

She sighed.

“Of course.”

Cedric opened the folder.

“The review has established that the additional paragraph attached to the original expenditure approval was not part of the genuine document. The former central official named in the record did not issue it.”

“Do we know who did?”

“Not yet.”

“Do we know who added the paragraph?”

“Not yet.”

“Do we know whether the money was improperly spent?”

“Not conclusively.”

Seraphina nodded.

“Then we still know very little.”

“We know more than we did.”

“That is not particularly comforting.”

Cedric smiled faintly.

“The northern property office has been asked to provide all original correspondence relating to the two maintenance allocations. The regional contractor has also been asked to provide the work records.”

“Has the work been inspected?”

“One section has.”

“And?”

“The work was completed, but the quality is being reviewed.”

Seraphina considered this.

“Then the money may have been spent on actual repairs, even if the authorization was altered.”

“That is possible.”

“Could the additional paragraph have been inserted to cover a legitimate payment?”

Cedric nodded.

“That is one possibility.”

“Could it have been inserted to conceal an improper payment?”

“That is another.”

Seraphina looked at him.

“Do we have any reason to favor one explanation?”

“Not yet.”

“Then we should not.”

Cedric closed the folder.

“That is what your father has instructed.”

Seraphina nodded.

She was relieved.

For once, nobody expected her to solve anything.

At noon, Duchess Evelyne invited Seraphina to lunch.

This was not unusual.

What was unusual was the presence of two additional people.

Duke Alaric sat at the head of the table.

Beside him was a woman Seraphina recognized as Lady Amelia Rosenthal.

The older aristocrat had visited the estate several days earlier.

She wore a dark blue dress and a pearl necklace, and her expression suggested that she had already noticed everything in the room.

Seraphina stopped at the entrance.

“Good afternoon.”

“Seraphina,” Evelyne said warmly. “Come and join us.”

Seraphina took her seat.

Lady Amelia smiled.

“I hear you have been visiting villages.”

Seraphina looked at her mother.

Evelyne continued arranging her napkin.

“I may have mentioned it.”

“Of course.”

Lady Amelia lifted her teacup.

“And apparently you have developed an interest in household administration.”

Seraphina looked at her father.

Alaric was examining the menu.

He did not appear interested in rescuing her.

“I have always been interested in household administration,” Seraphina said.

Lady Amelia smiled.

“Have you?”

Seraphina realized that this was probably not a conversation she could win.

“I am becoming more interested.”

“That sounds more honest.”

Evelyne gave her a small approving glance.

Lunch began.

For several minutes, the conversation remained ordinary.

They discussed the upcoming spring reception, the condition of the capital roads, and a recent disagreement between two noble families concerning the use of a hunting estate.

Seraphina listened.

She had become skilled at recognizing when a conversation was about to become complicated.

Lady Amelia was particularly good at making simple statements sound like questions.

Eventually, the older woman turned toward her.

“Tell me, Seraphina. Do you intend to continue visiting the villages?”

Seraphina considered her answer.

“If necessary.”

“And what do you hope to accomplish?”

“I would like the estate to respond to requests more reliably.”

Lady Amelia nodded.

“An admirable goal.”

“Thank you.”

“But perhaps an ambitious one.”

Seraphina looked at her.

“Why?”

“Because the people who request assistance are rarely the only people involved in providing it.”

Seraphina nodded.

“I have noticed.”

“Farmers ask for repairs. Administrators must decide which repairs are urgent. Contractors must be paid. Workers must be assigned. Roads must be accessible. Funds must be available.”

“Yes.”

“And when one matter is delayed, others may be affected.”

“I understand.”

Lady Amelia studied her.

“Do you?”

Seraphina set down her fork.

“I am beginning to.”

The older woman smiled.

“Then you are learning something useful.”

Evelyne joined the conversation.

“Seraphina has been careful not to make promises she cannot keep.”

“That is good,” Lady Amelia replied. “People are often disappointed not because help is refused, but because they are told it will arrive and then nothing happens.”

Seraphina thought of Westmere.

The village elder had not demanded that she promise repairs.

He had simply wanted someone to look at the irrigation channel.

She looked toward her mother.

“I think people should at least know what is happening with their requests.”

Alaric finally looked up.

“That would require a reliable system of communication.”

“Yes.”

“Which is not always easy across the duchy.”

“I know.”

He studied her.

“Then you should consider the cost of maintaining such a system before recommending it.”

Seraphina nodded.

She had expected that.

Lady Amelia watched the exchange with quiet interest.

“You have become rather practical, Seraphina.”

Seraphina looked down at her plate.

“I suppose I have.”

“Your mother tells me you have also been reading administrative texts.”

“Yes.”

“And financial reports?”

“Occasionally.”

“Do you enjoy them?”

Seraphina paused.

“No.”

Lady Amelia laughed.

Alaric looked amused.

Evelyne shook her head.

“That is probably the most honest answer she has given all afternoon.”

After lunch, Lady Amelia requested a private conversation with Evelyne.

Seraphina was preparing to leave when her mother spoke.

“Stay a moment, dear.”

Seraphina looked between them.

“Is something wrong?”

“No,” Evelyne replied.

Lady Amelia gestured toward the chairs.

“We would simply like to ask you something.”

Seraphina sat down again.

Lady Amelia folded her hands.

“Your behavior has changed considerably over the last few weeks.”

Seraphina's stomach tightened.

She knew this conversation was coming.

She had changed.

There was no denying it.

She no longer enjoyed the same social games.

She no longer found pleasure in making servants nervous.

She had stopped treating household affairs as matters beneath her attention.

She was also far more cautious about her words.

The problem was explaining why.

“I suppose I have been thinking about things differently,” she said.

“Why?”

Seraphina looked at her mother.

Evelyne's expression was calm.

Not suspicious.

Not accusing.

Just attentive.

“I think I used to assume that everything would continue working because it always had.”

Lady Amelia nodded.

“And now?”

“Now I realize that things work because people are doing the work. And sometimes they are doing it without enough information, resources, or support.”

“That is true.”

Seraphina continued.

“I also think I was careless about how I treated people.”

Evelyne's expression softened.

“Careless?”

“Yes.”

She looked down at her hands.

“I expected people to understand what I wanted without explaining it. And when they did not, I became impatient.”

Lady Amelia said nothing.

Seraphina continued.

“I do not think that was fair.”

For a moment, the room was quiet.

Then Evelyne reached across the small table and touched her daughter's hand.

“Recognizing that is important.”

Seraphina nodded.

She did not know what else to say.

Lady Amelia watched them.

“People can change, Seraphina. But they must understand that others may need time to believe it.”

“I know.”

“Do you?”

Seraphina looked up.

Lady Amelia's expression was gentle.

“An apology is not a command for someone to trust you again.”

Seraphina absorbed that.

She had not considered it in those words.

“I understand.”

“Good.”

The older woman rose.

“I should leave you both to your afternoon.”

Evelyne stood as well.

“Thank you for coming, Amelia.”

Lady Amelia turned toward Seraphina.

“I look forward to seeing you at the spring reception.”

Seraphina smiled politely.

“As do I.”

The older woman left.

Seraphina remained seated.

Her mother looked at her.

“You handled that well.”

“I was not certain what she wanted.”

“She wanted to know whether your changes were genuine.”

Seraphina looked down.

“Were they?”

Evelyne sat beside her.

“That is something you will have to answer through your actions.”

Later that afternoon, Seraphina visited the household office.

She had intended to check the progress of the new supply records.

Instead, she found Margaret Ellison speaking with two department supervisors.

A large sheet of paper had been placed on the table.

It contained a list of household responsibilities.

Seraphina stopped at the doorway.

“What is this?”

Margaret turned.

“My lady.”

The supervisors bowed.

Margaret gestured toward the paper.

“We are reviewing the current department schedules.”

Seraphina approached.

“Why?”

“Because several duties are listed under outdated positions. We thought it would be useful to clarify which supervisors are responsible for them.”

Seraphina studied the document.

The kitchen, laundry, stores, guest rooms, and general household departments were listed separately.

Each had a supervisor.

There were also notes concerning temporary replacements and reporting arrangements.

“This is very thorough.”

“Thank you, my lady.”

“Who prepared it?”

“Mr. Clarke began the first draft. The department supervisors have been reviewing it.”

Seraphina looked toward the younger receiving clerk.

He stood near the end of the table, looking distinctly uncomfortable.

“You prepared this?”

“Yes, my lady.”

“Why?”

Mr. Clarke hesitated.

“I thought it might help clarify the delivery responsibilities.”

Seraphina looked at the sheet again.

It was a good idea.

She also knew that the clerk had not been asked to prepare it.

“Does this create additional work for you?”

“A little.”

“Are you able to manage it alongside your regular duties?”

“I believe so.”

Margaret intervened.

“We have agreed to reduce some of his routine copying while the schedules are being reviewed.”

Seraphina nodded.

“Good.”

She looked at the document again.

“I do not want anyone taking on additional work simply because a new procedure has been introduced.”

Mr. Clarke looked relieved.

Seraphina continued.

“If the schedules are useful, keep them. But please make sure they reflect what people actually do, rather than what an old document says they should do.”

Margaret nodded.

“That is our intention.”

Seraphina smiled.

“Then I will leave you to it.”

She turned toward the door.

“Thank you, my lady,” Margaret said.

Seraphina paused.

“For what?”

“For asking us.”

Seraphina looked at her.

Margaret continued.

“Most of the time, the household is only noticed when something goes wrong.”

Seraphina remembered her mother's words.

She nodded.

“I will try to do better.”

Margaret smiled.

It was a small expression.

But it was genuine.

That evening, the family gathered for dinner.

Alaric had returned from his meeting.

Cedric was also present, having stayed at the estate to review the agricultural records.

The conversation began with ordinary matters.

Evelyne discussed the spring reception arrangements.

Alaric mentioned a letter from the royal council.

Cedric explained that the northern agricultural office had begun reviewing the old storehouse records.

Seraphina listened quietly.

Then her father turned toward her.

“Seraphina.”

“Yes?”

“I have something to ask.”

She set down her fork.

“What is it?”

“The regional administrator has suggested that you attend the next northern agricultural review meeting.”

Seraphina froze.

“No.”

Alaric raised an eyebrow.

“You have not heard the reason.”

“I am certain it involves reports.”

“It does.”

“Then no.”

Cedric coughed.

Evelyne gave her daughter a warning look.

Alaric continued calmly.

“The administrator believes your observations from Westmere may be useful.”

Seraphina stared at him.

“Why?”

“Because you identified several gaps in the village records.”

“I identified questions.”

“Which is often how problems are found.”

Seraphina looked at Cedric.

He was trying very hard not to smile.

“This is how it begins,” she said.

“How what begins?” Evelyne asked.

“First, I visit one village. Then I attend one meeting. Then someone asks me to review a report. Then I become responsible for the entire northern agricultural district.”

Alaric considered this.

“That is unlikely.”

“Why?”

“Because the northern agricultural district is not your responsibility.”

Seraphina relaxed slightly.

“Good.”

“You are the duke's daughter. You are expected to understand the estate's affairs, but that does not mean you must personally manage every department.”

She looked at him.

“That is reassuring.”

He continued.

“You may attend the meeting as an observer. You are not being asked to make decisions.”

Seraphina thought about it.

“Would I be required to speak?”

“Only if you have something useful to contribute.”

She sighed.

“That is an unfortunate condition.”

Cedric finally laughed.

Evelyne smiled.

Alaric looked almost pleased.

“Then I will inform the administrator that you will attend.”

Seraphina looked at him.

“I did not say yes.”

“You did not say no.”

“I said it was unfortunate.”

“That is not a refusal.”

Seraphina stared at her father.

She was beginning to suspect that the Veyr family had developed an unhealthy interest in administrative loopholes.

The following morning, a letter arrived from the regional agricultural office.

It confirmed the meeting date.

It also included a preliminary agenda.

The subjects were:

- Review of northern grain storage records.
- Condition of reserve facilities.
- Maintenance priorities for agricultural roads.
- Outstanding village requests.
- Proposed inspection schedule.

Seraphina read the list.

Then she put it down.

She was not going to panic.

She was not going to assume the meeting would become a disaster.

She was not going to volunteer for anything.

She repeated these promises while dressing.

By the time she reached breakfast, she had almost convinced herself.

Then she noticed her father reading a separate letter.

He looked up.

“Seraphina.”

She stopped.

“Yes?”

“The regional administrator has added another item to the agenda.”

She waited.

“Historical emergency reserve distribution records.”

Seraphina closed her eyes.

Of course.

The old storehouse.

The eighty missing measures.

The second location.

She sat down.

“Does the administrator know about N-09?”

Alaric looked at her.

“He does now.”

“Has it been located?”

“Not yet.”

Seraphina picked up her tea.

“Then perhaps the meeting will be interesting.”

Cedric, seated nearby, looked at her.

“Are you looking forward to it?”

“No.”

“Then why did you say that?”

“Because I am trying to be optimistic.”

Evelyne smiled.

“A commendable effort.”

Seraphina took a sip of tea.

She had wanted a quiet life.

She had wanted to avoid the royal court, the saintess, and dangerous magical experiments.

She had wanted to spend her days reading, walking in the garden, and occasionally doing something useful without becoming involved in anything complicated.

Instead, she was now expected to attend a regional agricultural meeting.

It was not a catastrophe.

It was not even particularly dramatic.

But it was beginning to feel as though the entire duchy had discovered that the duke's daughter was willing to ask questions.

And that was a dangerous reputation to acquire.

That afternoon, Seraphina received a final note from Margaret Ellison.

The household supply trial had been successful.

The departments had agreed to continue using the new delivery records.

The head housekeeper also proposed a monthly meeting between the department supervisors to review recurring problems.

Seraphina read the note.

Then she smiled.

It was a small improvement.

No grand announcement.

No political consequences.

No dramatic confrontation.

Just a household that might function a little better.

She wrote a short reply.

*Approved for a trial period of three months. Please ensure that the meetings remain practical and do not interfere with the departments' ordinary duties.*

She paused.

Then added:

*Thank you for taking the initiative.*

When she finished, she leaned back.

Perhaps this was what she wanted.

Not to control everything.

Not to become indispensable.

Not to turn the estate into some perfect administrative machine.

Just to make things a little less difficult for the people who lived and worked there.

She looked toward the window.

The gardens were quiet.

A gardener was trimming the hedges.

Two maids crossed the courtyard carrying folded linens.

Somewhere in the distance, a stable attendant called to a horse.

The estate continued functioning.

For once, Seraphina felt no immediate desire to inspect anything.

She picked up her book.

Then the door opened.

Mira entered.

“My lady, a messenger has arrived from the capital.”

Seraphina slowly lowered the book.

“Who is the message from?”

“The Royal Academy.”

A pause.

Seraphina stared at her.

“Why?”

Mira placed the sealed envelope on the table.

“It is addressed personally to Lady Seraphina Veyr.”

Seraphina looked at the royal seal.

She knew that institution.

She remembered its importance in the original novel.

The Royal Academy.

The place where the saintess would eventually study.

The place where the crown prince would become involved in several political disputes.

The place where magical research would begin to intersect with the kingdom's larger problems.

Seraphina did not touch the envelope.

She looked at Mira.

“Can we send it back?”

Mira blinked.

“My lady?”

“I am asking whether we can politely return it without opening it.”

“I do not believe that would be advisable.”

Seraphina sighed.

“Of course not.”

She broke the seal.

The letter was formal.

The Royal Academy had received an invitation from the Duchess of Veyr to attend the upcoming spring reception.

The academy's senior representatives had been asked to provide a short presentation concerning recent developments in magical infrastructure and public safety.

Seraphina read the final paragraph.

Then she read it again.

*Lady Seraphina Veyr is respectfully invited to attend a preliminary discussion with the Academy's administrative delegation, given her recent interest in regional infrastructure and estate maintenance.*

She slowly placed the letter on the table.

Mira watched her.

“Is something wrong, my lady?”

Seraphina stared at the words.

She had specifically written a rule about avoiding questionable magical projects.

She had not even attended the Academy.

Yet somehow, it had already found her.

She closed her eyes.

“I think,” she said carefully, “that my quiet life has become considerably less cooperative.”
