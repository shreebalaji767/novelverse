# Chapter 19 — The Clerk Who Was Transferred Twice

The first difficulty with an old missing document was discovering whether it had ever been missing.

The second was discovering who had been responsible for keeping it.

The third, as Seraphina was beginning to understand, was discovering whether that person had been transferred, dismissed, retired, promoted, or had simply moved to another office without telling anyone where the records had gone.

She stared at the three letters on her desk.

One came from the Royal Archive.

One came from the Northern Provincial Supply Office.

The third was a note from Cedric.

The first confirmed that the temporary Northern Consolidated Depot had received all 260 measures of grain during the winter emergency twenty-six years earlier.

The second confirmed that the Northern Provincial Supply Office had inherited responsibility for the depot's final reconciliation.

The third contained a sentence that Seraphina read twice.

*The records concerning Thomas Weller are not held in the office where we expected them to be.*

She put the letters down.

"Of course."

Mira, who was arranging the morning correspondence, glanced over.

"Is something wrong, my lady?"

"No. The records are simply behaving like aristocrats."

Mira paused.

Seraphina tapped the letter from Cedric.

"They have left their assigned residence without informing anyone."

Mira considered this.

"Should I ask the steward to search the guest rooms?"

"No. I suspect the records have gone somewhere considerably less convenient."

She picked up the third letter again.

Cedric's handwriting was neat, though the pressure of his pen had increased toward the bottom of the page.

He had visited the provincial office in person. The clerk there had found an appointment register listing Thomas Weller as a junior reconciliation clerk during the year after the emergency. However, a later personnel index showed Weller transferred to the Northern Agricultural Administration.

The Agricultural Administration's surviving index, meanwhile, listed him as having returned to the Provincial Supply Office.

Neither office had a departure record.

Neither had a receiving acknowledgment.

The matter had been marked as resolved.

Seraphina looked at the final sentence.

*The current clerk says this is not unusual for older transfers.*

She closed her eyes.

There were several things she disliked about that sentence.

The most obvious was that the clerk had apparently considered it a sufficient explanation.

The less obvious problem was that a person could not be in two offices at once, unless he had discovered a form of magic that the Royal Academy had neglected to mention.

"Send a message to Cedric," she said.

Mira reached for her writing materials.

"Tell him I want the original transfer entries, not another summary. Also ask whether the personnel registers contain Weller's appointment date and department."

"Yes, my lady."

"And ask the provincial office whether they have a forwarding address."

Mira hesitated.

"Would a forwarding address still be available after twenty-six years?"

"Possibly not. But I would prefer a refusal based on an actual search rather than an assumption."

Mira wrote it down.

Seraphina leaned back.

The old grain had been received.

That was something.

It had not yet been allocated.

That was the problem.

And the person assigned to reconcile it had apparently become an administrative ghost.

She reached for her tea.

It had gone cold.

She drank it anyway.

The Northern Provincial Supply Office occupied a long stone building beside the regional treasury.

It was not particularly impressive. Its windows were narrow, its corridors were crowded, and its entrance steps had developed a slight slope after years of winter frost.

Cedric had visited it twice in three days.

On the second visit, he was beginning to understand why his cousin disliked unresolved administrative questions.

Not because they were difficult.

Because they were often surrounded by people who believed they had already been answered.

"Mr. Weller was transferred to the Agricultural Administration," Cedric said.

The clerk behind the counter adjusted his spectacles.

"That is what the index says."

"And the Agricultural Administration says he returned here."

"That is what their index says."

"Then which is correct?"

The clerk looked at him as though Cedric had asked him to explain the weather.

"Both records are official."

Cedric waited.

The clerk waited.

A man behind Cedric coughed.

Finally, Cedric said, "I understand that both records are official. I am asking which office employed him."

The clerk opened a drawer.

"I shall see whether there is another register."

Cedric watched him disappear into a back room.

He had once believed that the greatest difficulty in estate administration was obtaining accurate information from people who did not want to provide it.

He now suspected that a more formidable difficulty was obtaining information from people who were entirely willing to help but had no idea where it was.

The clerk returned with a thick ledger.

"Here."

Cedric opened it.

The entry was brief.

**Thomas Weller. Junior Reconciliation Clerk. Northern Provincial Supply Office.**

A transfer notation had been added beneath it.

**Assigned temporarily to Agricultural Administration.**

No date of return.

No signature.

No receiving acknowledgment.

"Who made this entry?"

The clerk leaned forward.

"That would have been the personnel officer."

"Who was the personnel officer?"

"At the time?"

"Yes."

The clerk considered the question.

"I would have to check."

Cedric took out his notebook.

"Please do."

The clerk frowned slightly.

"That may take a while."

"I have already travelled here twice."

The clerk looked at him.

Cedric looked back.

After a moment, the clerk stood.

"I shall check."

This time, he returned with a different ledger.

The personnel officer had been a woman named Margaret Fenner.

She had retired nineteen years earlier.

There was no record of where she had gone.

Cedric rubbed his forehead.

"Does the office have a retirement register?"

"Yes."

"May I see it?"

The clerk looked uncomfortable.

"That is kept by the central personnel department."

"Where is that?"

"In the same building."

Cedric stared at him.

The clerk cleared his throat.

"Different floor."

Cedric slowly closed his notebook.

He had never felt so close to understanding Seraphina's complaints.

At Veyr Estate, Seraphina was having a different problem.

A farmer had arrived with a broken wheel.

Not a broken cart.

A wheel.

He had carried it into the administrative courtyard with the determination of a man who had decided that if the estate wanted to discuss agricultural supplies, it could begin by looking at the thing that had failed him.

The wheel was placed against the wall of the receiving room.

Its wooden rim had split near the iron binding.

The farmer, Tomas Reed, stood beside it with his cap in both hands.

"I was told the estate was reviewing supply problems," he explained.

Seraphina looked at the wheel.

"Who told you that?"

"Mr. Mercer."

"The village elder?"

"Yes, my lady."

"And he suggested you bring the wheel?"

"No. He suggested I report the problem."

Tomas looked down at the wheel.

"I thought it would be easier if I brought it."

It was, Seraphina admitted privately.

She gestured toward a chair.

"Please sit."

Tomas remained standing.

"Is the wheel yours?"

"Yes."

"Was it used for transporting grain?"

"Among other things. It carried seed, tools, firewood. Anything that needed moving."

"How long has it been broken?"

"Since last week."

"And you have been unable to replace it?"

Tomas shifted his cap.

"The wheelwright says he can repair it, but he needs iron fittings. The merchant has fittings, but he wants payment in advance. I have money coming after the next delivery."

Seraphina looked toward the clerk sitting at the side table.

"Do we have a record of this?"

The clerk consulted his notebook.

"Not under Tomas Reed's name. There is a general request from Westmere for transport equipment repairs."

"That is not the same thing."

"No, my lady."

She looked at Tomas again.

"How many carts are affected?"

"Three that I know of. Mine, Eliza Ward's, and one belonging to the miller."

"Are they all waiting for the same fittings?"

"Mostly. Different sizes."

Seraphina considered this.

It was tempting to treat the matter as an isolated hardship and authorize payment.

But if several farmers were waiting for the same materials, there might be a supply issue affecting more than one household.

She did not know enough to decide.

"Would you be willing to have the wheelwright send a list of the parts he needs and the cost of each repair?"

Tomas nodded.

"Yes, my lady."

"Ask him to separate materials from labour. I do not want him doing unpaid work, and I do not want the estate paying for materials twice."

Tomas looked surprised.

"I wouldn't ask him to work for nothing."

"I know. This is to make sure the account is clear."

He nodded again.

Seraphina glanced at the wheel.

"Can you still use the cart without it?"

"No, my lady."

"Then the estate will arrange a temporary cart for your next delivery. The repair request can be examined separately."

Tomas's shoulders relaxed.

"Thank you."

"Do not thank me yet. I have not found a cart."

He blinked.

Seraphina turned to the clerk.

"Check the transport office. We may have an unused cart or one awaiting repair that can be borrowed safely."

"Yes, my lady."

Tomas looked at her with an expression that suggested he was uncertain whether she had just made a joke.

She had not.

The estate had several carts.

Somewhere.

Probably.

The first response from the transport office was that all available carts were assigned.

The second was that one cart had been assigned but was not being used.

The third was that the cart was being used to store sacks of damaged harness fittings.

Seraphina read the report twice.

"Why are harness fittings stored in a cart?"

The clerk, who had been sent to retrieve the information, looked apologetic.

"The storeroom is full."

"Why is the storeroom full?"

"Because the replacement storeroom is under repair."

"Why is the replacement storeroom under repair?"

"Because the roof—"

"Stop."

He stopped.

Seraphina put the report down.

"Where is the cart?"

"In the east transport yard."

"Is it safe to use?"

"I don't know."

"Then please find out."

The clerk hurried away.

Mira watched him go.

"That seems to be happening often."

"What?"

"People being asked to find out whether something is safe."

Seraphina looked at her.

"Would you prefer they simply guess?"

"No, my lady."

"Neither would I."

The cart was eventually found to be structurally sound, though one of its sideboards needed replacing. The transport supervisor agreed to lend it to Tomas for three days.

The harness fittings were moved to a dry covered area.

The storeroom roof inspection was added to the maintenance schedule.

It was not a dramatic improvement.

Nobody had been rescued from a burning building.

No criminal had been exposed.

A farmer had received a cart.

Seraphina considered this an acceptable use of an afternoon.

Cedric returned just before the evening meal.

He looked tired.

He also carried a small stack of papers.

Seraphina found him in the study with Roland, who had been examining the current grain delivery accounts.

"Did you find him?" she asked.

Cedric placed the papers on the desk.

"Not yet. But we found the personnel officer's retirement record."

"Margaret Fenner?"

"Yes. She retired in the third month of the following year. Her successor was a man named Edwin Marsh."

Roland looked up.

"Marsh?"

"You know him?"

"I know the name. He was a regional auditor before being appointed to the central personnel office."

Cedric continued.

"More importantly, I found a second reference to Thomas Weller. It is in a departmental workload register, not the personnel index."

Seraphina moved closer.

Cedric opened the register.

The entry was dated two months after the emergency.

**Weller, Thomas — temporary assignment: Northern Agricultural Administration. Purpose: reconciliation of emergency grain allocations and return of temporary depot records.**

Seraphina read it carefully.

"That is more specific."

"Yes. It also includes an expected completion date."

"How long?"

"Three months."

"Was it completed?"

Cedric turned the page.

The next entry had been made six months later.

**Assignment extended pending receipt of village distribution confirmations.**

Seraphina looked at Roland.

"That sounds less like a transfer and more like a secondment."

"It does," Roland said. "He may have remained employed by the Provincial Supply Office while working at the Agricultural Administration."

"Then why does the other register say he returned?"

"Because someone may have closed the temporary assignment without recording the physical return."

Cedric tapped the page.

"Or because he was moved to another task."

Seraphina looked at the final line.

There was a handwritten note beside the extension.

**Further instructions to follow from central reconciliation office.**

No signature.

No date.

No attached document reference.

"Do we know what happened to the central reconciliation office?"

Roland's expression changed.

"It was reorganized."

"When?"

"About twenty years ago. Its responsibilities were divided between the Treasury's regional accounts division and the Agricultural Administration."

Seraphina stared at him.

"Was that before or after Weller's assignment?"

"After."

"Then the records may have been split again."

Roland nodded.

"That is possible."

Cedric looked unhappy.

"Which means we may need to search two departments."

"Possibly three," Roland said.

Seraphina sat down.

She had been hoping for a simple answer.

A signed allocation schedule would have been ideal. A list of villages, quantities, and receiving signatures. Something that would allow the old account to be closed properly.

Instead, the investigation had found a clerk who had been assigned to reconcile the matter, then assigned to continue reconciling it, then apparently disappeared into an office reorganization.

"Was there a deadline for the final reconciliation?" she asked.

Cedric checked the register.

"Not in this document."

"Any indication that Weller was disciplined?"

"No."

"Dismissed?"

"No."

"Retired?"

"No."

"Then we should not assume he failed to complete the work."

Roland nodded approvingly.

"Quite right."

Seraphina pointed to the workload register.

"We need the assignment file, the central office's instruction, and the village confirmations. We should also ask whether the Agricultural Administration retained a copy of the final allocation schedule even if the original went elsewhere."

Cedric began writing.

"And Weller?"

"Find out whether he is still alive."

Cedric looked up.

Seraphina added, "I am not suggesting we send someone to search every graveyard in the north. Start with the personnel records. If he retired, there may be a pension file."

Roland gave a quiet cough.

"That is a sensible place to begin."

Seraphina glanced at him.

"You sound relieved."

"I am relieved that we are not searching graveyards."

Cedric laughed.

It was brief, but it eased the tension in the room.

Seraphina looked back at the register.

There was one more detail.

The extension had been requested because village distribution confirmations had not arrived.

That meant the missing schedule might not be a single document.

It might have been a collection of confirmations that had never been assembled.

The grain could have reached villages.

The villages could have received it.

The records could have remained scattered.

Or the grain could have been diverted after reaching the temporary depot.

They did not know.

And the fact that they did not know was not proof of the worst possibility.

She wrote that down.

Not because she needed reminding.

Because everyone investigating the matter needed to remember it.

The next morning, a messenger arrived from the Northern Agricultural Administration.

The letter was addressed to Cedric, but its contents concerned the old emergency depot.

They had found a partial distribution ledger.

Not the final allocation schedule.

A separate working copy.

The ledger listed five destinations.

Westmere.

Northbridge.

Greyford.

A village called Millhaven.

And a small settlement named Alder's Crossing.

The first three entries contained quantities.

The last two were incomplete.

Westmere had received forty measures.

Northbridge had received sixty.

Greyford had received thirty.

Millhaven had a quantity of twenty-five, but the receiving signature was missing.

Alder's Crossing had no quantity recorded.

The total recorded distribution was 155 measures.

Seraphina looked at the figure.

"That leaves 105."

Roland checked the original depot register.

"Two hundred and sixty received. One hundred and fifty-five distributed according to this working ledger."

"Which means 105 are unaccounted for in this document."

"Yes. They may have been assigned elsewhere, retained as reserve, or distributed through a separate list."

Seraphina read both.

"Then Alder's Crossing may never have received its allocation."

"Or it may have received it later," Cedric said.

"Do we have a later record?"

"Not yet."

She pointed to the Millhaven note.

"Can we contact the village office?"

"The settlement still exists, though it is now part of the western agricultural district."

"Then ask whether its old village records survived."

Roland examined the ledger again.

"The distribution dates are spread over six weeks. The depot was not emptied in a single operation."

"That matters."

"Yes. It suggests the grain was dispatched in stages."

Seraphina nodded.

She was beginning to see the outline of the problem.

The emergency order had established a temporary depot.

The depot had received the grain.

A temporary clerk had been assigned to reconcile distribution.

The surviving working ledger recorded some deliveries, but not all.

The central office had later been reorganized.

Some records had been sent to the Agricultural Administration.

Others might have gone to the Treasury.

The final reconciliation had never been found.

This was not a simple question of whether 260 measures had vanished.

It was a question of what had happened to each portion of the grain, who had been responsible for recording it, and whether the final account had ever been completed.

A long process.

An unpleasant one.

But not impossible.

"Send copies of the relevant entries to the village offices," she said. "Ask them to search their own records. Do not ask them to certify something they cannot verify."

Cedric nodded.

"And the 105 measures?"

"Keep them as an unresolved balance. We should not assign them to a destination because the numbers would look tidier."

Roland gave a small smile.

"An unusual instruction in administrative work."

"Then perhaps administrative work should become more unusual."

That afternoon, Seraphina received a message from Liora.

It was short, written in a careful hand.

*Lady Seraphina,*

*Master Fenwick has confirmed that the Academy's inspection team will arrive in two days. We have also been informed that the western conduit has a second access point near the old mill road. I thought you should know before the team begins its preparations.*

*The local maintenance workers have asked whether the inspection will interfere with the irrigation repairs. I have told them that it should not, but I would prefer to confirm that with you.*

*With regards,*

*Liora Everwyn*

Seraphina read the message.

She had not expected Liora to contact her directly about the inspection.

It was a sensible question, however.

The irrigation repairs and the conduit inspection were scheduled within the same stretch of land. Even if the two projects were technically separate, they could interfere with each other.

Seraphina called for Cedric.

"Have the maintenance workers been told where the inspection team will be?"

"Not yet. The Academy's plan only gives the access point near the western boundary."

"Then we need the second access point marked before they arrive."

Cedric took the letter.

"Near the old mill road?"

"Yes. And confirm whether the irrigation work requires digging near it."

"I shall ask the surveyor."

Seraphina considered the Academy's formal plan.

She did not want to become responsible for a magical inspection.

She also did not want ordinary maintenance workers to accidentally damage a conduit because two departments had failed to communicate.

"Send a copy of the inspection plan to the local maintenance supervisor," she said. "Ask him to mark the areas where work is scheduled. The Academy can identify any conflicts."

Cedric nodded.

"And reply to Lady Liora?"

"Tell her I appreciate the warning. Also tell her that I would rather delay a minor inspection than have workers digging into something they do not understand."

Cedric folded the letter.

"That is probably wise."

"Probably?"

"Master Lucien may disagree."

"Master Lucien is welcome to disagree after explaining how much it costs to repair a damaged irrigation channel."

Cedric laughed again.

Seraphina returned to her desk.

There were still several letters to answer.

The grain investigation had gained a new lead.

The village cart had been borrowed.

The Westmere roof repairs were awaiting replacement supports.

The Academy would arrive in two days.

And somewhere in the old records, a clerk named Thomas Weller had been assigned to a task that had apparently outlived three departments.

She opened her notebook.

Under the heading *Northern Emergency Grain — Unresolved Questions*, she added:

1. Locate Weller's assignment file and pension or later employment record.
2. Obtain the complete working distribution ledger.
3. Verify the five recorded destinations.
4. Identify the remaining 105 measures without assuming they were lost.
5. Locate the final reconciliation, if one was ever completed.
6. Confirm the temporary depot's closure and return of the land.

She paused.

Then she added one more line.

7. Do not let the search for old records interfere with current grain deliveries.

That last point was important.

The people living in Westmere did not benefit from knowing what had happened to grain twenty-six years ago if their current supplies were delayed.

The past mattered.

But it was not an excuse to neglect the present.

Seraphina closed the notebook.

For a moment, the room was quiet.

Then Mira entered carrying a tray.

"My lady, your father has asked whether you will attend dinner with the regional commanders tomorrow."

Seraphina looked at her.

"Why?"

"He said it would be useful for you to hear their concerns about winter transport."

Seraphina stared at the tray.

Then at Mira.

Then back at the tray.

"Is there food?"

"Yes, my lady."

"Then I shall attend."

Mira smiled.

"Shall I tell His Grace?"

"Yes. And tell him I am willing to listen. I am not agreeing to reorganize the military."

"I shall make that clear."

Seraphina picked up her tea.

It was still warm.

A small victory.

She took a sip and looked toward the window, where the northern sky was turning grey.

The old emergency records had not revealed a villain.

They had revealed an incomplete process, a temporary office, a missing allocation schedule, and a clerk whose employment history had become difficult to follow.

That was less satisfying than a conspiracy.

It was also more believable.

And, unfortunately, considerably more work.

**End of Chapter 19**
