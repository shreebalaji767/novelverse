# Chapter 62 — The Rumour That Had Arrived Before Its Evidence

The Royal Agricultural Council replied after six days.

It sent an agenda.

It sent a list of participating departments.

It sent a proposed schedule.

It sent a preliminary report.

It did not send the names of the people who had requested the meeting.

Seraphina read the documents at her desk.

The proposed meeting concerned the possibility of adapting the regional inspection format for use in other districts.

The participating departments included the Royal Agricultural Council, the Ministry of Lands, the Treasury, and the Royal Infrastructure Department.

There were also representatives from three regional offices.

The preliminary report was twelve pages long.

The first four described the northern inspection programme.

The next three summarized the shared-property register.

The remaining pages discussed possible administrative applications.

Seraphina stopped at page eight.

A sentence had been underlined in red.

*The northern approach may offer a model for the consolidation of private property records under a centralized regional framework.*

She read it again.

Then she placed the report on the desk.

"That is not what we did."

Mira, who was arranging the correspondence, looked up.

"What did you do, my lady?"

"We recorded existing agreements."

"Is that different?"

"Very."

Seraphina turned the page.

The report suggested that a central office might maintain copies of estate agreements, inspect shared properties, and recommend revisions where arrangements appeared outdated.

The idea was not necessarily unreasonable.

The problem was that it had been derived from a project that had done none of those things.

House Veyr had not surrendered its private agreements.

The neighboring estates had not transferred their records to a central authority.

The regional office had not acquired the right to revise private arrangements.

The register was a practical tool.

The report was proposing something considerably larger.

Seraphina wrote a note in the margin.

*Clarify the distinction before attending.*

Then she turned to the final page.

There was a list of preliminary participants.

One name caught her attention.

Edmund Vale.

The same name printed beneath the first newspaper article.

Seraphina leaned back.

"Interesting."

Mira approached.

"Is something wrong?"

"Perhaps."

She showed her the list.

Mira read it.

"Is he the newspaper writer?"

"Yes."

"Why is he listed as an observer?"

"That is what I would like to know."

The list described him as an independent administrative correspondent invited to provide an external assessment.

Seraphina read the phrase twice.

A newspaper writer had published an article about her work.

The Royal Agricultural Council had then invited him to observe a meeting about adapting that work.

It might be coincidence.

It might be an ordinary arrangement.

It was not proof of anything.

But it was worth asking about.

Evelyne examined the report that afternoon.

She read it more slowly than Seraphina had.

When she finished, she closed the document.

"This is not simply a discussion about the register."

"No."

"They are considering a centralized framework."

"Yes."

"Do you support it?"

"I do not know enough about the proposal."

Evelyne nodded.

"That is the correct answer."

She opened the report again.

"Some parts are sensible. A central office could preserve copies of agreements. It might help estates locate records when documents are lost."

"That is possible."

"But who would have access?"

"That is not explained."

"Who would be responsible for protecting the records?"

"Not explained."

"Would the office have authority to recommend changes?"

"The report says recommend."

"Would the recommendations be voluntary?"

"It does not say."

Evelyne placed the report on the table.

"Then it is not ready for approval."

Seraphina agreed.

She was not opposed to better recordkeeping.

She was opposed to vague proposals that quietly acquired powers nobody had properly discussed.

"Will you attend?" Evelyne asked.

"I will ask for clarification first."

"Good."

Evelyne looked at the preliminary participant list.

"Edmund Vale."

"Yes."

"Do you know whether he has any connection to the council?"

"Not yet."

"Find out before the meeting."

Seraphina nodded.

Her mother continued.

"Do not assume that his presence means the council arranged the newspaper articles."

"I will not."

"And do not assume that because the articles were inaccurate, every person who repeats them is acting maliciously."

"I know."

Evelyne smiled.

"Good. I would prefer not to spend the next month defending ourselves against accusations we invented ourselves."

Seraphina considered that.

"It would be inefficient."

"Very."

The first official inquiry arrived the next morning.

It came from the Marlowe estate.

Lord Benedict Marlowe had received a letter from a distant landowner asking whether the recent drainage agreement had been revised under a new regional property policy.

He wanted clarification.

Cedric read the letter in the administrative office.

"Lord Marlowe is concerned that the revised channel responsibilities may be interpreted as a precedent."

Seraphina sat across from him.

"What does he mean?"

"He believes another estate may use the arrangement to demand access to a watercourse on his land."

"That agreement was specific to the properties involved."

"I know."

"Then tell him that."

Cedric nodded.

He prepared a response explaining that the southern drainage arrangement had been negotiated between House Veyr, the Marlowe estate, and Ashcombe.

It applied only to the identified channel and the properties covered by the agreement.

It did not establish a general right of access for other estates.

The letter was sent before noon.

That afternoon, a second inquiry arrived.

This one came from the Bellmont estate.

Its steward wanted to know whether the shared storehouse register would be submitted to the regional office.

Cedric explained that a copy of the relevant guidance format would be shared, but the private inventory and ownership records would remain with the estates.

A third inquiry arrived from a merchant association.

It asked whether the new system would require merchants using private mills to register their storage arrangements.

Seraphina stared at the letter.

"Why would it?"

Cedric sighed.

"I suspect they have read the second article."

"Then tell them there is no such requirement."

He wrote another response.

By the end of the day, the estate had answered five inquiries.

None concerned an actual dispute.

All concerned what people believed might happen.

Seraphina closed the final letter.

"Someone has made an entire administrative system out of a register."

Cedric leaned back.

"People are filling in the missing details themselves."

"With what?"

"Whatever they expect."

That was the problem.

A person who feared central authority would imagine compulsory inspections.

A person who wanted central authority might imagine an efficient new department.

A landowner who disliked House Veyr might imagine an attempt to expand its influence.

The same report could become three different threats depending on who read it.

Seraphina looked at the pile of letters.

She had wanted to improve the way her family understood its inherited agreements.

She had not intended to become a symbol in an argument about the future of estate administration.

Unfortunately, symbols were apparently cheaper to produce than actual policies.

Two days later, Cedric returned from the regional office with an unexpected visitor.

The visitor was a clerk named Thomas Barlow.

Not the veteran gardener who had inspected the orchard fence.

This Thomas Barlow worked for the regional agricultural administration.

He was a short, careful man in his late forties, carrying a leather folder and a letter of introduction from Harold Fenwick.

He requested a meeting with Cedric.

Seraphina joined them after reading the letter.

The clerk bowed.

"My lady."

"Please sit."

He placed his folder on the table.

"I have been asked to clarify the origin of several statements in the preliminary report."

"Which statements?"

"The claims concerning centralized property records and regional inspection authority."

Seraphina glanced at Cedric.

"Who asked you?"

"Director Fenwick."

"Why?"

The clerk opened his folder.

"Because the regional office has received inquiries from estates that believe the guidance document grants it powers it does not possess."

Seraphina nodded.

"That is understandable."

"We reviewed the document and the correspondence that preceded its preparation. We found that the report's description of the northern programme combines information from several separate sources."

"Which sources?"

"The Veyr register, the regional meeting notes, and a memorandum submitted to the Royal Agricultural Council."

"Who submitted the memorandum?"

The clerk turned a page.

"That is not entirely clear."

Seraphina waited.

"It was received by the council's secretariat on the twelfth day of the previous month. The document was unsigned. It was attached to a request for preliminary consideration of a centralized property-record system."

Cedric frowned.

"Was it based on our register?"

"The memorandum refers to it."

"Does it quote the register?"

"Several passages appear to have been adapted from it."

Seraphina looked at the folder.

"Who prepared the memorandum?"

"We do not know."

"Was it submitted by the newspaper?"

"No. The council's receiving record identifies the sender as a private correspondent. The name was not recorded."

"Then how did Edmund Vale become an observer?"

The clerk hesitated.

"He was invited by the council's secretariat after submitting a request for access to the preliminary discussion."

"Did he submit the memorandum?"

"We cannot establish that."

"Did he provide the council with information?"

"His request included a summary of the newspaper articles."

Seraphina considered this.

That did not prove the writer had coordinated anything.

He had published an article.

The council had used it as part of the background for a discussion.

The memorandum had been submitted by an unknown correspondent.

The report had combined several sources.

It was a chain of events.

It was also a chain with missing links.

"Can we see the memorandum?" she asked.

The clerk opened his folder.

"I brought a copy."

Seraphina read it.

The document was only four pages long.

It argued that private estate agreements were difficult to locate and that disputes often arose because responsibilities were not recorded clearly.

That part was reasonable.

It proposed a central index of shared-property agreements.

That was also potentially useful.

But it then suggested that regional offices should have authority to require estates to submit copies of their agreements and recommend revisions when necessary.

The final section described the northern inspection programme as evidence that estates were willing to cooperate with a centralized process.

Seraphina stopped reading.

"We never agreed to that."

"No, my lady."

"Did the other estates?"

"Not according to the regional office's records."

"Then the memorandum is misrepresenting the meeting."

The clerk nodded.

"It appears to have treated the exchange of a voluntary format as support for a wider administrative framework."

Cedric leaned forward.

"Who would benefit from such a system?"

The clerk looked uncomfortable.

"I cannot answer that."

Seraphina closed the memorandum.

"Neither can we."

She handed it back.

"Do not assign motives without evidence."

The clerk nodded.

"I understand."

He then produced another sheet.

"There is one more matter."

Seraphina looked at it.

"The Royal Agricultural Council has requested that the regional office confirm whether House Veyr would participate in a pilot programme."

"What pilot programme?"

"A voluntary submission of selected shared-property records to a central index."

Seraphina stared at him.

"Has the programme been approved?"

"No."

"Has the council established rules for access?"

"No."

"Has it identified who will maintain the records?"

"Not yet."

"Then what are they asking us to pilot?"

The clerk did not answer.

Cedric looked down.

Seraphina folded her hands.

"Please tell Director Fenwick that House Veyr is willing to discuss recordkeeping standards, but it cannot submit private agreements to an undefined system."

"I will convey that."

"And please ask the council to distinguish between the regional inspection format and its proposed centralized framework."

The clerk nodded.

"I believe that would be helpful."

He gathered his papers.

Before leaving, he hesitated.

"My lady, there is something else."

Seraphina looked up.

"The preliminary report has already been circulated to several noble households in the capital."

She waited.

"Some have expressed concern that the proposal is intended to weaken private estate authority."

"Has anyone formally objected?"

"Not yet."

"Then the council should explain the proposal before people begin arguing about it."

The clerk bowed.

"Yes, my lady."

After he left, Cedric remained silent.

Seraphina looked at him.

"Well?"

He exhaled.

"We have a problem."

"We have several."

"Do you think the memorandum was deliberately written to make the northern estates appear supportive?"

"Possibly."

"Or was it simply careless?"

"Possibly."

"Do you think the newspaper articles were intended to prepare people for the proposal?"

Seraphina looked at the memorandum.

"I think the articles made the proposal easier to misunderstand."

"That is not quite an answer."

"It is the only answer we can support."

Cedric nodded.

She continued.

"We should preserve the memorandum, the receiving record, and the preliminary report. We should also obtain the original meeting notes and the regional office's clarification."

"And the newspaper articles?"

"Keep them."

"All of them?"

"Yes."

Cedric looked mildly disappointed.

He had apparently hoped that the newspapers could be thrown away.

Seraphina understood.

She did not enjoy keeping them either.

That evening, the matter reached the Duke.

Alaric listened as Seraphina explained the memorandum.

He read the document himself.

Then he looked at the final paragraph.

"They are using your work as an example of cooperation."

"They are using it as evidence of support for a proposal we have not endorsed."

Alaric placed the paper on the table.

"That is a significant difference."

"Yes."

"Would you support a central index of shared-property agreements?"

Seraphina considered the question.

"Possibly, if it were voluntary, secure, and properly maintained."

"And compulsory submission?"

"I would need to know who could access the records and what authority they would have."

Alaric nodded.

"Good."

He turned toward Evelyne.

"What do you think?"

Evelyne had been listening quietly.

"I think the council has a legitimate administrative problem and an incomplete proposal."

"Explain."

"Old agreements are difficult to locate. Estates may lose records. Disputes arise when responsibilities are unclear. A central index could help."

"Provided the records are protected," Seraphina added.

"Exactly. But the proposal does not explain how private information would be handled. It also assumes that a regional office can judge whether an agreement should be revised."

Alaric leaned back.

"Then we should not reject the entire idea."

"No," Evelyne said. "We should ask for a proper proposal."

Seraphina nodded.

The conversation was different from the earlier discussions about the newspaper.

The articles were irritating.

The proposal was important.

It could affect landowners across the kingdom.

It could help preserve records.

It could also create a system that gave officials access to private agreements without clear limits.

That deserved careful examination.

Alaric looked at his daughter.

"Will you attend the council meeting?"

"I will, if they provide a revised agenda."

"Why?"

"Because they are discussing a system that may affect the estates. I want to understand it before deciding whether to support it."

"That is a reasonable reason."

Seraphina paused.

"But I will not lead the proposal."

Alaric smiled.

"I did not expect you to."

Evelyne looked at the preliminary report.

"Someone may expect you to."

Seraphina sighed.

That was the trouble with expectations.

They rarely asked permission before arriving.

The next morning, a letter arrived from the Royal Agricultural Council.

It contained a revised agenda.

The council had added a section on data access, confidentiality, and the legal limits of regional authority.

It had also removed the phrase *the northern approach* from the proposed framework.

Instead, it referred to the Veyr register as one example among several existing estate recordkeeping practices.

Seraphina read the changes.

They were improvements.

At the bottom of the letter was a handwritten note from the council secretary.

*The council wishes to clarify that no participating estate is considered to have endorsed a centralized system at this stage.*

Seraphina handed it to Cedric.

"Better."

"Yes."

He glanced at the list of participants.

"Edmund Vale is still attending."

"Then we will hear what he has to say."

"Do you trust him?"

"I do not know him."

Cedric nodded.

"Fair enough."

Seraphina placed the letter in the correspondence folder.

The meeting would take place in the capital the following week.

She would attend.

She would ask questions.

She would listen.

And she would make no promises on behalf of people who had not authorized her to speak for them.

That evening, she went to the library.

A stack of old estate agreements waited on the table.

She had asked Cedric to retrieve several examples of the original records used in the regional register.

She wanted to check whether the proposed central index could preserve the difference between an agreement's original terms and its later amendments.

The first document concerned the shared mill.

The second concerned the bridge.

The third concerned the orchard watercourse.

Each had a different history.

Each had different parties.

Each contained obligations that had changed over time.

A central index could help locate them.

It could not replace the documents themselves.

Seraphina wrote that down.

Then she heard a knock.

Mira entered.

"My lady, a messenger has arrived from the capital."

"Another letter?"

"Yes."

Mira handed it to her.

The envelope was not from the Royal Agricultural Council.

It was from a newspaper.

Seraphina opened it.

Inside was a single printed sheet.

It contained a short notice announcing that the *Aurelion Morning Chronicle* would publish an investigative series on the northern estate reforms.

The series would examine the role of House Veyr, the regional agricultural office, and the proposed central property-record system.

At the bottom was a line.

*Readers may expect further revelations concerning the private interests behind the new northern administrative movement.*

Seraphina read it twice.

There was no evidence.

No named source.

No specific allegation.

Only the promise of revelations.

She placed the notice on the table.

Mira looked at it.

"Is it bad, my lady?"

Seraphina considered the wording.

"It may be."

"Should I inform the Duke?"

"Yes."

Mira took the paper.

Then Seraphina stopped her.

"Wait."

Mira turned.

"Please make a copy first. Keep the original with the other newspaper articles."

"Of course."

Seraphina looked at the stack of estate agreements.

The kingdom was beginning to discuss a system that might affect every noble family.

Some people were interested in improving it.

Some were afraid of it.

Some were already arguing about what it would become.

And someone had decided that the best way to explain the matter was to promise revelations about private interests.

She picked up her pen.

The next week was going to be unpleasant.

She hoped, at the very least, that the council meeting would have decent chairs.
