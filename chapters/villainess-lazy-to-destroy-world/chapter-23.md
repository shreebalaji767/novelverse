# Chapter 23 — The Invitation That Could Not Be Ignored

Seraphina had always believed that an invitation was a request.

A request could be accepted.

A request could be declined.

A request could be misplaced beneath a stack of other requests until the person who sent it became too embarrassed to ask again.

Unfortunately, royal invitations did not appear to follow these principles.

She sat in the Veyr residence in Aurelion, looking at the thick cream-coloured envelope on the table.

The royal crest had been stamped into the red wax. The paper was expensive enough that Seraphina suspected it had been manufactured by someone who had never experienced the indignity of writing on an ordinary sheet.

Mira stood beside the tea service.

"Would you like me to put it away, my lady?"

"Can you?"

"Of course."

"Somewhere nobody can find it?"

Mira considered this.

"That would be difficult. It has the royal crest."

"Then somewhere I cannot find it."

"I believe that would be even more difficult."

Seraphina sighed.

The invitation was for the formal reception marking the opening of the new administrative season. It was to be held at the Royal Palace in three weeks, and it had been addressed to all three members of the immediate ducal family: Duke Alaric, Duchess Evelyne, and Lady Seraphina Veyr.

The wording was polite.

The paper was beautiful.

The implications were exhausting.

Her mother had not yet answered her earlier question.

Seraphina looked toward the window. Aurelion's streets were busy below, filled with carriages, merchants, delivery carts and people who appeared to have somewhere important to be.

She envied them.

They could presumably walk away from an invitation.

"Your mother is in the eastern sitting room," Mira said. "She finished her correspondence a moment ago."

"Is she in a good mood?"

"She was smiling."

"That doesn't answer my question."

"It is the most reliable answer I have."

Seraphina rose.

"Bring the tea."

"Of course, my lady."

"And the invitation."

Mira picked up the envelope.

"Would you prefer that I carry it?"

"No. I would prefer that it had never been delivered."

Mira followed her out.

Duchess Evelyne was examining a collection of letters when Seraphina entered.

She wore a pale blue dress with a high collar, her dark hair arranged in an elegant knot. A small silver brooch rested near her shoulder.

She looked composed, as always.

Seraphina had begun to suspect that her mother could receive news of a collapsing bridge, an unexpected royal decree and a kitchen fire within the same minute without allowing her expression to change.

"Seraphina," Evelyne greeted her. "Come in."

Seraphina sat opposite her.

Mira placed the tea on the table and withdrew after receiving a small nod from the duchess.

Evelyne glanced at the envelope in her daughter's hands.

"So," she said, "you have been studying the invitation."

"I have been considering whether it is possible to become temporarily unavailable."

"Temporarily?"

"Until the administrative season ends."

"That would be several months."

"An unfortunate commitment."

Evelyne smiled.

"Did you think refusing would be simple?"

"I thought it would involve a letter."

"It does."

"Excellent."

"And an explanation."

"Less excellent."

"And an understanding of what the refusal would communicate."

Seraphina placed the envelope on the table.

"There it is."

Evelyne set down her correspondence.

"Tell me what you believe attending would mean."

"That House Veyr acknowledges the opening of the administrative season."

"Good."

"That Father is willing to be seen in the capital."

"Also good."

"That I am willing to stand in a large room while people decide whether my dress, expression and breathing are appropriate."

"That is an unavoidable part of court life."

"Then I am not sure court life deserves my cooperation."

Evelyne laughed softly.

"Attending does not mean agreeing with every person present. Nor does it mean accepting every responsibility they might attempt to place upon you."

"People seem remarkably willing to interpret attendance as agreement."

"Some will. Others will interpret absence as disagreement. That is why we must decide what we want our actions to communicate."

Seraphina leaned back.

"Could we communicate that we are tired?"

"We could. The palace would interpret that as either a private family matter or an invitation to speculate about our health."

"Could we communicate that we are busy?"

"Then someone will ask what occupies us."

"Could we communicate that we have no interest in the capital?"

"That would be interpreted as a political statement."

Seraphina stared at her.

"Does every ordinary sentence become a political statement here?"

"Not every sentence."

"How many?"

"Enough that you should think before speaking."

Seraphina looked at the invitation again.

She was beginning to understand why her mother was so careful with words.

At court, silence could be interpreted.

A refusal could be interpreted.

Even a perfectly ordinary explanation could become an opportunity for someone else to construct a story.

"What would happen if we attended and left early?" Seraphina asked.

"Very little, provided we leave appropriately."

"Define appropriately."

"We arrive, greet the king and queen, acknowledge the crown prince, speak to the relevant members of the Royal Council, and remain long enough that our departure is not interpreted as an insult."

"How long?"

"That depends."

"Of course it does."

Evelyne ignored the remark.

"We need not attend every conversation. We need not accept every invitation to dine. We need not allow anyone to keep us occupied beyond the necessary courtesies."

Seraphina considered this.

"So we can attend without becoming available."

"Precisely."

"That sounds useful."

"It is a skill."

"Is it taught?"

"Not formally. Most people learn after making a mistake."

Seraphina glanced at her mother.

"And how many mistakes did you make?"

Evelyne's smile became slightly more thoughtful.

"Enough."

For a moment, Seraphina saw something behind her mother's composure.

Not embarrassment.

Experience.

She had never seriously considered how much work it must take to maintain a position in a court where every gesture could be examined. Evelyne had spent years navigating those expectations, not because she enjoyed every part of them, but because the duchy's interests required it.

Seraphina looked down.

"I don't want to make things more difficult for you or Father."

"Then learn what your presence means before deciding whether to give it."

"That sounds like another responsibility."

"It is."

"Mother."

"Yes?"

"I was hoping you would say I could stay home."

"I know."

Evelyne reached for her teacup.

"But I will not ask you to attend merely because it is expected. I will ask you to understand the consequences of either choice."

That was less comforting than Seraphina had hoped.

It was also fair.

The following morning, Seraphina received an unexpected visitor.

Crown Prince Theodore had sent a brief message requesting a private conversation before her departure from the capital.

The message did not mention the reception.

Seraphina suspected this was deliberate.

She found him in a smaller audience chamber at the palace, accompanied by one secretary and a guard stationed outside the doors.

Theodore was examining a document when she entered.

"Lady Veyr."

"Your Highness."

"Thank you for coming."

"I was told the meeting would be brief."

"It should be."

"That is reassuring."

Theodore gestured toward a chair.

Seraphina sat.

He placed a document on the table between them.

"The Royal Academy has revised its regional inspection proposal."

"Already?"

"Your objections were not unreasonable."

"That must have been disappointing."

Theodore looked at her.

"Why?"

"Because unreasonable objections are easier to dismiss."

The secretary lowered his eyes, though Seraphina suspected he was hiding a smile.

Theodore unfolded the document.

"The revised proposal separates preliminary inspections from repair authorizations. It also requires each region to identify the authority responsible for granting access to individual sites."

"That is an improvement."

"Ownership and maintenance responsibility will be recorded separately."

"Another improvement."

"The Academy will provide technical personnel. Regional authorities will identify local access requirements and existing maintenance records."

Seraphina examined the first page.

There were several sections describing the proposed inspection process, the limits of Academy authority, and the conditions under which further investigation would be required.

It was considerably clearer than the earlier draft.

"Who reviewed this?"

"The Academy's legal adviser, two Treasury officials, and representatives from the regional administration."

"Did they agree?"

"Not entirely."

"Good."

Theodore raised an eyebrow.

"Good?"

"If everyone agrees immediately, I become suspicious."

"That is an unfortunate habit."

"It is a useful one."

He turned another page.

"The northern conduit inspection remains separate from the national proposal. The Academy will send a technical team to Veyr once the local access arrangements are confirmed."

"That is acceptable."

"Your father has not yet approved the wider regional partnership."

"He should not."

"Why?"

"Because the revised proposal is clearer, but clarity is not the same as necessity. House Veyr should know what it is being asked to provide before agreeing to anything."

Theodore nodded.

"I expected that answer."

"Then why ask?"

"Because I wanted to know whether your position had changed."

"It has not."

"Very well."

He set the document aside.

"There is another matter."

Seraphina looked at him.

"The reception?"

"The invitation has reached your family."

"It has."

"Will you attend?"

"That depends."

"On?"

"Whether I can find a convincing reason not to."

Theodore's expression remained serious.

"You understand that your absence may be noticed."

"I have been informed."

"By your mother?"

"She explained it more elegantly."

"Then she has explained it correctly."

Seraphina folded her hands.

"Your Highness, may I ask something?"

"Of course."

"Is the reception intended to introduce the administrative season, or to measure which families are willing to support the Crown's priorities?"

Theodore did not answer immediately.

The secretary looked up.

Seraphina waited.

"Both," Theodore said at last. "Though the official purpose is the former."

"Thank you for being honest."

"Some families will use the gathering to signal their support for particular policies. Others will use it to demonstrate their distance from the Crown. Some will simply attend because they have attended every year."

"And what does the Crown want from House Veyr?"

"Your father's continued cooperation on northern infrastructure and agricultural matters."

"That sounds like a reasonable request."

"It is."

"Then ask him directly."

Theodore regarded her for a moment.

"Would that be sufficient?"

"It would be clearer than hoping a room full of people will interpret our presence correctly."

The prince leaned back.

"Your family has always maintained a careful relationship with the Crown."

"Father has."

"And you?"

"I have maintained a careful relationship with my bedroom."

Theodore's secretary coughed.

The prince's expression changed only slightly.

"That is not a political position."

"I am relieved."

"But it may become one."

Seraphina did not answer.

Theodore continued.

"Your recent work has attracted attention. Some of it is justified. Some of it is not. You have questioned old regional records, procurement procedures and the Academy's inspection arrangements. People may begin to assume you have an interest in broader administrative reform."

"I have an interest in not having a warehouse collapse on someone."

"That is a reasonable interest."

"It should be a common one."

"It should."

For a moment, the room was quiet.

Then Theodore said, "I will not ask you to advise the Royal Council."

"Thank you."

"But I would appreciate your family's continued cooperation where the northern region is concerned."

"That is a conversation for my father."

"I know."

Seraphina stood.

"Then I believe we have finished."

Theodore nodded.

"One final thing, Lady Veyr."

She paused.

"The reception is not a test you must pass. Attend if your family considers attendance appropriate. Decline if there is a substantial reason. Do not allow other people's expectations to become your only consideration."

Seraphina looked at him.

It was unexpectedly sensible advice.

"Your Highness, do you give that advice to everyone?"

"No."

"Why not?"

"Most people do not ask the questions you ask."

Seraphina decided not to investigate that remark.

She bowed and left.

On the journey back to the Veyr residence, Mira watched Seraphina through the carriage window's reflection.

"Was the meeting difficult, my lady?"

"No."

"Did the prince ask you to do something?"

"No."

"Did you refuse?"

"No."

Mira looked surprised.

"Then it must have been a very successful meeting."

"That is a dangerous conclusion."

"What happened?"

"The Academy has improved its proposal. The Crown Prince wants Father's cooperation. I was reminded that attending a reception does not mean accepting every responsibility offered there."

"That sounds useful."

"It is."

Mira hesitated.

"Will you attend?"

"I haven't decided."

"Would you like to?"

Seraphina looked outside.

A pair of children were trying to move a wooden cart over a broken section of pavement. One pulled from the front while the other pushed from behind. The cart barely moved.

A shopkeeper eventually came out and helped them lift its wheel over the damaged stones.

The children thanked him and continued on their way.

Seraphina watched until they disappeared around the corner.

"I don't dislike the people," she said. "I dislike the expectations."

"Then perhaps you should attend only to see the people you want to see."

"That is an irritatingly reasonable suggestion."

Mira smiled.

"I learned from you."

"Then I apologize."

The Veyr family returned north two days later.

Duke Alaric was waiting in his study when Seraphina arrived.

Cedric stood near the desk, holding a folder.

The sight of them together made Seraphina pause.

"Am I interrupting something?"

"No," Alaric said. "Come in."

Cedric closed the folder.

"I was just giving your father the latest reports from the regional offices."

Seraphina glanced at the documents.

"Anything urgent?"

"Nothing requiring immediate action," Cedric replied. "Westmere's irrigation repair has begun. The storage roof supports have been replaced, and the remaining work is scheduled for next week."

"Good."

"The northern road inspection is also underway. General Cassian sent a preliminary report this morning."

"Any problems?"

"Several sections need attention, but none are currently impassable."

Alaric looked at her.

"How was the capital?"

"Expensive."

"That is not a report."

"It is the most accurate summary."

Cedric smiled faintly.

Alaric gestured toward a chair.

"Your mother told me about the reception."

Seraphina sat.

"Then you know I am considering refusing."

"I know."

"Would that cause trouble?"

"It could cause speculation."

"Would attending prevent it?"

"No."

"Then what is the point?"

Alaric considered the question.

"Attendance allows us to show that House Veyr remains engaged with the Crown. It does not prevent speculation, but it gives us control over part of the message."

Seraphina looked between her parents' empty chairs.

"Mother said something similar."

"Your mother is usually correct about these matters."

"Usually?"

"Occasionally she is correct in ways that are inconvenient."

Seraphina nodded.

"That sounds familiar."

Alaric's expression softened.

"I intend to attend. Your mother will accompany me. Whether you come is your decision."

Seraphina studied him.

"Would you prefer that I attend?"

"I would prefer that you understand what you are agreeing to."

"That seems to be the family position."

"It is a sensible one."

She looked at Cedric.

"What do you think?"

Cedric was briefly caught off guard.

"About the reception?"

"Yes."

"I think your presence would attract attention."

"That is not an answer."

"It is the answer I have."

Alaric glanced at him.

Cedric continued.

"You have been discussed by several people in the capital. Some are curious about your recent work. Others are interested in whether your behaviour represents a change in House Veyr's position."

Seraphina closed her eyes.

"Wonderful."

"However," Cedric added, "your absence would not make those discussions disappear. It might give them something new to discuss."

"That is even more wonderful."

Alaric leaned forward.

"Seraphina, you cannot control what people say. You can only decide what actions are worth taking."

She opened her eyes.

"Then I will attend."

Cedric looked surprised.

Alaric did not.

"Are you certain?"

"No. But I understand the reason."

"That is sufficient."

"On one condition."

Alaric raised an eyebrow.

"What condition?"

"I will not accept any new responsibilities at the reception."

"That is reasonable."

"I will not agree to fund an Academy project."

"Agreed."

"I will not promise access to private Veyr land without a proper request."

"Agreed."

"I will not be placed on a committee."

Alaric looked at Cedric.

Cedric looked at the ceiling.

"Agreed," the duke said.

Seraphina paused.

"And if someone asks me to inspect a magical conduit?"

"Refer them to the Academy's technical office."

"Excellent."

Alaric smiled.

"You have become very good at refusing things."

"I have been practising."

That evening, Seraphina found her mother in the family sitting room.

Evelyne was reading a letter from the southern provinces. Several pages lay beside her, covered in notes.

Seraphina sat across from her.

"I have decided to attend the reception."

Evelyne lowered the letter.

"Your father told me."

"Then I have nothing to report."

"You can still tell me why."

Seraphina thought about it.

"Because refusing would communicate something I don't intend to communicate."

"And attending?"

"That House Veyr is willing to maintain a relationship with the Crown. Nothing more."

Evelyne nodded.

"Good."

"I have also decided not to accept any new responsibilities."

"That is sensible."

"And I will leave if the evening becomes unpleasant."

"Within reason."

"What does that mean?"

"It means you cannot leave in the middle of the king's speech because someone has asked an irritating question."

"That seems unnecessarily restrictive."

"It is."

Seraphina sighed.

Evelyne placed her letter aside.

"There is something else you should understand about these gatherings."

Seraphina waited.

"People will approach you for different reasons. Some will genuinely want to speak. Some will be curious. Some will want information. Others will offer compliments because they hope you will feel obliged to return them."

"That sounds exhausting."

"It can be."

"What should I do?"

"Listen. Answer what you wish to answer. Decline what you do not wish to accept. And remember that politeness does not require surrendering your judgement."

Seraphina looked at her mother.

"How do you do it?"

Evelyne smiled.

"By remembering that I am not responsible for making everyone happy."

Seraphina considered this.

It was perhaps the most useful thing she had heard all day.

The next morning, a letter arrived from the Royal Academy.

It was addressed to Duke Alaric, with a separate copy for Seraphina.

The Academy confirmed that its revised regional inspection proposal had been submitted to the Crown's administrative office. The technical team assigned to the western conduit would begin preparations for the northern visit once the land-access documents were completed.

There was also a short note from Lucien Arkwright.

He had contacted the retired engineer who supervised the old junction modification twenty-two years earlier.

Master Oren Whitlock had agreed to review his surviving records.

However, he had made one unusual request.

He wanted to see the original maintenance marker before discussing the modification.

Seraphina read the sentence twice.

"Why would he need the marker?" Mira asked.

"Perhaps it contains information that isn't in the records."

"Can it?"

"I don't know."

Seraphina folded the letter.

The missing marker had not been destroyed. Lucien had described the bolts as deliberately removed, but the object itself had not yet been located.

It might have been taken for repair.

It might have been moved during later construction.

Or someone might have wanted it removed.

There was no reason to decide before they had evidence.

Seraphina placed the letter on her desk.

"Write to the Academy. Tell them the marker has not been recovered and that we cannot promise to produce it."

"Yes, my lady."

"Also tell them Master Whitlock may inspect the relevant site after the proper arrangements are made."

Mira nodded.

"Anything else?"

Seraphina looked at the royal invitation, which had been placed beside the Academy correspondence.

For the first time, the two documents seemed connected.

The reception would bring together the people responsible for the kingdom's administration.

The Academy's inspection proposal would eventually require decisions from those same institutions.

She did not intend to become involved in those decisions.

But she could already imagine the conversations.

Someone would ask about the conduits.

Someone would ask about funding.

Someone would ask whether House Veyr supported the project.

And someone would almost certainly attempt to turn a simple answer into a promise.

Seraphina picked up her notebook.

She wrote:

*Royal reception.*

*Attend with Father and Mother.*

*Greet the necessary people.*

*Do not make promises.*

*Do not accept committees.*

*Do not discuss technical matters without the Academy's written reports.*

She paused.

Then she added:

*Leave before becoming unreasonable.*

Mira glanced over her shoulder.

"Is that an official rule, my lady?"

"It is a personal safety measure."

"For whom?"

"Everyone."

Mira accepted this explanation.

Seraphina closed the notebook.

Outside, the northern estate continued its ordinary business. Workers were repairing a roof in Westmere. Surveyors were inspecting a road. Roland was comparing the latest delivery records. Cedric was attempting to persuade three departments to submit their reports on time.

The Academy was preparing an inspection.

The Crown was preparing a reception.

And somewhere in the old archives, a retired engineer was searching for a record from twenty-two years ago.

None of these things required Seraphina to take over the kingdom.

That was important.

She intended to preserve that distinction.

Unfortunately, the distinction was becoming increasingly difficult to maintain.

Three days later, a messenger from the capital arrived with a sealed packet.

It contained the formal guest arrangements for the royal reception.

The Veyr family had been assigned a place among the senior regional houses, close enough to the royal family to be visible but not so close as to suggest special favour.

Evelyne examined the seating plan.

Alaric read the accompanying note.

Seraphina looked at the names printed beside hers.

One name caught her attention.

**Master Lucien Arkwright — Royal Academy.**

He had been assigned to the same reception.

Beside him was Professor Edmund Vale.

And two places farther along the table was Saintess Liora Everwyn.

Seraphina stared.

"Why are they attending?"

Evelyne glanced at the page.

"The Academy is presenting its administrative priorities for the coming season."

"Of course it is."

Alaric folded the note.

"Does that concern you?"

"No."

She looked at the seating plan again.

"Not yet."

Evelyne smiled.

"Then we shall prepare accordingly."

Seraphina slowly lowered the paper.

The reception was still three weeks away.

She had time to prepare.

She had time to arrange her clothes, review the guest list, and remind herself of every possible way to avoid an unwanted commitment.

She even had time to hope that the evening might pass without incident.

It was a modest hope.

A perfectly reasonable hope.

And, judging by the guest list, an increasingly unrealistic one.

**End of Chapter 23**
