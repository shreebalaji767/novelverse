# Chapter 65 — The Assembly That Had Already Chosen Its Sides

The Northern Noble Assembly had not yet begun.

Nevertheless, by the time Seraphina arrived at the Veyr administrative hall on Monday morning, three families had already expressed dissatisfaction with it.

One had withdrawn its representative.

Another had requested that the meeting be postponed.

The third had sent a letter asking whether House Veyr intended to use the gathering to promote its proposed administrative reforms.

Seraphina read the third letter twice.

Then she handed it to Cedric.

"Reply that House Veyr has no proposed administrative reforms."

Cedric nodded.

"Should I mention the regional register?"

"Yes. Explain that it is a recordkeeping format developed for our own estate agreements."

"And the central index?"

"That is a proposal under consideration by the Royal Agricultural Council. We are not responsible for it."

Cedric made a note.

Seraphina returned to the letter.

The writer was Lord Alistair Corven, whose estate had recently received a copy of the southern drainage agreement.

He had apparently interpreted the newspaper coverage as evidence that House Veyr was attempting to establish a regional standard for property access.

It was an incorrect conclusion.

But it was not entirely surprising.

The newspaper articles had repeatedly described the work as a movement, a reform programme, and a new approach to regional administration.

The actual work had been considerably less ambitious.

A bridge had needed repairs.

A mill had needed a schedule.

An orchard had needed water.

Seraphina had not expected these activities to become a political identity.

She was beginning to suspect that the kingdom had too few practical problems and too many people willing to invent larger ones.

Evelyne joined them shortly before midday.

She had been reviewing the invitations to the assembly.

"How many families have confirmed?"

"Seven," Cedric replied.

"How many have declined?"

"Two."

"And the remaining families?"

"Three have not responded."

Evelyne looked at Seraphina.

"Will you still attend?"

"Yes."

"Good."

Cedric placed the latest newspaper on the desk.

Evelyne read the headline.

"Have you seen this?"

"Yes."

"It says the northern nobles are divided."

"They may be."

"But the article was published before the meeting."

"Yes."

Evelyne placed it down.

"Then it is describing a division that may not exist."

Seraphina nodded.

The newspaper had quoted an unnamed landowner who supposedly believed that House Veyr was attempting to influence the regional estates.

It also quoted another anonymous source who claimed that the central index was designed to transfer private records to royal control.

Neither claim had been supported by a document.

The article then described the upcoming assembly as a confrontation between traditional landowners and a new administrative faction.

Seraphina found the phrase particularly irritating.

There was no faction.

There was no movement.

There was not even a formal proposal from House Veyr.

There were simply several estates trying to understand their old agreements.

Evelyne sat down.

"Do not let the article determine how you behave at the meeting."

"I won't."

"And do not feel obliged to defend yourself against every accusation."

"I know."

"Answer what is asked. Correct what is false. Do not argue about what people might be thinking."

Seraphina nodded.

Her mother smiled.

"That is all."

Then she looked at Cedric.

"Have you prepared the documents?"

"Yes."

"Bring the original register, the regional guidance document, and the copies of the agreements relevant to the discussion."

"Already arranged."

"Good."

Evelyne stood.

"Then perhaps this meeting will be useful after all."

Seraphina looked at her.

"How?"

"It may show us which concerns are genuine and which have been created by the newspapers."

Seraphina considered that.

It was a reasonable expectation.

She hoped it would prove correct.

The assembly was held at the old northern council residence.

It had once been used for meetings between the regional nobility and the ducal administration.

The building was smaller than the royal council chamber, but it had a large central hall and several private rooms.

Seraphina arrived with Cedric and Evelyne.

Alaric had chosen not to attend.

He had sent a formal letter stating that House Veyr would be represented by the Duchess, Lady Seraphina, and Cedric.

This was deliberate.

The Duke did not want the gathering to appear to be a confrontation between two noble houses.

He also had actual ducal business to attend.

Seraphina suspected that the second reason was more important.

At the entrance, a clerk checked their names.

Inside, the hall was already crowded.

Several noble families had sent their heirs or estate administrators.

There were also two legal advisers, a regional surveyor, and a representative from the agricultural office.

Seraphina recognized Lady Helena Ashcombe.

She greeted her.

"Lady Veyr."

"Lady Helena."

"I hope the journey was comfortable."

"It was."

Lady Helena glanced around the hall.

"Rather more people than I expected."

Seraphina looked at the gathering.

"Yes."

"Have you read the latest article?"

"Unfortunately."

Lady Helena smiled.

"Then perhaps we should be grateful that the room has chairs."

Seraphina almost laughed.

It was an improvement over the newspapers.

The assembly began with a statement from Lord Edmund Bellmont, who chaired the meeting.

He was a broad-shouldered man in his late fifties with a reputation for practical judgment.

He explained that the gathering had been arranged to discuss concerns surrounding the regional inspection programme and the proposed central property index.

He emphasized that the assembly had no authority to decide the council's policy.

Its purpose was to allow the northern estates to discuss the matter among themselves.

He then turned toward the participants.

"Before we begin, I would like to make one point clear. We are not here to accuse House Veyr of anything. We are here to understand what has happened and what may happen next."

Seraphina appreciated the opening.

It was more sensible than the newspaper headline.

Lord Bellmont continued.

"Several families have expressed concern about the possibility of centralized oversight. Others believe that better recordkeeping would benefit everyone. Both positions deserve consideration."

He gestured toward the first speaker.

Lord Corven stood.

He was a thin man with a carefully trimmed beard and a severe expression.

"I will speak plainly," he said. "I do not believe private estates should be required to submit their agreements to a royal office."

Several people nodded.

"I also do not believe that a regional administrative office should be permitted to revise private arrangements simply because it considers them outdated."

More nods followed.

"However, I understand that records are sometimes lost. I understand that disputes arise. I am not opposed to a voluntary index, provided it remains voluntary."

He sat down.

Seraphina wrote a note.

His concern was reasonable.

The next speaker was a landowner from the northern hills.

He supported the idea of a central index.

His estate had recently spent several months locating a forty-year-old grazing agreement.

The original document had been stored in a solicitor's office that no longer existed.

A later copy contained only part of the agreement.

He believed that a central index could have saved time.

He also supported a system for recording amendments.

Another landowner disagreed.

He feared that a central index might eventually become a database of private obligations that could be used to pressure estates.

The discussion became lively.

But it remained civil.

For nearly an hour, the assembly examined the practical concerns.

Seraphina listened.

Some arguments were well considered.

Others were based on assumptions.

One landowner believed the central index would allow the Crown to increase taxes.

A legal adviser explained that the proposal did not concern taxation.

Another believed that regional offices would be able to demand access to private roads.

The agricultural representative clarified that no such authority had been proposed.

A third believed that the register might create a new system for settling boundary disputes.

The surveyor explained that it could not establish boundaries without proper surveys and legal records.

Seraphina began to understand something.

The newspapers had not created every concern.

They had given existing anxieties a common subject.

The estates were worried about central authority.

They were worried about taxation.

They were worried about losing control of private land.

The proposed index had become a convenient place to attach those fears.

After the first discussion, Lord Bellmont invited Seraphina to speak.

She stood.

Several people turned toward her.

She disliked that part.

Not the speaking itself.

The expectation that the words would carry more significance than they deserved.

She looked at the assembly.

"I would like to clarify what House Veyr has done."

She opened the document case.

"Several years ago, our estate records contained agreements that were difficult to understand and, in some cases, incomplete. We began reviewing them because they affected the ordinary management of our properties."

She placed a copy of the register on the table.

"We recorded the ownership, access rights, maintenance responsibilities, and current condition of each property. We also preserved the original agreements and noted where later amendments existed."

She turned a page.

"We did not create a new regional authority. We did not request that neighboring estates submit their records. We did not change their property rights."

She paused.

"The regional office later asked to examine the format. It decided to share a voluntary version with other estates."

She placed the register down.

"That is the extent of House Veyr's involvement."

Lord Bellmont nodded.

"Thank you."

Lord Corven raised his hand.

"May I ask a question?"

"Of course," Lord Bellmont replied.

Corven turned toward Seraphina.

"Why did House Veyr review so many agreements at once?"

"Because the first review revealed several other agreements that needed attention."

"Did you expect neighboring estates to follow?"

"No."

"Did you encourage them?"

"We shared the format when asked."

"Did you recommend that they revise their agreements?"

"No. We recommended that they examine their own records."

Corven nodded.

"That is a distinction I understand."

Another landowner spoke.

"Would you object if the regional office maintained a central index of the agreements?"

Seraphina considered the question.

"Not necessarily. But the index should record the existence and location of agreements, not replace them. It should distinguish between verified documents and uncertain references. It should also have clear rules about access, correction, and withdrawal."

The landowner nodded.

"And who should be allowed to access it?"

"That depends on what information it contains. A public index could identify the existence of a document without revealing its private financial terms."

The legal adviser added that different access levels could be established.

Seraphina sat down.

She had answered the questions.

That was enough.

The discussion took a different direction when Lord Corven raised the newspaper articles.

"Several reports have suggested that House Veyr intends to establish a new regional administrative system. Others have suggested that the proposed index is already being implemented."

He looked toward the assembly.

"Neither claim appears to be true."

A few people murmured.

Corven continued.

"Who is responsible for these reports?"

No one answered.

He turned toward the newspaper correspondent seated near the back of the hall.

Edmund Vale was not present.

The correspondent was a younger man named Thomas Greer from the *Aurelion Morning Chronicle*.

Greer stood.

"I can only speak for the articles published by our newspaper."

"Then please do so," Corven said.

Greer adjusted his collar.

"The newspaper reported on developments that were considered significant to the northern estates."

"Did you verify the claims?"

"Which claims?"

"The claim that estates had already begun submitting records."

Greer hesitated.

"That information came from a source familiar with the discussions."

"Did you verify it with the Royal Agricultural Council?"

"No."

"Did you verify it with the regional office?"

"No."

"Did you ask House Veyr?"

"No."

Corven looked at him.

"Then what exactly did you verify?"

The correspondent shifted.

"We verified that a proposal was under consideration."

"That is not the same claim."

"No."

The room had become very quiet.

Lord Bellmont intervened.

"We are not holding a hearing about the newspaper."

Corven nodded.

"Of course. I simply wanted the record to be clear."

The correspondent sat down.

Seraphina wrote a note.

The assembly had just discovered that the article's most important claim had not been checked.

It was not a conspiracy.

It was a failure of reporting.

That was serious enough.

During the next break, Lady Helena approached Seraphina.

"That was well handled."

"Thank you."

"Lord Corven seems less hostile than I expected."

"He had legitimate concerns."

"Yes. I think the newspaper made the situation worse."

Seraphina nodded.

Lady Helena glanced toward the hall.

"Do you think the council will proceed with the index?"

"Probably. But the rules need work."

"And House Veyr?"

"We will review the revised format when it is ready."

Lady Helena smiled.

"Then perhaps this will all settle down."

Seraphina looked toward the table.

A group of landowners had gathered around the legal adviser.

They were discussing access rights and record retention.

The conversation appeared productive.

"Perhaps," Seraphina said.

Lady Helena lowered her voice.

"There is something else."

Seraphina turned.

"What?"

"One of the northern families received a letter last week."

"From whom?"

"They would not say."

"What did it concern?"

"An old property agreement. The writer suggested that the family should be careful about sharing its records with the regional office."

Seraphina considered that.

"Was the letter threatening?"

"No. It simply warned them that old agreements could contain obligations they had forgotten."

"That is true."

"Yes. But the writer seemed to know the contents of the family's private records."

Seraphina looked at her.

"Did the family confirm that?"

"They said the letter mentioned a specific amendment."

"Was the amendment public?"

"I do not know."

Seraphina nodded slowly.

"That is worth examining."

Lady Helena continued.

"They did not want to raise it at the assembly. They were afraid it would create more rumors."

Seraphina understood.

If the letter was genuine, it could indicate that someone had access to private estate records.

If it was a misunderstanding, the family might be reacting to an ordinary administrative notice.

There was not enough information to conclude anything.

"Can you ask them whether they would be willing to show the letter to the regional office?" she said.

"I can ask."

"Do not send it to me. Let the appropriate office examine it."

Lady Helena nodded.

"Of course."

The bell rang.

The break was over.

The afternoon session focused on the newspaper coverage.

The assembly agreed that the regional office should issue a clear public statement describing the actual scope of the inspection programme.

It would also publish the proposed central index's status once the council had completed its review.

Several landowners wanted a formal objection to the newspaper articles.

Lord Bellmont advised against it.

"The assembly should not become a press tribunal," he said. "We can correct inaccurate information without claiming authority over the newspapers."

The legal adviser agreed.

A formal complaint could be made by any affected party.

But the assembly itself had no general power to compel a correction.

Seraphina supported the decision.

She did not want the meeting to become a dispute about journalism.

There were actual estate matters to discuss.

Lord Bellmont then proposed a practical resolution.

The assembly would recommend that participating estates:

1. Retain original agreements and amendments.
2. Record the source of every administrative summary.
3. Distinguish verified obligations from disputed or uncertain ones.
4. Avoid treating another estate's agreement as a general precedent.
5. Seek legal advice before changing existing rights or responsibilities.

The resolution was not compulsory.

It did not create a new authority.

It simply recorded the assembly's shared position.

The participants approved it.

Seraphina looked at the document.

It was useful.

It was also modest.

She preferred it that way.

The meeting ended shortly before sunset.

Several landowners approached Seraphina to ask questions about the Veyr register.

One wanted to know whether the format could record a shared road used by merchants.

Another asked how to handle an agreement with no surviving original.

A third asked whether a family could maintain a private index without sharing it with the regional office.

Seraphina answered where she could.

For questions involving legal interpretation, she directed them to the appropriate advisers.

She did not promise to examine anyone's records.

She did not accept responsibility for any neighboring estate.

It was a successful afternoon.

At least, it was successful by her standards.

Cedric joined her near the entrance.

"That went better than expected."

"Yes."

"Lord Corven has asked for a copy of the register."

"Send him the public format."

"He also asked whether we would share the bridge inspection report."

"That can be arranged."

Cedric smiled.

"You have become rather popular."

Seraphina looked at him.

"Do not say that."

He laughed.

They were preparing to leave when Lord Bellmont approached.

"Lady Veyr."

"Lord Bellmont."

"I wanted to thank you for attending."

"Thank you for arranging the meeting."

He nodded.

"There is one matter I would like to discuss privately."

Seraphina waited.

"The northern estates have been receiving unusual correspondence."

She glanced toward Cedric.

"Lady Helena mentioned one letter."

"Then you have heard something already."

"What sort of correspondence?"

"Letters concerning old agreements, private obligations, and possible administrative changes."

"Are they all from the same sender?"

"We do not know."

"Are they threatening?"

"Not directly."

Lord Bellmont paused.

"But they contain details that should not be widely known."

Seraphina thought about the private amendment Lady Helena had mentioned.

"Have the families checked whether the details are accurate?"

"Some have."

"And?"

"In two cases, the details were correct. In one, the writer referred to an amendment that had been replaced."

"That is interesting."

"Yes."

Lord Bellmont looked toward the hall.

"I do not want to create unnecessary alarm. But if someone is collecting information about old estate obligations, it would be useful to know why."

Seraphina nodded.

"Has the regional office been informed?"

"Not yet."

"Then I recommend that the families preserve the letters and have their own legal advisers examine the references."

Lord Bellmont agreed.

"And the council?"

"If the letters contain information obtained from official records, the council should be informed. But we should not assume that is where the information came from."

He smiled.

"You are careful."

"I prefer not to accuse people without knowing what happened."

"That is a useful habit."

He bowed.

"Good evening, Lady Veyr."

"Good evening."

The carriage journey home was quiet.

Cedric reviewed the assembly's resolution.

Seraphina watched the darkening fields.

The meeting had achieved something useful.

The estates had agreed on basic principles.

The regional office would issue a clarification.

The central index remained under review.

The newspapers had been challenged on their claims.

And several landowners had raised a new concern.

Letters about private obligations.

It was not necessarily connected to the newspaper articles.

It might be a separate matter.

But the timing was uncomfortable.

Cedric closed the document case.

"Do you think someone is trying to frighten the estates?"

"I don't know."

"Could it be a political campaign?"

"Possibly."

"Or someone trying to stop the central index?"

"Possibly."

Cedric looked at her.

"You have not given me much to work with."

"We do not have much to work with."

He sighed.

Seraphina continued.

"We should not assume the letters are part of the newspaper dispute. We should examine the letters themselves."

Cedric nodded.

"That is fair."

The next morning, a messenger arrived from the capital.

He carried a sealed envelope addressed to Duke Alaric Veyr.

Alaric opened it in his study.

Seraphina was present.

The letter came from a retired legal clerk named Master Percival Dunn.

He had previously assisted several northern families with the preservation of historical property documents.

He wrote that he had received a request to examine a collection of old estate agreements.

The request had not identified the person seeking the information.

It had included a list of document references.

Several belonged to estates in the northern region.

One reference belonged to House Veyr.

Seraphina leaned forward.

"What document?"

Alaric turned the page.

The reference concerned an old financial guarantee signed by House Veyr nearly forty years earlier.

It was not the same as the orchard, mill, bridge, or drainage agreements.

It was a separate document.

The letter explained that the guarantee had been recorded in an old estate ledger but that the original contract was not among the papers held by Master Dunn.

He had been asked whether he could locate it.

Seraphina read the reference.

Then she looked at her father.

"Do we know this guarantee?"

Alaric's expression had changed.

"Not from memory."

"Could it be an old estate obligation?"

"Possibly."

"Should we look for the original?"

"Yes."

He folded the letter.

"Send a reply to Master Dunn. Ask him who made the request and whether he has a copy of the reference list."

Seraphina nodded.

The newspaper dispute had begun with old agreements and private records.

Now someone was asking about a financial guarantee House Veyr had signed decades earlier.

It might be entirely legitimate.

It might concern an obligation that had long since expired.

But the timing was difficult to ignore.

Alaric looked at his daughter.

"Do you remember the old financial records you reviewed last year?"

"Some of them."

"Then we will begin with the estate archive."

Seraphina looked at the reference again.

For the first time in several weeks, she felt that the next problem might not be about what people believed House Veyr had done.

It might be about what House Veyr had actually promised.

And promises, unlike newspaper headlines, could remain in force long after everyone had forgotten making them.
