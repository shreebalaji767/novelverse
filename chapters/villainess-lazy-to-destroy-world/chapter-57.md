# Chapter 57 — The Apples That Had Gone to the Wrong Account

The apple discrepancy was resolved in less than an hour.

Seraphina considered this a promising development.

It was followed by a much less promising discovery.

The kitchen had not been the only department recording transfers incorrectly.

The infirmary had received forty baskets of apples, thirty-six of which had been used. Four had spoiled.

The kitchen recorded the entire delivery as a transfer.

The storehouse recorded it as a loss.

The infirmary recorded thirty-six baskets as consumed and four as spoiled.

All three records were accurate.

They simply did not describe the same stage of the transaction.

Seraphina had barely finished correcting the entries when Thomas Hale returned.

He carried another ledger.

"My lady, I believe there may be a similar problem with the winter vegetables."

Seraphina looked at the ledger.

"How similar?"

"Not exactly the same."

"That is reassuring."

"The storehouse records thirty-two baskets of turnips as transferred to the kitchen. The kitchen records twenty-eight received."

"Where are the other four?"

Hale opened the ledger.

"That is what I am trying to establish."

Seraphina closed her eyes.

She had hoped the apples were an isolated inconvenience.

The estate, apparently, had other plans.

"Who signed the transfer?"

"One of the storehouse assistants."

"Who received it?"

"The kitchen porter."

"Did either count the baskets?"

"The assistant recorded thirty-two. The porter recorded twenty-eight."

"Then find them."

Hale nodded.

Seraphina stopped him.

"Not by accusing someone of taking them. Check the route, the delivery time, and whether the baskets were divided before they reached the kitchen."

"Yes, my lady."

He left.

Seraphina stared at the ledger.

Four baskets of turnips.

It was not a matter that would threaten the estate.

It was also not a matter she wanted to become a recurring mystery.

The investigation took most of the morning.

The storehouse assistant had counted thirty-two baskets before loading them onto a handcart.

The kitchen porter had received twenty-eight.

The remaining four had been placed beside the infirmary entrance.

They had not been delivered to the infirmary.

They had been left there because the porter had been told that the infirmary required additional vegetables for its winter soup.

The instruction had come from a kitchen supervisor.

The infirmary had not requested them.

The kitchen supervisor had assumed that the infirmary had requested them.

The storehouse assistant had assumed that the kitchen porter knew about the division.

The porter had assumed that someone else had already recorded it.

The four baskets had spent two hours beside the infirmary entrance before being carried back to the kitchen store.

No vegetables were missing.

No one had stolen anything.

The baskets had simply completed an unnecessary journey.

Seraphina listened to the explanation.

Then she looked at Thomas Hale.

"Why did the kitchen supervisor believe the infirmary needed them?"

"He had heard that their vegetable stores were low."

"From whom?"

"One of the servants."

"Did anyone ask the infirmary?"

"No."

"Then we have a rumor that became a delivery."

Hale nodded.

"Yes, my lady."

Seraphina leaned back.

"At least the turnips returned safely."

Hale looked as though he was uncertain whether this was a joke.

It was.

A small one.

She continued.

"Ask the infirmary supervisor what supplies they actually need. Then establish one procedure for requests. No more sending food because someone heard that someone else might need it."

"Understood."

"And please tell the kitchen that the infirmary is not a convenient place to leave vegetables."

"I will."

Mistress Eleanor arrived that afternoon.

She was responsible for the estate infirmary and had little patience for administrative confusion.

She examined the vegetable records and confirmed that the infirmary had sufficient supplies.

"We did not request turnips," she said.

"I gathered that."

"We would have accepted them if they had been delivered."

"Would you have needed them?"

"Not particularly."

Seraphina nodded.

Eleanor placed the ledger on the table.

"This is not the first time someone has tried to solve our supply problems without asking us."

"Does it happen often?"

"Often enough. Someone hears that we are short of something. They send whatever they think we need. Sometimes it is useful. Sometimes it is unsuitable. Once, we received a large quantity of dried beans when we had no storage space left."

"What did you do?"

"We distributed them to the workers."

Seraphina considered that.

"At least the beans were useful."

"They were useful after someone spent two days finding containers."

Eleanor looked at the apple records.

"I would prefer to request what we need."

"Then we should establish a regular request schedule."

"That would help."

"Would you like weekly requests or monthly?"

"Weekly for perishable supplies. Monthly for ordinary provisions. Emergency requests whenever necessary."

Seraphina nodded.

"That seems reasonable."

Eleanor added another condition.

"And please make sure the kitchen knows that an emergency request does not mean they can send whatever happens to be available."

"I shall make that clear."

Eleanor smiled.

"Thank you."

When she left, Seraphina added another line to the estate's supply procedure.

The infirmary would request supplies.

The kitchen would confirm availability.

The storehouse would record the quantity dispatched.

The receiving department would confirm the quantity received.

Spoilage, shortages, and substitutions would be recorded separately.

It was not complicated.

It merely required everyone to understand which part of the process they were responsible for.

The new procedure was tested the following week.

It worked.

The infirmary requested twelve baskets of potatoes, six baskets of carrots, and four baskets of onions.

The kitchen confirmed the quantities.

The storehouse dispatched them.

The infirmary received them.

All three records matched.

Seraphina examined the completed form.

"Good."

Thomas Hale looked pleased.

"It was straightforward."

"Then why was it not done before?"

Hale thought for a moment.

"Because each department had its own way of recording deliveries."

Seraphina nodded.

"That is the problem with a system that works only when everyone remembers what everyone else means."

Hale looked at the form.

"Perhaps we should use the same one for other transfers."

"Perhaps."

He waited.

Seraphina looked at him.

"Do not make a new form for every department."

"I wasn't going to."

"Good."

She signed the procedure.

Then she sent it to the storehouse, kitchen, infirmary, and household office.

The matter was settled.

At least, she hoped it was.

Two days later, Cedric brought a letter from the regional agricultural office.

The office had received House Veyr's shared-property inspection format.

It had also received inquiries from three neighboring estates.

The office wanted to organize a small meeting to discuss how the format might be used across the district.

Seraphina read the letter.

Then she placed it on the table.

"No."

Cedric blinked.

"You haven't finished reading it."

"I have read enough."

"It is only a request for a meeting."

"Then you may attend."

"They specifically requested you."

"Then you may explain that I am unavailable."

Cedric looked at her.

"For how long?"

"Indefinitely."

He sighed.

Seraphina picked up the letter again.

The regional office was not asking her to supervise the estates.

It wanted her to explain how House Veyr had separated ownership, access, maintenance, current use, and inspection records.

That was reasonable.

It was also exactly the sort of reasonable request that could grow into a responsibility if she accepted it without boundaries.

She handed the letter back.

"Tell them we will provide the format and a written explanation. If they want to discuss it, you may attend as the estate administrator."

Cedric nodded.

"Should I mention that the system is still being tested?"

"Yes. It is not a universal solution. Different agreements have different legal conditions."

"And if they ask whether you will advise other estates?"

"Tell them I will not."

Cedric smiled.

"I thought so."

Seraphina returned to her work.

She had no intention of becoming the district's unofficial guardian of missing boundary stones.

The meeting took place without her.

Cedric returned with a report two days later.

The regional office had invited representatives from five estates.

Two had already begun reviewing their shared roads.

One had discovered that a drainage channel was being maintained by an estate that no longer owned the land it crossed.

Another had found that a pasture access agreement granted rights to a family that had ceased to exist.

The fifth had no major issues.

It simply wanted to update its records.

Seraphina read the report.

"Did they ask for anything else?"

"Copies of the inspection format. A list of recommended fields. And permission to adapt the procedure."

"That is fine."

"One estate asked whether we could inspect its bridge."

Seraphina looked up.

"Why?"

"Their bridge has a similar maintenance arrangement."

"Then they should hire a surveyor."

"They asked whether Master Armitage could assist."

Seraphina considered it.

Armitage was competent.

He was also employed by House Veyr.

"Can he take private work?"

"Yes, provided it does not interfere with his estate duties."

"Then ask him whether he is willing. The estate should pay his normal fee."

Cedric nodded.

"Anything else?"

"Did the regional office request that House Veyr take responsibility for maintaining the inspection register?"

"No."

"Good."

Cedric smiled.

"You are becoming suspicious of every invitation."

"I am becoming familiar with how invitations work."

He laughed.

The next problem arrived from the northern pasture.

It was not a document.

It was a cow.

The animal belonged to a tenant farmer named Eliza Ward and had wandered into the western orchard through a gap in the old fence.

It had eaten several young branches.

The damage was not severe.

The cow was unharmed.

The fence was not.

Seraphina received the report during breakfast.

She read it while Evelyne was discussing an upcoming visit from the neighboring estate.

"Is the animal still there?" Seraphina asked.

"No," Cedric replied. "It was returned to the pasture."

"Was the fence repaired?"

"Temporarily."

"Who owns the fence?"

"House Veyr maintains it."

"Then repair it properly."

Cedric nodded.

"Should the farmer pay for the damaged branches?"

Seraphina looked at him.

"How many?"

"Three young trees."

"Can they recover?"

"The gardener believes so."

"Then no."

Cedric paused.

"Even though the cow entered the orchard?"

"The fence was our responsibility. The animal wandered through a gap in it. Unless the farmer deliberately drove it into the orchard, I see no reason to charge her."

Cedric nodded.

"I agree."

Evelyne watched them.

"You have become remarkably forgiving toward livestock."

"I have become unwilling to charge people for failures caused by our own fences."

"An admirable distinction."

Seraphina returned to her breakfast.

The cow had been returned.

The fence would be repaired.

The trees would probably survive.

There was no need to make the incident larger than it was.

Later that afternoon, Seraphina visited the orchard.

The damaged branches were visible.

The gardener, Thomas Barlow, was examining them.

He had been tending the orchard for thirty-four years and treated its trees with a degree of respect that Seraphina suspected exceeded what he felt for most people.

"The branches should recover," he said.

"Will they bear fruit next year?"

"Possibly. The damage is not deep."

"Then we leave them."

Barlow nodded.

He showed her the gap in the fence.

The lower rail had rotted.

The post had shifted.

The cow had not forced its way through.

It had simply discovered an opening.

Seraphina looked along the fence.

"How many other sections are like this?"

Barlow frowned.

"Several."

"How many?"

"Six, perhaps."

"Have they been reported?"

"Some."

"Why not all?"

He hesitated.

"Because we usually repair them when they become a problem."

Seraphina looked at him.

"That is not a maintenance schedule."

"No, my lady."

She studied the fence.

The orchard had been in use for generations. Its boundaries were familiar. The gardeners knew where the posts stood, which sections needed attention, and which trees required pruning.

But familiarity was not the same as inspection.

"Can you prepare a list of the damaged sections?"

"Yes."

"Not just the ones that have already failed. Include the ones that are weakening."

Barlow nodded.

"Would you like the whole orchard inspected?"

"Yes. But do not stop ordinary work to do it. Arrange the inspection around your existing duties."

"Understood."

Seraphina looked at the young trees again.

The damage was small.

The cause was not.

A fence that failed in one place would eventually fail somewhere else.

It was easier to repair it before a cow discovered the next opening.

That evening, Alaric joined her in the orchard.

He had received the report from Barlow.

"Your mother says you have ordered a complete fence inspection."

"Only the orchard."

"That is sensible."

Seraphina looked at him.

"Do you disagree?"

"No. I was thinking that your grandfather would have preferred a fence repair to a discussion about fence responsibility."

"Would he have repaired it?"

"Immediately."

"Then why did he not inspect the entire fence?"

Alaric smiled faintly.

"Because he had a hundred other things to do."

Seraphina considered that.

"That is a poor excuse."

"It is a familiar one."

They walked slowly along the orchard path.

The evening air smelled of damp soil and fallen leaves.

Alaric stopped beside an old apple tree.

"Your grandfather knew this orchard well. He could tell which trees were failing by looking at their leaves. He remembered where the old watercourse ran. He knew which neighboring farmers had access to the lower pasture."

"And no one wrote it down?"

"Some things were written down. Others were simply known."

Seraphina looked toward the fence.

"Then when he died, the knowledge became uncertain."

"Not all at once. People remembered pieces. The gardeners remembered the trees. The steward remembered the agreements. The surveyor remembered the boundaries. No one remembered everything."

Seraphina thought about the old bridge.

The missing marker.

The mill schedule.

The storage building.

The orchard.

Each had once been managed by people who understood the arrangement.

Over time, the people changed.

The arrangement remained.

Alaric looked at her.

"You are trying to make sure the next generation does not have to guess."

"Yes."

"That is worthwhile."

Seraphina glanced at him.

"I am also trying to avoid being summoned every time a fence breaks."

Alaric laughed.

"That may be more difficult."

She sighed.

The estate was improving.

Unfortunately, the estate was also learning that she could be asked questions.

And once people discovered that someone was willing to answer them, they tended to ask more.

Seraphina decided that the next time a cow escaped, she would make certain the fence had been inspected first.

It was not a grand ambition.

It was a perfectly adequate one.
