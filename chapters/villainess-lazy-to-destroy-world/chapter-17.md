# Chapter 17 — The Reception That Refused to Remain Simple

Seraphina had prepared for the spring reception.

She had selected a dress, reviewed the guest list, memorized the names of the principal Academy representatives, and reminded herself several times that the evening was not a battlefield.

She had even eaten lunch.

This last achievement was particularly important.

Unfortunately, preparation could not account for the behaviour of other people.

At five o'clock, Margaret Ellison entered Seraphina's bedroom carrying a small velvet box.

“My lady, your final accessories.”

Seraphina looked up from her book.

“I thought we had finished.”

“We have.”

“Then why is there another box?”

“Because your mother selected an additional hair ornament.”

Seraphina closed her book.

“Does it serve a purpose?”

“It is decorative.”

“Then it is unnecessary.”

Margaret placed the box on the dressing table.

“Your mother specifically requested that you wear it.”

Seraphina looked at the box.

Then at Margaret.

“Is this a political instruction?”

“It is a mother's instruction.”

“Those are often more difficult to refuse.”

Margaret opened the box.

Inside lay a delicate silver ornament shaped like a cluster of small leaves. It was simple, elegant, and considerably less extravagant than Seraphina had feared.

She sighed.

“Fine.”

Mira, standing behind her, began arranging her hair.

“You look lovely, my lady.”

“I look expensive.”

“That is generally the intention of formal clothing.”

“I would prefer to look comfortable.”

“You can do both.”

Seraphina glanced at the dress.

The dark green fabric was embroidered with silver thread along the sleeves and waist. It was elegant without being excessively elaborate.

She had chosen it because it was comfortable enough to sit in for several hours.

That was the most important quality of formal clothing.

Not appearance.

Not status.

The ability to survive an evening without developing back pain.

“Do you think the reception will be crowded?” she asked.

Margaret answered before Mira could.

“Very.”

“Will there be enough chairs?”

“Yes.”

“Enough food?”

“Yes.”

“Enough servants?”

“Yes.”

“Enough quiet corners?”

Margaret paused.

“No.”

Seraphina closed her eyes.

“Then the evening is already a failure.”

The reception began at six.

The main hall had been transformed by candlelight and flowers. The musicians played near the eastern windows, while servants moved quietly between the guests with trays of drinks and small dishes.

Aristocrats arrived in carefully chosen clothing, accompanied by family members, attendants, and occasionally children who looked deeply unhappy about being required to wear formal shoes.

Seraphina stood beside her parents near the entrance.

She smiled.

She bowed.

She remembered names.

She did not accidentally insult anyone.

For the first twenty minutes, everything proceeded remarkably well.

Then Lady Amelia Rosenthal arrived with her nephew.

“Duchess Veyr,” Lady Amelia greeted warmly. “Your reception is splendid.”

Evelyne smiled.

“You are kind to say so.”

Lady Amelia turned toward Seraphina.

“And there is our young hostess.”

Seraphina bowed.

“Lady Amelia.”

“I hope you are prepared for a long evening.”

“I have been warned.”

Lady Amelia laughed.

Her nephew, a young man with a serious expression, bowed as well.

“This is my nephew, Lord Henry Rosenthal. He has recently returned from the eastern territories.”

“An honour to meet you,” Seraphina said.

“And you, Lady Veyr.”

Lady Amelia continued.

“Henry has been helping with our family's trade accounts. He has become rather interested in regional procurement.”

Seraphina immediately sensed danger.

“Have you?”

Henry nodded.

“There are several difficulties with grain transport in the eastern territories. Prices have increased, but the records do not always show where the additional cost originates.”

Seraphina kept her smile.

“That sounds complicated.”

“It is.”

Lady Amelia looked pleased.

“I thought you two might have something to discuss.”

Seraphina looked at her.

Then at Henry.

Then back at Lady Amelia.

“Perhaps another time.”

Lady Amelia's smile widened.

“Of course.”

She moved away with her nephew.

Seraphina watched them disappear into the crowd.

Mira, standing a short distance behind her, whispered, “You handled that well.”

“I did not say anything.”

“Exactly.”

The Academy delegation arrived shortly afterward.

Professor Edmund Vale entered first, accompanied by two senior scholars.

Lucien Arkwright followed.

He looked considerably more presentable than he had during his arrival the previous day, although his hair still refused to remain properly arranged.

Liora Everwyn came last.

She wore a simple white dress with gold embroidery around the collar. The small emblem of the Church rested at her shoulder.

Guests turned to look as she entered.

Not everyone bowed.

Some simply watched.

Others whispered.

The saintess had become a familiar figure in the capital, but her presence still attracted attention.

Seraphina noticed that Liora's expression remained composed.

She also noticed that the young woman immediately looked toward the servants carrying refreshments.

A moment later, Liora quietly helped an elderly guest whose cane had become caught beneath a chair.

No announcement.

No dramatic gesture.

She simply moved the chair, checked that the guest was comfortable, and returned to her conversation.

Seraphina found herself smiling.

Then she remembered that she was supposed to be greeting guests.

She stepped forward.

“Lady Everwyn.”

Liora turned.

“Lady Seraphina. Good evening.”

“I hope your journey here was comfortable.”

“It was, thank you. The roads were in better condition than I expected.”

Seraphina glanced at Lucien.

“That is encouraging.”

Lucien nodded.

“The western bridge inspection was completed last week.”

“Was the repair satisfactory?”

“The engineers believe so.”

“Believe?”

Lucien paused.

“We have not yet received the final report.”

Seraphina stared at him.

He seemed to realize what he had said.

“I will make sure it is obtained.”

“Thank you.”

Liora smiled.

“Do you often ask people for reports?”

“Only when they mention work that has supposedly been completed.”

Lucien looked thoughtful.

“That is reasonable.”

Seraphina suspected that she had just acquired another problem.

She decided not to pursue it.

The first hour passed pleasantly.

Seraphina spoke with several families, exchanged greetings with regional officials, and listened to a long explanation about a new textile pattern from Merrow.

She did not understand the textile pattern.

She pretended to understand the textile pattern.

It was one of the oldest aristocratic traditions.

At seven o'clock, the musicians began a new piece.

Duke Alaric was speaking with General Cassian near the western windows. Duchess Evelyne was discussing charitable arrangements with Lady Amelia and several other guests.

Seraphina found herself beside Liora.

“You seem more comfortable tonight,” Liora said.

Seraphina looked at her.

“Is that noticeable?”

“A little.”

“I was uncomfortable yesterday.”

“I thought you were simply tired.”

“I was also uncomfortable.”

Liora smiled.

“Why?”

Seraphina considered how much truth she could reasonably provide.

“I have never been particularly fond of large gatherings.”

“Neither have I.”

Seraphina looked surprised.

“You?”

Liora nodded.

“People tend to assume I enjoy being surrounded by strangers.”

“Because you are the saintess?”

“Yes.”

“That seems like a rather unfair assumption.”

“It can be.”

Liora looked around the hall.

“Still, gatherings like this are useful. People who would never visit the Church may speak more freely in a place like this.”

Seraphina followed her gaze.

An elderly baron was speaking with a merchant. Two regional administrators were discussing road repairs. A young woman from a southern family was introducing herself to one of the Academy scholars.

“Do you enjoy meeting people?” Seraphina asked.

“Sometimes. I like hearing what people are working on.”

“Even when they talk too much?”

Liora laughed.

“Especially when they talk too much. It gives me time to think.”

Seraphina decided she liked that answer.

The evening became more complicated when Professor Vale approached them.

“Lady Seraphina. Lady Everwyn.”

Both women turned.

“Professor,” Seraphina greeted.

“I hope you are enjoying the reception.”

“Very much.”

Professor Vale smiled.

“I was hoping to introduce you to one of our senior engineers.”

A man standing behind him stepped forward.

He was in his early fifties, with grey hair and a narrow face. His formal robes bore the insignia of the Royal Academy's engineering division.

“This is Master Alistair Fenwick,” Professor Vale said. “He supervises regional mana conduit inspections.”

Seraphina bowed.

“An honour.”

Master Fenwick returned the greeting.

“Lady Veyr. I understand you have been reviewing several regional maintenance records.”

“I have been reviewing some estate and agricultural records.”

“Indeed. Professor Vale mentioned your interest in inspection procedures.”

Seraphina looked at Professor Vale.

He appeared entirely innocent.

“I am interested in whether inspections are actually carried out.”

Master Fenwick nodded.

“A reasonable concern.”

“It seems to be a recurring problem.”

“Unfortunately.”

He glanced toward Lucien, who had joined them.

“Master Arkwright has informed me about the western conduit.”

Seraphina's shoulders tightened.

“Has he?”

“Yes. I understand you have permitted an inspection after the reception.”

“I have not yet granted formal permission.”

Lucien looked at her.

“You said we could inspect it after the reception.”

“I said the inspection could wait until after the reception.”

There was a pause.

Professor Vale looked between them.

Master Fenwick slowly nodded.

“An important distinction.”

Lucien rubbed his forehead.

“I see.”

Seraphina continued.

“I would like to know exactly what you intend to inspect, how long it will take, whether it will interfere with the irrigation system, and who will be responsible for restoring anything disturbed during the process.”

Master Fenwick's expression changed.

Not irritation.

Interest.

“Those are sensible questions. We can prepare a written inspection plan.”

“Good.”

“We will also identify the section of conduit before requesting access.”

“Better.”

Lucien looked as though he had just realized something.

“Perhaps we should have prepared the plan before approaching the ducal household.”

Seraphina smiled politely.

“That would have been helpful.”

Professor Vale laughed.

Master Fenwick did not.

He was already making notes.

A little later, Crown Prince Theodore Aurelian arrived.

The announcement caused a subtle shift in the room.

Conversations paused.

Several nobles turned.

Duke Alaric stepped forward to greet him.

“Your Highness.”

Theodore bowed.

“Your Grace. Thank you for inviting me.”

“It is an honour to receive you.”

The prince wore a formal black uniform with silver detailing. His expression was composed, his movements deliberate.

Seraphina remembered him.

The original novel had described him as intelligent, ambitious, and increasingly concerned with the kingdom's political instability.

He had also been one of the central figures in the original Seraphina's downfall.

She straightened.

Theodore greeted Duchess Evelyne, then turned toward her.

“Lady Seraphina.”

“Your Highness.”

“I hear you have been occupied with the northern agricultural administration.”

Seraphina briefly wondered who had been discussing her work.

“Only a little.”

“Your father tells me you have been reviewing the old reserve records.”

She looked at Alaric.

He was speaking with a guest and did not appear to notice her expression.

“Some of them,” she replied.

“Have you discovered anything significant?”

“Several incomplete records.”

Theodore's eyes narrowed slightly.

“An old emergency programme?”

“Yes.”

“I have heard of it. The northern winter emergency, twenty-six years ago.”

Seraphina nodded.

“We are trying to establish how the reserve transfers were recorded and whether the final distribution was reconciled.”

“Was it not?”

“We have not found the final confirmation.”

Theodore considered this.

“That is concerning.”

“It is incomplete. I would not call it concerning until we know more.”

The prince looked at her for a moment.

Then he nodded.

“An appropriate distinction.”

Seraphina was relieved.

She had expected a political interrogation.

Instead, he seemed genuinely interested in the administrative problem.

“Have you requested the royal archive records?” he asked.

“Yes. We are waiting for copies of the committee authority register and the original order records.”

“I can ask the archive to prioritize the request.”

Seraphina hesitated.

She did not want to owe the crown prince anything.

But the records were important.

“Thank you, Your Highness. That would be helpful.”

Theodore nodded.

“Then I shall see what can be done.”

He moved away to greet another guest.

Seraphina watched him go.

She had spoken to the crown prince.

Nothing terrible had happened.

No dramatic confrontation.

No romantic misunderstanding.

No sudden declaration of political allegiance.

He had simply offered to help locate old documents.

She considered this a successful evening.

The trouble began with a toast.

At half past eight, Duke Alaric raised his glass.

The musicians stopped.

The guests turned toward him.

“Friends and honoured guests,” he began, “House Veyr is pleased to welcome you this evening. The spring season reminds us that prosperity is not secured by any single family or institution. It is built through the work of many people, often beyond the attention of those who benefit from it.”

Seraphina listened.

Her father spoke warmly about cooperation between the regions, the Academy, the Church, and the Crown.

He thanked the guests for their attendance.

He made no grand promises.

He did not mention grain.

Seraphina was grateful.

Then he said:

“And I am particularly pleased that the younger members of our institutions have joined us. Their work will shape the kingdom's future.”

Several guests turned toward Liora, Theodore, and the Academy representatives.

Seraphina quietly lowered her glass.

She knew what was coming.

Not because of prophecy.

Because she had attended enough formal gatherings to recognize the pattern.

Duke Alaric continued.

“I hope that tonight's conversations will lead to lasting cooperation.”

Applause followed.

The musicians resumed.

And within minutes, Seraphina found herself standing between Crown Prince Theodore, Saintess Liora, and Lucien Arkwright.

She looked at the three of them.

Then at the room.

Then back at them.

This was not a coincidence.

This was a social arrangement.

Someone had deliberately placed them together.

She suspected her mother.

“Lady Seraphina,” Theodore said, “I understand the Academy has been discussing regional infrastructure with you.”

Seraphina looked at Lucien.

Lucien looked at Professor Vale.

Professor Vale looked at the ceiling.

“I have agreed to listen to their proposals,” Seraphina said carefully.

“An excellent beginning,” Theodore replied.

Liora smiled.

“Master Arkwright mentioned that you have been reviewing the old maintenance records.”

“Only the administrative side.”

“That is often where important problems begin,” Theodore said.

Seraphina looked at him.

“Do you believe the Academy's infrastructure records are unreliable?”

“I believe every institution should examine its records periodically.”

“That sounds diplomatic.”

“It is also true.”

Lucien stepped forward.

“The Academy has begun reviewing several older conduit networks. Some were constructed decades ago, and their maintenance histories are incomplete.”

“Why?”

“Because responsibility for them has changed several times. The Academy built some of them, regional offices maintain others, and certain sections are managed by local authorities.”

Seraphina nodded.

“So when something goes wrong, everyone assumes someone else is responsible.”

Lucien looked at her.

“That is one of the difficulties.”

“Then the first thing you need is a responsibility register.”

Master Fenwick, who had approached during the conversation, immediately nodded.

“That is precisely what we are trying to establish.”

Seraphina glanced at him.

“Good.”

Lucien continued.

“We also need to identify the sections that have not been inspected recently.”

“Do you know how many?”

“Not yet.”

Seraphina stared.

“Then how do you know where to begin?”

Lucien looked thoughtful.

“We begin with the oldest sections.”

“Why?”

“Because they are more likely to require maintenance.”

“Not necessarily. A newer conduit can be poorly maintained, and an older one can be well maintained.”

Master Fenwick nodded again.

“Correct.”

Lucien frowned.

“Then we need a complete inventory.”

Seraphina took a sip of her drink.

“Yes.”

He looked at her.

“Would House Veyr be willing to assist with the administrative records?”

She nearly swallowed incorrectly.

There it was.

The request.

The beginning of a new responsibility.

Seraphina looked at Theodore.

The prince appeared interested.

She looked at Liora.

The saintess looked sympathetic.

She looked at Master Fenwick.

He was already waiting for an answer.

Seraphina set down her glass.

“I can arrange for the Academy to submit a formal request to the ducal administration. The office can identify which records are held locally and which are held centrally.”

Lucien nodded.

“That would be helpful.”

“However, I cannot personally supervise the inventory.”

“Understood.”

“Nor can I certify the condition of any magical conduit.”

“Of course.”

“And I will not authorize repairs without a technical assessment.”

Master Fenwick smiled.

“That is exactly the distinction we need.”

Seraphina relaxed slightly.

Perhaps she had handled that well.

Then Theodore spoke.

“Would you be willing to attend a preliminary meeting with the Academy's administrative representatives next week?”

Seraphina stared at him.

The prince's expression remained perfectly calm.

Lucien looked hopeful.

Master Fenwick looked interested.

Liora looked as though she wanted to help but did not know how.

Seraphina took a breath.

“Could I receive the agenda first?”

Theodore smiled.

“Certainly.”

“Then I will consider it.”

“An excellent answer.”

She suspected he had expected nothing less.

The evening continued.

Seraphina spoke with several more guests and managed to avoid being assigned anything else.

She even enjoyed a small portion of the supper.

The food was excellent.

The musicians were good.

The reception hall was warm.

For a brief period, she forgot about the old reserve programme, the Academy's conduits, and the political significance of the people standing around her.

Then she noticed Liora near the entrance to the eastern gallery.

The saintess was speaking with a young servant.

The servant appeared anxious.

Seraphina approached.

“Is everything all right?”

The servant bowed quickly.

“My lady.”

Liora turned.

“Everything is fine. The young man was asking where he should deliver the remaining refreshments.”

Seraphina looked at the tray in his hands.

“Why is he worried?”

Liora smiled gently.

“He seems to have been told that the eastern gallery was closed.”

Seraphina looked toward the gallery.

It was open.

“Who told you that?” she asked.

The servant hesitated.

“A steward, my lady. He said the guests were not to enter.”

Seraphina frowned.

“Was the gallery reserved?”

“I do not know.”

Liora glanced at her.

“The guests near the entrance were looking for somewhere quieter.”

Seraphina understood.

The reception had become crowded, and the eastern gallery was usually used as a secondary sitting room.

She turned to the servant.

“Please ask Margaret whether the gallery can be opened. If it is already prepared, there is no reason to keep it empty.”

“Yes, my lady.”

The servant bowed and left.

Liora watched him go.

“You noticed quickly.”

“It's my family's house.”

“That is not what I meant.”

Seraphina looked at her.

Liora continued.

“You noticed that he was afraid to answer.”

Seraphina thought about it.

“He probably expected to be blamed for asking.”

“Perhaps.”

Seraphina glanced toward the main hall.

The evening had been full of people speaking about cooperation, institutions, and the kingdom's future.

Yet a servant had been too afraid to ask whether a room could be opened.

It was a small thing.

But small things were often where problems became visible.

She turned toward Liora.

“Would you like to see the gallery?”

Liora smiled.

“I would.”

They walked together.

By the time the reception ended, Seraphina was exhausted.

The guests had departed.

The musicians had packed their instruments.

The servants were clearing the tables.

Margaret was inspecting the reception rooms with the determination of a military commander reviewing a battlefield.

Seraphina stood near the staircase.

“Was everything successful?”

Margaret looked at her list.

“No major incidents.”

“That sounds promising.”

“One guest misplaced a glove.”

“Tragic.”

“Two guests requested different seating arrangements.”

“Unfortunate.”

“The musicians were paid.”

“Excellent.”

“The eastern gallery was opened.”

Seraphina nodded.

“And?”

“Several guests used it. They were pleased.”

“Good.”

Margaret made a final note.

“However, one of the flower arrangements was moved.”

Seraphina closed her eyes.

“Who?”

“We are investigating.”

“Please don't.”

Margaret's expression remained serious.

“It was placed beside the western staircase.”

Seraphina looked at the empty hall.

“Perhaps it wanted a better view.”

Margaret did not smile.

Seraphina decided to go to bed.

In her room, Mira helped her remove the hair ornament.

“How was the reception, my lady?”

“Long.”

“Did you enjoy it?”

“Some of it.”

“Did you speak with the saintess?”

“Yes.”

“And the crown prince?”

“Yes.”

“And Master Arkwright?”

“Yes.”

Mira paused.

“Did anything happen?”

Seraphina looked at her.

“Several people attempted to give me responsibilities.”

“Did you accept?”

“No.”

“Then it sounds successful.”

Seraphina considered that.

Perhaps it was.

She sat at her desk and opened her notebook.

She wrote:

**Spring Reception — Notes**

- Lady Amelia's nephew, Henry Rosenthal, is interested in regional trade accounts.
- Liora Everwyn is thoughtful, practical, and aware of the Church's limitations.
- Crown Prince Theodore offered assistance with the royal archive request.
- Lucien Arkwright and Master Fenwick are reviewing older magical infrastructure.
- The Academy may submit a formal request for local administrative records.
- No personal supervision promised.
- The eastern gallery was opened after a communication misunderstanding.
- One flower arrangement moved for reasons unknown.

She paused.

Then added:

**The evening was not disastrous.**

That was a considerable achievement.

She closed the notebook.

A knock sounded at the door.

Mira opened it.

A servant stood outside holding a sealed letter.

“For Lady Seraphina. It arrived shortly before the reception ended.”

Seraphina frowned.

“From whom?”

“The Royal Archive, my lady. It bears the Crown's administrative seal.”

She took the letter.

The seal was intact.

Seraphina broke it carefully and unfolded the paper.

The archive had located the authority register for the old winter emergency.

The document confirmed that Sir Geoffrey Marlowe had presided over the committee during Lord Chancellor Harrow's absence.

But there was another detail.

The register contained a handwritten amendment dated three days after the emergency order.

It transferred responsibility for the Northern Consolidated Depot from the central emergency committee to a regional office.

The amendment had been signed by an official whose name Seraphina did not recognize.

And beside that name was a note:

**Final reconciliation to be completed by the receiving authority.**

Seraphina read the line again.

Then she looked at the date.

Three days after the original transfer order.

She sat very still.

The grain had not simply disappeared from the records.

Responsibility for it had been formally transferred.

The question was no longer only who had ordered the grain to be moved.

It was who had been responsible for accounting for it afterward.

Seraphina folded the letter.

“Tomorrow,” she said quietly.

Mira looked at her.

“Tomorrow, my lady?”

Seraphina placed the letter beside her notebook.

“Tomorrow, we find out who received responsibility for two hundred and sixty measures of grain.”

She stood and walked toward the bed.

“And tonight, I am going to sleep.”

For once, nobody argued.
