# Chapter 3 — The Villainess Has a Schedule

Seraphina Veyr discovered that a quiet life required an unreasonable amount of planning.

She had assumed that avoiding the royal court, refusing unnecessary invitations, and staying away from dangerous magical experiments would be sufficient.

It was not.

At breakfast, Mira presented her with a schedule.

At first, Seraphina thought it was a joke.

The paper was folded neatly, sealed with the household crest, and written in a hand so elegant that the letters appeared to have been trained in aristocratic etiquette.

She unfolded it.

**Lady Seraphina Veyr — Daily Schedule**

Sixth Bell — Morning preparation  
Seventh Bell — Breakfast with the Duke and Duchess  
Eighth Bell — Household correspondence  
Ninth Bell — Lessons in estate administration  
Eleventh Bell — Riding practice  
Twelfth Bell — Luncheon  
First Bell After Noon — Social correspondence  
Second Bell — Music or literature  
Fourth Bell — Tea with the Duchess, when required  
Sixth Bell — Evening meal  
Eighth Bell — Private study

Seraphina stared at the paper.

Then she looked at Mira.

“Who made this?”

“Your mother, my lady.”

“Why?”

“To help you recover your routine after your illness.”

Seraphina lowered the schedule.

“Was my previous routine worse?”

Mira considered her answer carefully.

“It was more flexible.”

“That sounds promising.”

“Your lessons were frequently postponed.”

Seraphina brightened.

“Excellent.”

“Your riding practice was often shortened.”

“Wonderful.”

“Your music instructor once waited for nearly an hour.”

Seraphina's expression faded.

Mira continued.

“Your household correspondence was sometimes left until the following day.”

“That's not ideal.”

“No, my lady.”

Seraphina looked at the schedule again.

It was not a routine.

It was a prison with decorative lettering.

“Could you bring me a blank sheet?”

Mira did.

Seraphina took a pen.

She crossed out the sixth bell.

Then the eighth bell.

Then the ninth.

Mira watched in silence.

Seraphina added a new heading.

**A More Reasonable Schedule**

Morning — Wake up  
Breakfast — Eat  
Late morning — Necessary work  
Afternoon — Unnecessary work, if unavoidable  
Evening — Rest

She admired it.

It was simple.

It was efficient.

It was entirely useless.

Mira gently took the paper.

“I believe Her Grace will want to discuss this.”

Seraphina leaned back.

“Of course she will.”

The Duchess did, in fact, want to discuss it.

By the time Seraphina arrived in the family sitting room, Evelyne had already examined the revised schedule.

The duchess held it between two fingers.

“Your proposed routine is very short.”

“I believe it is concise.”

“Concise is not always practical.”

Seraphina sat opposite her.

“Neither is waking before sunrise.”

Evelyne smiled.

“Your father wakes before sunrise.”

“Father has responsibilities.”

“So do you.”

Seraphina considered arguing.

She decided against it.

The duchess placed the paper on the table.

“I do not expect you to follow every hour exactly. But you must understand that your position requires certain habits.”

“Such as?”

“Keeping appointments. Reading correspondence. Maintaining your education. Understanding the estate. Learning how to manage your own household.”

Seraphina nodded slowly.

Those were reasonable.

They were also inconvenient.

She glanced at the schedule.

“Could I choose which lessons to prioritize?”

“Certainly.”

“Could I reduce the music lessons?”

Evelyne raised an eyebrow.

“Do you dislike music?”

“No. I dislike being required to practice the same passage until it becomes a personal enemy.”

The duchess laughed.

“Then we shall speak to your instructor.”

Seraphina relaxed.

“And riding?”

“You may continue at your own pace.”

“Excellent.”

Evelyne folded her hands.

“But the estate administration lessons will remain.”

Seraphina's relaxation ended.

“Why?”

“Because you are the heir.”

“I have heard this argument before.”

“And you will hear it again.”

Seraphina looked toward the window.

The garden was peaceful.

The flowers were arranged in orderly beds.

The fountain was running.

A gardener was trimming the hedges.

It was the sort of place where a person could spend an entire afternoon doing absolutely nothing.

Unfortunately, the person was a duke's daughter.

“Very well,” she said.

Evelyne gave her a small, approving nod.

“Then let us begin with something simple.”

Seraphina looked at the schedule.

“Please define simple.”

“Household correspondence.”

Seraphina's expression became blank.

### Letters, Seals, and Other Forms of Misfortune

The household correspondence arrived in three wooden trays.

The first contained invitations.

The second contained requests from estate tenants.

The third contained letters that had been forwarded from the ducal administration.

Seraphina sat at a desk in the family office.

Mira stood nearby with a fresh supply of ink.

A clerk had explained the basic arrangement.

Letters requiring the duke's direct attention were marked with a red seal.

Letters concerning routine matters were marked with a blue seal.

Invitations were placed in a separate category.

Seraphina examined the first tray.

The invitations were written in a variety of styles.

Some were short.

Some contained several paragraphs about the host's family history.

One included a description of the refreshments that would be served.

Seraphina found this unusually helpful.

At least the writer had understood the purpose of an invitation.

She began sorting them.

A reception in the capital.

A hunting gathering in the eastern territories.

A charitable luncheon.

A musical evening.

A formal dinner hosted by a family she did not recognize.

She placed the capital reception in the attendance pile.

The hunting gathering went into the refusal pile.

The charitable luncheon required further consideration.

The musical evening was declined.

The formal dinner was placed beside the capital reception.

Mira watched the growing piles.

“Are you certain about the hunting gathering?”

“Quite.”

“Your father usually attends.”

“Then he may enjoy it without me.”

“The host may be disappointed.”

Seraphina looked at the invitation.

“Does the host expect me to hunt?”

“Not necessarily.”

“Then why is the event called a hunting gathering?”

Mira did not answer.

Seraphina placed it in the refusal pile.

“Perhaps I am being unreasonable,” she said.

Mira offered no opinion.

That was wise.

The second tray contained requests from tenants.

Seraphina read the first letter.

A farmer in the northern district requested permission to repair a damaged irrigation channel.

The second requested a delay in payment because several animals had died during the winter.

The third concerned a boundary dispute between two neighboring properties.

Seraphina placed the letters together.

“These should go to the estate office.”

“Yes, my lady.”

“Do they usually come here?”

“Some do.”

“Why?”

Mira hesitated.

“Because the tenants believe the ducal family may respond more quickly.”

Seraphina looked at the letters again.

That was not necessarily a bad thing.

But it suggested that the ordinary process might not be working as well as it should.

She wrote a note beside the irrigation request.

**Please forward to the agricultural supervisor. Confirm whether repair is urgent and whether the channel serves more than one property.**

For the payment request, she wrote:

**Ask the estate accountant to review the circumstances before deciding.**

For the boundary dispute:

**Refer to the local magistrate. Do not make a decision without examining the original land records.**

She handed the letters to the clerk.

The man looked surprised.

“Would you like them sent immediately, my lady?”

“Yes.”

He bowed and withdrew.

Seraphina leaned back.

That had been relatively painless.

She had not solved a regional crisis.

She had not exposed a conspiracy.

She had simply sent three letters to the people responsible for handling them.

Perhaps a quiet life was possible after all.

The third tray was more troublesome.

It contained a letter from the estate's financial office.

The writer was Roland Ashford.

Seraphina recognized the name from the previous day's discussion.

The letter concerned the northern grain reports.

Roland had reviewed the records and found that the discrepancies came from several different measurement systems.

He had proposed a standard reporting method.

The agricultural supervisor would visit the villages over the following weeks.

No immediate shortage had been confirmed.

The report also included a note about the estate's annual maintenance budget.

Seraphina read it twice.

The same vague contractor payment she had noticed in the household accounts appeared in the summary.

The amount was not particularly large.

The description was.

**Regional structural maintenance — preliminary allocation.**

No location.

No specific building.

No attached work order.

Seraphina frowned.

She was not an accountant.

She was not qualified to accuse anyone of wrongdoing.

But she understood that money should have a purpose.

She wrote a short note.

**Please ask the financial office to identify the relevant properties and attach the supporting work orders. No conclusion should be drawn until the records are reviewed.**

She signed her name.

Then she stopped.

The signature was elegant.

It was also unfamiliar.

She had signed several documents since waking in this world, but each time she had felt as though she were copying someone else's handwriting.

The original Seraphina had been trained to sign her name beautifully.

Seraphina had not.

She looked at the ink.

A small part of her wondered whether the signature itself might become important someday.

She dismissed the thought.

There was no reason to turn an ordinary letter into a mystery.

She placed it in the outgoing tray.

Then she looked at the remaining correspondence.

There were still twenty-seven invitations.

Seraphina closed her eyes.

“I would like to resign.”

Mira adjusted the tray.

“From what, my lady?”

“Everything.”

### A Lesson in Being an Heir

The estate administration lesson began after luncheon.

Seraphina had hoped to spend the afternoon in the library.

Instead, she found herself in the western office with Cedric Veyr.

Her older cousin was twenty-six, tall, and possessed the expression of a man who had been given too much responsibility and not enough sleep.

He was the estate administrator.

He had been managing the daily affairs of House Veyr for several years.

Seraphina had met him only briefly before her fever.

He bowed.

“Lady Seraphina.”

“Cedric.”

He looked at the documents on the table.

“Your father asked me to explain the basic structure of the estate's administration.”

Seraphina sat.

“Please begin with the least complicated part.”

Cedric gave her a cautious smile.

“The household has several departments.”

He placed a diagram before her.

Agriculture.

Finance.

Transport.

Property maintenance.

Household management.

Legal affairs.

Military administration.

Each department had its own staff and reporting procedures.

Seraphina examined the diagram.

It was more complicated than she had expected.

“Who supervises the departments?”

“The duke.”

“And you?”

“I coordinate the daily reports and ensure that the departments communicate.”

Seraphina nodded.

That sounded exhausting.

Cedric continued.

“The estate's income comes from several sources. Agricultural rents, mining concessions, transport fees, and commercial agreements.”

“Are they all recorded in the same system?”

“Not entirely.”

Seraphina looked up.

Cedric seemed to recognize the question.

“Some departments maintain specialized records. The financial office consolidates them.”

“Does the consolidation happen monthly?”

“Usually.”

“Usually?”

Cedric rubbed his forehead.

“Some departments submit late.”

Seraphina stared at him.

There it was again.

The word that had haunted her previous life.

Late.

“Why?”

“Different reporting schedules. Some records require confirmation from regional offices. Others depend on weather or transport conditions.”

Seraphina looked down at the diagram.

She did not know how to manage a duchy.

She had never managed anything larger than a small office project.

But she knew what happened when a system depended on everyone remembering to do something at the right time.

Eventually, someone forgot.

And then the entire process became a collection of apologies.

“Could the reports have fixed deadlines?”

Cedric considered it.

“They do.”

“Then why are they late?”

“Because the deadlines are not always realistic.”

Seraphina nodded.

That was a better answer.

A deadline that ignored reality was not a deadline.

It was a decorative threat.

She pointed to the diagram.

“Could we identify which reports are regularly late?”

“Yes.”

“And which departments depend on them?”

Cedric's expression changed.

He looked at her more carefully.

“That would be useful.”

Seraphina immediately regretted the suggestion.

She had not intended to become useful.

Useful people were given more work.

More work led to more responsibility.

More responsibility led to the royal court.

The royal court led to the original novel.

The original novel led to the execution.

She lowered her hand.

“Only if it is not too much trouble.”

Cedric smiled faintly.

“It is not.”

Seraphina stared at the diagram.

She had made a mistake.

A small mistake.

An administrative mistake.

The kind that would not cause a catastrophe.

Probably.

### The Schedule Changes

By late afternoon, Seraphina had completed the estate administration lesson.

She had also discovered that her schedule was not entirely unreasonable.

The problem was that every reasonable task seemed to produce another reasonable task.

She returned to her bedroom with a revised plan.

Mira was waiting.

“Her Grace has asked whether you would like tea.”

Seraphina looked at the clock.

“Is it the scheduled tea?”

“Yes.”

“Then I suppose I must.”

Mira smiled.

Seraphina sat at her desk.

She opened the notebook she had begun the previous day.

On a new page, she wrote:

**My Current Responsibilities**

1. Understand the household accounts.
2. Attend the estate administration lessons.
3. Respond to necessary correspondence.
4. Maintain a reasonable relationship with my family.
5. Avoid unnecessary social events.
6. Do not interfere in royal politics.
7. Do not become involved in magical experiments.
8. Do not antagonize the saintess.
9. Do not become the villainess.

She looked at the list.

Then she added:

**10. Do not volunteer for additional responsibilities.**

That was important.

She underlined it twice.

Mira entered with the tea.

Seraphina closed the notebook.

“Tomorrow,” she said, “I intend to do absolutely nothing.”

Mira placed the cup on the table.

“Your riding instructor will arrive at the ninth bell.”

Seraphina stared at her.

“The schedule says riding practice at the eleventh bell.”

“Your mother changed it.”

“Why?”

“Because the weather is expected to become warmer in the afternoon.”

Seraphina looked toward the window.

The sky was clear.

The sun was shining.

The garden was beautiful.

The world was behaving perfectly.

And yet, somehow, her schedule had become worse.

She took a sip of tea.

It was excellent.

That was the only consolation.

That night, Seraphina sat beside the window and watched the estate lights appear one by one.

The servants were finishing their work.

The guards were changing shifts.

Somewhere in the western office, Cedric was probably reviewing the reports she had asked him to examine.

Somewhere in the financial department, Roland Ashford was preparing a response to her letter.

She had not intended to set anything in motion.

She had only wanted to understand the world around her.

But understanding required attention.

Attention created questions.

Questions created work.

And work, apparently, was the natural enemy of a peaceful life.

Seraphina closed the curtains.

She placed the revised schedule beside her bed.

Then she crossed out the words **Do absolutely nothing**.

Underneath, she wrote:

**Do only what is necessary.**

It was not a perfect plan.

But it was a plan.

And for the moment, that would have to be enough.
