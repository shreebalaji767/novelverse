# Chapter 53 — The Mill That Belonged to Everyone

The mill had been operating for seventy-one years.

It had processed grain for House Veyr, neighboring estates, several villages and merchants who paid for milling services.

It had also been maintained almost entirely at House Veyr's expense.

Seraphina discovered this before she finished her second cup of tea.

She stared at the accounts.

Then she looked at Cedric.

"How much?"

Cedric turned a page.

"The annual maintenance expenditure varies. The average over the last five years is approximately 460 crowns."

"And the neighboring estates?"

"They have been using the mill without paying the standard processing charge."

"All of them?"

"According to the account summaries."

Seraphina placed her cup down.

"Then why does the property register say the arrangement ended twenty years ago?"

Cedric turned to the relevant page.

"It says the shared arrangement was terminated and the mill became an exclusive Veyr property."

"Was the mill actually made exclusive?"

"I don't know."

"Did the neighboring estates stop using it?"

"No."

Seraphina looked at him.

Cedric looked back.

Neither spoke.

Finally, Seraphina said, "I would like to meet the person who wrote that property register."

The northern mill stood beside a shallow river, approximately four miles from the main estate.

It was an old stone building with a timber upper floor and a broad waterwheel on its eastern side.

The wheel turned slowly.

Water passed beneath it through a narrow channel, driving the internal mechanism.

Several carts stood outside.

Men were unloading grain sacks while a miller checked the weights.

The place was busy.

It did not look like a property that had spent twenty years operating under an uncertain agreement.

Seraphina stepped out of the carriage.

Cedric followed.

A middle-aged man approached them.

"Lord Cedric. My lady."

"This is Master Edmund Pike," Cedric said. "He manages the mill."

Pike bowed.

Seraphina looked at the building.

"How long have you managed it?"

"Seventeen years, my lady."

"Who pays for maintenance?"

"House Veyr."

"Who owns the equipment?"

"House Veyr, as far as I know."

"Who decides which customers may use it?"

Pike hesitated.

"The arrangement has always been the same."

"Which arrangement?"

"The neighboring estates bring grain here. We process it. House Veyr pays for the mill's upkeep."

Seraphina nodded.

"And do the neighboring estates contribute anything?"

"They occasionally provide timber or labor when repairs are needed."

"Occasionally?"

"Yes."

"Is that recorded?"

"Some of it."

Cedric looked at the mill.

"How much grain does the mill process each year?"

Pike considered.

"Approximately six thousand measures, though it varies with the harvest."

Seraphina glanced at Cedric.

That was considerably more than she had expected.

Inside, the mill smelled of grain dust, damp timber and old machinery.

Pike led them through the main processing room.

The machinery was well maintained, although some of the wooden fittings had been replaced several times.

A young worker adjusted a lever while another checked the grain flow.

Seraphina watched for a moment.

"Does the mill ever operate beyond capacity?"

"During harvest season," Pike replied.

"What happens then?"

"We schedule deliveries."

"Who prepares the schedule?"

"I do."

"Do the neighboring estates receive priority?"

"Under the old arrangement, yes."

"How is priority determined?"

Pike looked uncomfortable.

"By the order in which their grain arrives."

Cedric frowned.

"That was not what the old agreement stated."

Pike turned toward him.

"It wasn't?"

"No. The agreement provided each estate with an allocated processing period."

Pike appeared genuinely surprised.

Seraphina looked at him.

"Have you ever seen the agreement?"

"No, my lady."

"Then how did you learn the arrangement?"

"From the previous mill manager."

"Who learned it from?"

Pike paused.

"I couldn't say."

Seraphina sighed quietly.

The mill had not been operating according to a written agreement.

It had been operating according to a tradition passed from one manager to another.

Traditions were useful.

They were less useful when nobody remembered what they were supposed to preserve.

The accounts were kept in a small office above the main room.

Pike retrieved three ledgers.

The first recorded grain received.

The second recorded processing charges.

The third recorded maintenance expenditure.

Seraphina opened the first.

The entries were clear.

Each delivery had a date, a name, a quantity and a receipt.

The second ledger was less consistent.

Some customers were charged.

Others were marked *shared arrangement*.

A few had no charge recorded at all.

Seraphina checked the names.

The neighboring estates appeared regularly.

There were also merchants who had no connection to the original agreement.

"Why are these merchants using the mill without paying?" she asked.

Pike looked at the entries.

"They are not supposed to be exempt."

"Then why are they marked this way?"

"That may have been a mistake."

"Whose mistake?"

"I don't know."

Seraphina closed the ledger.

"Do you have a list of authorized users?"

Pike shook his head.

"Not a current one."

Cedric looked at the accounts.

"How long has this been happening?"

Pike thought.

"At least since I became manager."

"Seventeen years."

"Yes."

Seraphina glanced at the ledger again.

"Then we need to distinguish the old shared arrangement from ordinary commercial use."

The original agreement was found in the estate archive two days later.

It had been signed by four parties:

- House Veyr.
- House Marlowe.
- House Bellmont.
- House Ashcombe.

The mill had originally belonged to House Veyr, but the four estates had agreed to share its use.

The agreement established fixed processing periods.

Each estate received a certain number of days during the harvest season.

House Veyr was responsible for the mill's structural maintenance.

The other estates were responsible for contributing to the cost of the water channel and providing labor when necessary.

There was also a clause concerning commercial customers.

The mill could accept outside grain when the allocated periods were not in use.

Such customers were required to pay the standard processing charge.

Seraphina read the agreement.

It was straightforward.

Then she opened the amendment.

The amendment had been signed twenty-two years earlier.

It transferred the maintenance responsibility for the water channel to House Veyr in exchange for the neighboring estates surrendering their fixed processing periods.

The estates would retain access to the mill, but their use would be subject to available capacity.

In return, they would no longer be required to contribute to channel maintenance.

Seraphina read the final paragraph.

*This amendment shall take effect upon confirmation of the revised operating schedule by all participating estates.*

She checked the signatures.

All four estates had signed.

The amendment was valid.

However, there was no revised operating schedule attached.

Cedric examined the document.

"Then the old processing periods were supposed to end."

"Yes."

"But the neighboring estates retained access."

"Correct."

"And House Veyr took responsibility for the channel."

"Correct."

He looked at the property register.

"Then why does it say the shared arrangement was terminated?"

"Because the person who updated the register may have interpreted the end of fixed processing periods as the end of shared use."

Cedric frowned.

"That would be inaccurate."

"Yes."

"But the amendment itself is valid."

"Yes."

Seraphina placed the documents side by side.

The problem was not that the neighboring estates had no right to use the mill.

They did.

The problem was that the operating terms had changed, but the mill's management had never received a revised schedule.

The old tradition had continued.

The accounts had gradually become inconsistent.

The property register had recorded an incomplete description of the arrangement.

And House Veyr had continued paying for maintenance without establishing whether its obligations had changed.

Seraphina rubbed her forehead.

"At least this time the original agreement exists."

Cedric smiled.

"That is an improvement."

The next question concerned the maintenance costs.

The amendment had transferred responsibility for the water channel to House Veyr.

It had not necessarily transferred responsibility for every part of the mill.

Seraphina checked the wording.

The estate was responsible for maintaining the channel, the main structure and the waterwheel.

The neighboring estates were no longer required to contribute to those expenses.

That meant the current maintenance arrangement was broadly correct.

The commercial charges were not.

Neither were the processing schedules.

She wrote three questions in her notebook.

1. Which estates retained access rights?
2. What processing capacity could be allocated without affecting ordinary commercial customers?
3. How should commercial charges be recorded?

Cedric looked over her shoulder.

"Should we contact the other estates?"

"Yes."

"All three?"

"All three."

"And the merchants?"

"Not yet. First, we need to establish the mill's available capacity."

Cedric nodded.

The first response came from House Marlowe.

Its estate manager confirmed that the family understood its right to use the mill but had not been aware that the original processing schedule was no longer in force.

The second came from House Bellmont.

It stated that the estate had continued sending grain because the mill had never informed it of any change.

The third response came from House Ashcombe.

Its steward was considerably less cooperative.

He insisted that the old fixed processing periods remained in effect.

Seraphina read the letter.

Then she checked the amendment.

The Ashcombe steward had apparently received a copy of the original agreement but not the later amendment.

She wrote a reply.

It included a copy of the signed amendment and requested confirmation that the estate accepted the revised terms.

There was no reason to argue.

The document was clear.

Meanwhile, Cedric and Pike examined the mill's capacity.

The mill could process approximately six thousand measures in a normal year.

During a particularly strong harvest, that figure could increase slightly if the machinery operated for longer hours.

However, extended operation increased wear on the waterwheel and grinding equipment.

The current arrangement allowed House Veyr to process its own grain first.

Neighboring estates used the mill when capacity was available.

Commercial customers were accepted between scheduled deliveries.

The actual order of work was determined by Pike.

Seraphina studied the figures.

"How much capacity is left after House Veyr's grain?"

"Approximately thirty-five percent in an average year," Cedric replied.

"And how much of that is used by neighboring estates?"

"About twenty percent."

"What about commercial customers?"

"Ten percent."

"Then five percent remains unused."

"Approximately."

Seraphina considered the figures.

The mill was not being overwhelmed.

But the lack of a clear schedule meant that customers sometimes waited unnecessarily.

There was also no consistent method of determining whether a delivery was covered by shared access or ordinary commercial use.

The solution did not require rebuilding the mill.

It required a proper schedule and accurate charges.

The revised schedule was drafted over the next week.

House Veyr would retain priority for its own agricultural requirements.

The three neighboring estates would receive reserved periods during the main harvest season, based on the available capacity and their historical usage.

Outside those periods, they could request additional processing time.

Such requests would be treated as ordinary bookings if capacity allowed.

Commercial customers would pay the standard charge.

All deliveries would be recorded separately.

The maintenance costs would remain the responsibility of House Veyr under the amendment.

The neighboring estates would not be asked to contribute unless a new agreement was negotiated.

Seraphina reviewed the draft.

"Have we included a provision for poor harvests?"

Cedric nodded.

"If one estate has a significantly reduced harvest, its reserved period can be adjusted by mutual agreement."

"Good."

"And what happens if the mill is damaged?"

"The manager will notify all participating estates. The schedule will be suspended until repairs are completed."

Seraphina looked at Pike.

"Can you manage this?"

Pike studied the new schedule.

"Yes, my lady."

"Do you need additional staff?"

"Not at present."

"Do you need new equipment?"

"No. The current machinery is adequate."

"Then we will not buy any."

Pike looked relieved.

The Ashcombe estate finally replied.

Its steward accepted the revised terms.

However, he requested that the estate's reserved processing period be retained during the first two weeks of the harvest season.

Cedric considered the request.

"It is possible, but it would overlap with part of our own schedule."

"Can we move our period?"

"Yes, but it would require changing the transport arrangements."

Seraphina looked at the calendar.

"Would that increase our costs?"

"Not significantly."

"Would it delay our grain deliveries?"

"By two days."

She considered the matter.

"Then ask whether they can accept the third week instead. If not, we can adjust our own schedule."

Cedric smiled.

"You are being generous."

"I am being practical. There is no point fighting over two days if the mill has enough capacity."

The revised agreement was signed at the estate a week later.

The three neighboring families accepted the new schedule.

The mill's operating records were updated.

Pike received a copy of the original agreement, the amendment and the revised schedule.

A separate list was created for commercial customers.

The accounts now distinguished between:

- House Veyr's own grain.
- Shared-access deliveries.
- Ordinary commercial processing.
- Maintenance and repair expenditure.

For the first time in many years, the mill's accounts reflected the arrangement under which it actually operated.

Seraphina examined the completed records.

"That is better."

Cedric nodded.

"Much better."

There was still one unresolved matter.

The property register.

The original entry stated that the shared arrangement had ended twenty years earlier.

That was not entirely accurate.

The fixed processing periods had ended.

The shared access rights had continued.

Seraphina requested that the entry be corrected.

The archive clerk prepared a revised description:

*The mill remains the property of House Veyr. Access rights are shared with the participating estates under the amendment dated twenty-two years earlier. Fixed processing periods have been replaced by a revised operating schedule.*

Seraphina approved it.

The old entry was preserved rather than erased.

The correction included the date, the source documents and the reason for the amendment.

She did not want another future administrator to discover two conflicting entries without understanding why they differed.

That evening, Cedric joined her in the library.

He placed a report on the desk.

"The mill is operating under the new schedule."

"Good."

"The neighboring estates have acknowledged the revised terms."

"Good."

"The accounts have been separated."

"Excellent."

He sat down.

"I also checked the estate's other shared properties."

Seraphina looked up.

"Why?"

"Because of the mill."

"How many?"

"Four."

She stared at him.

"Four what?"

"Other shared properties."

"Which properties?"

"A bridge, a drainage channel, a pasture access road and a small storage building."

Seraphina slowly closed her book.

"Do we have agreements for all four?"

"Yes."

"Are they current?"

Cedric hesitated.

"I don't know."

Seraphina looked at him for several seconds.

Then she opened her book again.

"That is your responsibility."

Cedric smiled.

"I know."

"Then why are you telling me?"

"Because I thought you should know before you discovered them by accident."

Seraphina considered that.

It was a thoughtful gesture.

Unfortunately, it had not improved the situation.

"How old are the agreements?"

"Between thirty and eighty years."

She closed her eyes.

"Of course."

Cedric stood.

"I will review them with the estate surveyor and the legal office."

"Good."

"We will identify the current rights, obligations and responsibilities."

"Good."

"And we will not involve you unless there is a matter that requires your approval."

Seraphina opened one eye.

"That is the most encouraging sentence I have heard all week."

Cedric laughed.

He reached the door, then turned.

"One more thing."

Seraphina looked at him.

"The bridge agreement involves the Marlowe estate."

She stared at him.

Cedric smiled apologetically.

"And the drainage channel may connect to the western orchard."

Seraphina slowly lowered her book.

"Go away."

Cedric left, laughing.

The following morning, Seraphina received a letter from her mother.

It concerned a charity gathering planned for the coming month.

The Duchess wanted Seraphina to attend.

The letter included a list of expected guests, a schedule and a short description of the event.

It also mentioned that the Marlowe family would be present.

Seraphina placed the letter beside the mill agreement.

She looked at both documents.

The Marlowes appeared to be becoming a recurring feature of her life.

She did not particularly object to them.

They had been reasonable during the orchard discussions and cooperative during the mill revision.

But she was beginning to suspect that the Veyr and Marlowe families had shared more than a boundary.

They had shared an alarming number of responsibilities.

That afternoon, Mira entered the library.

"My lady, the estate surveyor has arrived."

"Why?"

"He wishes to discuss the bridge."

Seraphina put down her pen.

"Did Cedric send him?"

"Yes."

"Then why is he here?"

"He says there is a question about the bridge's maintenance agreement."

Seraphina looked at the window.

The garden was peaceful.

The sky was clear.

She had intended to spend the afternoon reading.

Instead, she was about to hear about another old arrangement.

"Tell him to speak with Cedric."

Mira nodded.

"He says Lord Cedric is already waiting."

Seraphina sighed.

"Of course he is."

She stood and collected her notebook.

At least the bridge was not a magical relay station.

That was something.

Probably.

The bridge stood on the northern road, where a narrow river divided the Veyr estate from the Marlowe lands.

It was a stone structure with three arches.

The road crossing it was used by farmers, merchants and estate workers.

Seraphina arrived to find Cedric, the surveyor and two representatives from the Marlowe estate examining the bridge.

One of them was Arthur Bell.

He greeted her.

"My lady."

Seraphina looked at the bridge.

"What is the problem?"

Armitage opened a document.

"The maintenance agreement assigns responsibility for the eastern arch to House Veyr and the western approach to House Marlowe."

"That sounds straightforward."

"It would be, except the river has shifted."

Seraphina looked down.

The water now flowed closer to the western arch.

Armitage pointed to a line on the survey map.

"The original channel ran nearer the center. The current flow is gradually undermining the western foundation."

Bell added, "Our estate has been maintaining the western approach, but we were not aware that the river movement might affect the bridge structure."

Seraphina looked at Cedric.

"Has the bridge been inspected?"

"Not structurally. The road surface is inspected each year."

"By whom?"

"The estate road maintenance teams."

"And the foundations?"

"No formal inspection has been recorded in twelve years."

Seraphina looked at the bridge again.

She did not like the sound of that.

"Then the first task is to inspect the foundations. We should not argue about which estate pays for repairs before we know what repairs are needed."

Bell nodded.

"Agreed."

Cedric looked relieved.

The surveyor began preparing the inspection request.

Seraphina turned toward the road.

A cart was approaching from the northern village.

It crossed the bridge slowly.

The structure held.

For now.

She watched it disappear along the road.

Then she looked at the old agreement in Cedric's hand.

"How old is this one?"

"Eighty years."

Seraphina closed her eyes.

The orchard had been a simple problem.

The mill had been an old agreement.

The bridge was an old agreement with a river that had moved.

She was beginning to understand why her family had so many maps.

They had apparently been collecting obligations for generations.

And the kingdom, as far as she could tell, had never run out of rivers.
