# Chapter 39 — The Engineer Who Remembered the Visit

Master Elias Rook did not look like a man who had spent his life maintaining magical infrastructure.

He looked like a man who had spent his life repairing things that other people had broken and then explaining why the repairs would take longer than expected.

He was sixty-three, broad-shouldered, and slightly stooped. His hair had turned almost entirely grey. A faint scar crossed his left eyebrow, and his hands bore the small marks of someone accustomed to tools.

He arrived at the Royal Infrastructure Department with a leather satchel and an expression of cautious irritation.

"I was told this concerns W-2."

"It does," Adrian Holt replied.

"That station should have been closed twenty years ago."

Holt glanced at Seraphina.

She glanced back.

Neither spoke immediately.

Master Rook sat down.

"Well?"

Seraphina introduced herself and explained the committee's purpose.

"We are reviewing the historical records of the six regional facilities. We are not reopening the original construction contract, and we are not asking you to accept responsibility for decisions made by other offices."

"Then what do you want?"

"To understand what happened after the corrective work order was issued."

Rook looked at the document.

He did not need to read it for long.

"I remember this."

Seraphina leaned forward slightly.

"Do you remember visiting W-2?"

"Yes."

"Approximately when?"

"Late autumn. The year after the original contractor stopped working there."

"Twenty years ago?"

"About that."

"Why did you visit?"

"Because the regional authority received a complaint about an incomplete regulating assembly."

He tapped the paper.

"That was my assignment."

Holt opened his notebook.

"Who submitted the complaint?"

Rook thought for a moment.

"The maintenance superintendent. I believe it was Master Harlan."

Seraphina recognized the name from earlier correspondence.

"Was he responsible for W-2?"

"He was responsible for the regional maintenance schedule. Not the construction contract."

"Did you find a problem?"

Rook gave a short laugh.

"I found several."

The first problem was the equipment.

The regulating assemblies had been delivered, but they were not all in the condition expected.

One had been installed.

A second had been placed in storage.

The third had a damaged outer casing.

"The casing wasn't dangerous," Rook explained. "But it could not be fitted until it had been inspected and repaired."

"Was the repair completed?"

"No."

"Why not?"

"Because the replacement component was not available."

Seraphina looked at Holt.

He recorded the answer.

"Was the component ordered?"

"I submitted a request."

"Do you remember the reference?"

"No. It was a long time ago."

"Was the request approved?"

"I never received the replacement."

Rook shifted in his chair.

"That was the difficulty. The construction team had finished its work, the equipment had arrived, and the station was supposed to enter commissioning. But the materials required to complete the installation were not available."

"Did you report this?"

"Of course."

"To whom?"

"Master Harlan first. He said the regional stores had no suitable replacement. I sent a written report to the maintenance authority."

"Do you have a copy?"

Rook opened his satchel.

He removed a small bundle of folded papers.

"I kept these."

Seraphina looked at him.

"Why?"

"Because I knew someone would eventually ask why the station had never been commissioned."

That was an unexpectedly practical answer.

The report was dated twenty years earlier.

It described the damaged casing and the missing replacement component.

It also contained a recommendation:

*The station should remain disconnected until all regulating assemblies have been inspected, fitted and calibrated. Structural completion must not be treated as commissioning approval.*

Seraphina read the final sentence twice.

"Did you send this to the regional maintenance authority?"

"Yes."

"Do you have proof of receipt?"

Rook produced another sheet.

It was a dispatch register extract.

The report had been received by the regional authority and assigned reference RM-204.

Holt compared it with the records already collected.

"RM-204 does not appear in our central correspondence register."

"That doesn't surprise me," Rook said.

"Why?"

"Because the regional authority had its own records. Not everything went to the capital."

Seraphina looked at the dispatch extract.

"Did you receive a response?"

"Yes."

"What did it say?"

Rook's expression changed.

"It said the matter would be reviewed after the replacement component was located."

"Was it ever located?"

"Not while I was assigned there."

"Did you follow up?"

"Twice."

"Do you have those letters?"

"No. The copies were kept at the regional office."

He paused.

"At least, they were supposed to be."

The committee's technical records changed considerably after Rook's testimony.

The missing commissioning report was no longer the only obstacle.

There had been a specific equipment defect.

A replacement component had been requested.

The regional stores had apparently been unable to provide it.

The station had remained disconnected on the engineer's recommendation.

The work order had never been completed because the necessary part had not arrived.

None of this established why the component had gone missing.

But it explained why a visit to the station had not resulted in a completed work order.

Lucien examined the technical description.

"Was the casing unique to W-2?"

"No," Rook replied. "It was used in the same assembly at other stations."

"Could another station have supplied a replacement?"

"Not without affecting its own equipment."

"Was there a regional reserve?"

"There was supposed to be."

Lucien looked up.

"Supposed to be?"

Rook shrugged.

"The stores register listed two spare casings when I first joined the authority. When I requested one, the storekeeper said neither was available."

Seraphina looked at Holt.

"Do we have the regional stores inventory for that year?"

"We have not located it."

"Then add it to the outstanding records."

Holt wrote it down.

The Crown Prince arrived later that afternoon.

He listened to the summary without interruption.

"So the station was deliberately kept disconnected."

"Yes," Lucien said. "At least according to Rook's report."

Theodore turned to Seraphina.

"Does that settle the question of why W-2 was never commissioned?"

"It explains one reason."

"One reason?"

"The missing component prevented the regulating assembly from being completed. But we still need to know what happened to the replacement request, whether the component existed in regional stores, and whether the station received any later inspection."

Theodore considered this.

"Could the station have been completed without the component?"

"Not safely, according to the technical specification."

Lucien nodded.

"The engineer's recommendation was appropriate."

Theodore looked at Rook.

"Did anyone pressure you to connect it?"

Rook took a moment before answering.

"Not directly."

"Indirectly?"

"There were questions about why the station remained inactive. The regional authority had been told that construction was complete. People wanted to know why it was not producing the expected result."

"Who asked?"

"Mostly administrators. I remember one visit from a representative of the central infrastructure office."

"Do you remember his name?"

"No."

"Did he ask you to connect the station?"

"No. He asked whether the work was complete."

"And what did you say?"

"I told him the building was complete. The circuit was not."

Seraphina nodded.

A familiar distinction.

One that had caused considerable trouble already.

After Rook left, Lucien remained with the technical documents.

"The damaged casing is important," he said.

"Because it prevented commissioning?"

"Yes. But there is another question."

Seraphina waited.

"Why was a replacement component unavailable if two spare casings had been recorded?"

"That depends on whether those two entries represented actual stock."

"Exactly."

"Could they have been reserved for other stations?"

"Possibly."

"Could they have been damaged?"

"Possibly."

"Could they have been transferred?"

Lucien looked at her.

"Also possible."

Seraphina sighed.

"Then we need the stores inventory."

Lucien smiled faintly.

"You have become very good at asking for documents."

"I have become very good at discovering that documents are missing."

The next morning, Holt sent a request to the regional authority's successor office.

He asked for the stores inventory, transfer register and correspondence connected to RM-204.

The response arrived three days later.

The office had located a partial stores ledger.

It listed two regulating casings under the western infrastructure reserve.

One had been marked *issued*.

The other had been marked *transferred*.

Neither entry contained a complete destination.

Seraphina examined the copies.

The first entry was dated six months before Rook's inspection.

The second was dated three months before it.

The first had a signature.

The second had only an initials mark.

Neither could yet be connected to W-2.

"Do we know who authorized the transfers?" she asked.

Holt shook his head.

"The signatures are incomplete."

"Could the original register still exist?"

"The office is checking."

Seraphina looked at the entries again.

Two casings.

Two movements.

One damaged assembly at W-2.

A missing replacement component.

The connection was tempting.

It was also unproven.

She wrote a note beneath the ledger extract:

*Do not identify either transfer as the W-2 replacement component without matching equipment numbers or receiving confirmation.*

Then she placed it in the committee folder.

Theodore reviewed the updated report during their next meeting.

"The records now suggest that W-2 could not be commissioned because a replacement component was unavailable."

"Correct," Seraphina said.

"And two spare casings were recorded in the regional reserve."

"Correct."

"Both were moved before the inspection."

"Correct."

"Could those be the same casings?"

"Possibly."

Theodore looked at the ledger.

"That seems more than a coincidence."

"It is a lead."

"Not proof."

"Exactly."

Minister Garrick examined the figures.

"If the casings were transferred elsewhere, we should be able to find the receiving records."

"Unless those records were lost," Holt said.

Garrick looked displeased.

"Why does every office in this kingdom have a different opinion about keeping records?"

Seraphina answered before anyone else could.

"Because keeping a record and keeping the record where someone can find it are apparently separate administrative responsibilities."

Lucien coughed into his hand.

Theodore looked down at the report.

For a moment, the room was quiet.

Then he said, "We should find the receiving records."

That evening, Seraphina returned to Veyr Estate.

Mira had prepared tea and placed a small plate of biscuits beside her documents.

Seraphina examined the plate.

"How many?"

"Six."

"Why?"

"You have been working late."

"Six biscuits will not improve the kingdom's infrastructure."

"No, my lady. But they may improve your mood."

Seraphina considered the argument.

"That is more realistic."

Mira poured the tea.

"Was the meeting successful?"

"We found out why a station was not connected twenty years ago."

"That sounds useful."

"It is."

"Then why do you look unhappy?"

"Because the missing replacement component may have been transferred somewhere else, and nobody knows where."

Mira thought about this.

"Perhaps someone borrowed it."

Seraphina looked at her.

"Borrowed it?"

"Yes. People borrow things and forget to return them."

"These were specialized magical components."

"People forget to return ordinary things too."

Seraphina glanced at the household inventory on the adjoining desk.

She remembered three missing silver serving trays, two gardening tools and an entire set of kitchen measuring weights.

Mira followed her gaze.

"Those were eventually found."

"The trays were found in the east pantry."

"Yes."

"The gardening tools were in the greenhouse."

"Yes."

"And the measuring weights?"

Mira hesitated.

"Master Hale had taken them to check the kitchen stores."

Seraphina slowly put down her tea.

"How long ago?"

"Four months."

Mira looked toward the kitchen corridor.

"I can ask him to return them."

Seraphina leaned back.

"Please do."

There was a pause.

Then she added, "And perhaps ask him not to borrow the entire set next time."

Mira smiled.

"I will."

The following morning, a new message arrived from the regional authority.

The partial ledger had led the archivists to another register.

It was an equipment transfer book.

The book contained a reference to the first casing.

It had been transferred to a different western station for emergency repairs.

The receiving station was identified as **N-4**.

Seraphina read the entry.

N-4.

The northern relay station in Veyr territory.

The same station whose old equipment records had remained incomplete.

Theodore looked at the date.

The transfer had occurred three months before Rook inspected W-2.

That meant one of the recorded spare casings had been sent to N-4 before W-2's replacement request.

She placed the ledger beside the earlier reports.

There was no evidence yet that the transfer had been improper.

N-4 might have needed the casing more urgently.

The equipment might have been unsuitable for W-2.

The transfer might have been authorized under an emergency maintenance procedure.

But one question had become unavoidable.

Why had the western infrastructure reserve sent a spare component to N-4 while W-2 remained unfinished?

Seraphina wrote to Holt.

*Please obtain the N-4 equipment receipt, the authorization for the transfer and the technical specification of the casing. We must establish whether the transferred component was compatible with W-2 before drawing any conclusions.*

She paused, then added:

*Please also confirm whether N-4 ever installed or used the component.*

She sealed the letter.

The investigation had begun with a missing commissioning report.

It now involved two stations, a damaged assembly, an incomplete work order and a component that had travelled somewhere else.

Seraphina looked at the stack of papers.

"Six facilities," she murmured.

There were still more than half of them to examine.

She reached for a biscuit.

For the first time in several days, she was grateful that the next question concerned an object rather than a person.

Objects, at least, did not insist on explaining themselves.
