# Chapter 5 — The Accountant Is Having a Terrible Day

Roland Ashford had three rules for surviving his profession.

The first was to never trust a number without knowing where it came from.

The second was to never trust a signature without knowing who had signed it.

The third was to never, under any circumstances, allow a duke's daughter to become interested in accounting.

Unfortunately, the third rule had been broken.

And the person responsible was currently sitting in the financial office, holding a ledger upside down.

Roland looked at her.

Lady Seraphina Veyr looked at the ledger.

Neither spoke.

Finally, Seraphina turned the book around.

“Good morning, Master Ashford.”

“Good morning, my lady.”

“I hope I am not interrupting.”

Roland glanced at the papers covering his desk.

He had been preparing the monthly expenditure summary, reviewing mining revenue, and attempting to reconcile two regional transport accounts that had arrived with contradictory figures.

He was also missing a document that had apparently been signed by three different people, none of whom could remember signing it.

“No, my lady,” he replied.

It was a lie.

A polite and necessary lie.

Seraphina sat opposite him.

“I wanted to ask about the household expenditure records.”

Roland closed his eyes briefly.

“Of course.”

### **An Accountant's Natural Enemy**

Roland Ashford was fifty-two years old.

He had served House Veyr for twenty-one years and considered himself a patient man.

He had survived a mining dispute, two unusually severe winters, a failed transport contract, and a duke who occasionally believed that all administrative problems could be solved by issuing a sufficiently firm instruction.

He had also survived the previous Lady Seraphina.

The former young lady had rarely visited the financial office.

When she did, it was usually to request money for clothing, jewelry, or some social event that required an unreasonable quantity of flowers.

Roland had therefore been rather pleased when the young lady developed an interest in reading.

He had not expected her to read the accounts.

He certainly had not expected her to ask questions.

“My lady,” he began carefully, “the household accounts are maintained by Mr. Wren. My office receives the consolidated figures for the estate's annual expenditure.”

“I understand.”

“Then I am uncertain how I can assist.”

“I have been reviewing the household records. Several entries were difficult to understand, so I asked Mr. Wren to clarify them.”

Roland nodded.

“I received his revised inventory report.”

“Good.”

“It is quite clear.”

Seraphina smiled.

“I'm glad.”

Roland waited.

She placed a document on the desk.

“This entry concerns the regional maintenance allocation.”

Roland looked down.

His expression changed.

He knew the entry.

He had seen it several times during the previous month.

“Where did you find this?”

“In the household expenditure summary.”

“That payment was processed through the property office.”

“Yes. Mr. Wren provided the supporting documents.”

“And?”

“The documents explained the work. I simply wondered why the original description was so vague.”

Roland examined the page.

“Because the payment covered several separate properties.”

“Would it not be clearer to list them individually?”

“It would.”

“Then why wasn't it done?”

Roland leaned back.

He was beginning to understand that this conversation was not going to end quickly.

“The property office uses a consolidated payment system. Individual work orders are retained separately.”

Seraphina nodded.

“Would it be possible to attach a summary?”

“Yes.”

“Would that create additional work?”

“Some.”

“Then I shall not ask for it unless it is necessary.”

Roland studied her.

She was not demanding.

She was not accusing him.

She was simply asking questions.

That was somehow worse.

Accusations could be answered.

Questions multiplied.

### **The Ledger That Would Not Behave**

Seraphina placed another paper on the desk.

“This is from the household maintenance record.”

Roland read it.

“An old entry.”

“It lists a payment to a contractor for repairs to the western storage building.”

“Yes.”

“And this entry, from the following month, lists another payment to the same contractor for structural reinforcement.”

“Those are separate works.”

“Could I see the original work orders?”

Roland looked at her.

“Why?”

“Because I would like to understand the difference.”

The accountant exhaled.

He opened a drawer and removed a file.

Inside were several documents.

He spread them across the desk.

The first work order concerned damaged roof supports.

The second concerned reinforcement of a wall.

The third was a separate inspection.

Seraphina read them carefully.

The work had been performed on different dates.

The descriptions were clear.

The payments were supported.

She nodded.

“Thank you.”

Roland began gathering the papers.

Then Seraphina noticed another entry.

It was dated six months earlier.

The amount was larger.

The description was shorter.

**Structural stabilization — western storage complex.**

She looked at the work orders.

“Was this related?”

Roland stopped.

He took the page.

His eyes narrowed.

“I will need to check.”

“Of course.”

He searched through another file.

Then a second.

Then a third.

After several minutes, he found a reference number.

The number led to a payment record.

The payment record led to a contractor's invoice.

The invoice led to a work order.

The work order referred to a regional inspection.

The regional inspection had been filed under a different property name.

Roland stared at the papers.

Seraphina waited.

“This is not a duplicate,” he said at last.

“I didn't think it necessarily was.”

“The earlier payment concerned the main storage building. The later payments concerned separate repairs.”

“So the descriptions are simply too similar?”

“Yes.”

Seraphina nodded.

“That should probably be clarified.”

Roland looked at the documents again.

“Yes,” he said quietly.

He was not angry.

He was embarrassed.

The accounts were technically correct, but the filing system made them difficult to follow.

And that was his responsibility.

Seraphina noticed his expression.

“I am not suggesting that you have done anything wrong.”

Roland looked up.

“I know, my lady.”

“I only want to understand how the records work.”

“I understand.”

He began arranging the papers into a neat pile.

Then he stopped.

“My lady, may I ask you something?”

“Certainly.”

“Why have you become interested in these matters?”

Seraphina considered the question.

She could not tell him the truth.

She could not explain that she had once been an ordinary person who had spent years watching people misunderstand numbers because nobody had bothered to define them.

She could not explain that she was frightened of a future she barely remembered.

So she offered a simpler answer.

“Because I will inherit this estate.”

Roland looked at her.

“That is true.”

“And I would rather understand what I am inheriting before someone expects me to make decisions about it.”

For the first time that morning, Roland smiled.

“That is a sensible answer.”

Seraphina smiled back.

“Thank you.”

He looked at the documents.

Then he added, “Unfortunately, understanding the accounts will take considerably longer than you may expect.”

Seraphina's smile weakened.

“How much longer?”

Roland did not answer immediately.

“Several years, if you intend to understand everything.”

Seraphina stared at him.

Several years.

She had been hoping for several afternoons.

### **A Small Discrepancy**

Roland might have ended the meeting there.

He should have.

But Seraphina's questions had reminded him of an issue he had been avoiding.

It concerned the estate's regional maintenance expenditure.

He pulled a separate ledger from the shelf behind him.

“My lady, since you are interested in the records, there is something I would like to show you.”

Seraphina looked suspicious.

“Is it necessary?”

“Not immediately.”

“Then I am listening.”

Roland opened the ledger.

“The estate maintains several storage buildings, bridges, drainage channels, and transport structures across the duchy. Each property has an annual maintenance allocation.”

Seraphina nodded.

“The money is distributed through regional offices.”

“Yes.”

“Do the regional offices report directly to you?”

“Not all of them. Some report through the property office. Others submit their figures through local administrators.”

“That sounds complicated.”

“It is.”

Roland turned to a page marked with a blue ribbon.

“Three months ago, the northern property office requested an additional allocation for emergency repairs.”

Seraphina examined the entry.

“How much?”

Roland showed her the amount.

It was not small.

But it was not enormous compared with the estate's overall expenditure.

“Was the work completed?”

“The regional office reported that it was.”

“Then what is the problem?”

Roland placed a second document beside it.

“This is the consolidated expenditure record.”

The amount was slightly different.

Seraphina frowned.

“Why?”

“An adjustment was made after the initial report.”

“Was the adjustment approved?”

“The record indicates that it was.”

“By whom?”

Roland turned the page.

There was a signature.

Seraphina looked at it.

She did not recognize the name.

“Who is this?”

“An assistant officer from the northern property administration.”

“Was he authorized to approve the adjustment?”

Roland paused.

“That is what I am trying to confirm.”

Seraphina sat back.

She had not expected this.

“Could it be a clerical error?”

“Yes.”

“Could the amount have been corrected after a revised invoice?”

“Possibly.”

“Could the work have changed?”

“That is also possible.”

Seraphina nodded.

“Then we should not assume anything until the original documents are checked.”

Roland looked at her.

For a moment, he seemed almost relieved.

“That is precisely what I intend to do.”

“Good.”

He placed the ledger aside.

“I will request the original work orders, the regional inspection report, and the authorization record.”

Seraphina stood.

“Then I shall leave you to it.”

Roland looked surprised.

“You do not wish to examine the other accounts?”

Seraphina glanced at the shelves.

There were dozens of ledgers.

Possibly hundreds.

“No.”

“Are you certain?”

“Quite.”

She walked toward the door.

Then she paused.

“Master Ashford?”

“Yes, my lady?”

“Please do not spend the entire day searching for documents because I asked one question.”

Roland smiled faintly.

“I shall try.”

Seraphina left.

The accountant watched the door close.

Then he looked at the ledger.

His day had become considerably worse.

### **The Cost of Being Curious**

By late afternoon, Roland had requested documents from three departments.

The first department replied that the original work order had been transferred to the regional archive.

The second said that the inspection report was being reviewed.

The third claimed that the authorization had been signed by an officer who had since been reassigned.

Roland read the responses.

Then he read them again.

He rubbed his temples.

“Wonderful.”

His assistant, a young clerk named Edwin, looked up.

“Sir?”

“Nothing.”

“Should I prepare the monthly report?”

“Yes.”

“Which version?”

Roland stared at him.

“What do you mean, which version?”

“The regional expenditure figures have not been reconciled. The property office sent an updated summary this morning.”

Roland slowly put down the letters.

“Why?”

“They corrected the northern maintenance allocation.”

“By how much?”

Edwin told him.

Roland went very still.

The difference was not enormous.

But it was not negligible.

“Did they provide the supporting documents?”

“No, sir.”

Roland looked at the ceiling.

He had a sudden desire to retire.

He was not old enough to retire.

That was unfortunate.

“Send another request.”

Edwin nodded.

“And ask the property office not to submit another revised summary until the current figures are reconciled.”

“Yes, sir.”

The clerk withdrew.

Roland returned to the ledger.

He had been an accountant long enough to know that numbers rarely became confusing without a reason.

Sometimes the reason was innocent.

Sometimes it was negligence.

And sometimes it was something else.

He did not intend to guess.

He intended to find out.

### **The Duke Notices**

That evening, Roland requested a private meeting with Duke Alaric.

The duke received him in the eastern office.

“What is it, Ashford?”

“The household accounts have been reviewed by Lady Seraphina.”

Alaric looked up.

“I know. Wren sent me a report.”

“She has also asked questions about the regional maintenance allocation.”

The duke's expression became more attentive.

“What questions?”

Roland explained the discrepancy.

He described the adjustment, the missing authorization record, and the differences between the original and revised summaries.

Alaric listened without interrupting.

When Roland finished, the duke leaned back.

“Do you believe there has been misconduct?”

“I do not know.”

“Your opinion?”

“My opinion is that the records are insufficiently clear. I cannot make a responsible judgment until I have the original documents.”

Alaric nodded.

“Then obtain them.”

“I have already sent requests.”

“Good.”

Roland hesitated.

“There is another matter.”

“Continue.”

“Lady Seraphina did not make any accusations. She was careful to distinguish between an unclear record and an improper payment.”

Alaric looked toward the window.

“That is encouraging.”

“She also asked me not to spend the entire day searching for documents simply because she had raised a question.”

The duke's mouth twitched.

“That sounds like my daughter.”

Roland looked uncertain.

“Does it?”

“She has always been particular about how people spend their time when she is watching.”

Roland was not entirely convinced that the duke and the accountant were discussing the same person.

Alaric folded his hands.

“Continue the review. Keep the matter within the financial and property offices for now. If the discrepancy is explained, I want the record corrected. If it is not, bring it to me.”

“Understood, Your Grace.”

Roland bowed and left.

Alaric remained seated.

He looked at the report from the household steward.

Then at Roland's summary.

His daughter had asked a few questions.

Those questions had revealed weaknesses in the way the estate recorded its spending.

He was proud of her.

He was also beginning to suspect that her sudden interest in administration might become expensive.

Not financially.

In time.

In attention.

In the number of people who would start bringing her problems.

### **The Accountant's Nightmare**

The next morning, Roland arrived at the financial office before sunrise.

He had intended to spend the first hour reviewing the annual mining accounts.

Instead, he found three messages waiting on his desk.

The first was from the northern property office.

The second was from the regional archive.

The third was from an assistant administrator who claimed to have located the original authorization.

Roland opened the first.

It contained an updated expenditure summary.

The second contained a list of archived documents.

The third contained a note stating that the authorization had been issued under a general maintenance instruction rather than an individual repair order.

Roland read the note twice.

Then he reached for the original ledger.

He compared the references.

Something was wrong.

Not necessarily fraudulent.

Not necessarily intentional.

But the general instruction appeared to cover a broader allocation than the one recorded in the regional summary.

He checked the dates.

The instruction had been issued before the repair request.

The adjustment had been recorded afterward.

And the person who had approved the revised amount had not signed the original work order.

Roland leaned back.

He needed the original instruction.

Not a summary.

Not a copied extract.

The original.

He wrote a request to the archive.

Then he wrote another to the property office.

Then he requested the contractor's invoice.

He looked at the clock.

It was not yet the second bell.

He had been awake for hours.

And he had not touched the mining accounts.

Roland closed his eyes.

He now understood why Lady Seraphina had asked him not to spend the entire day searching for documents.

She had been considerate.

It had not helped.

### **A Very Ordinary Afternoon**

Meanwhile, Seraphina was enjoying a peaceful afternoon in the garden.

She had a book.

She had tea.

She had a comfortable chair.

And, most importantly, she had no ledgers.

Mira stood nearby.

“Your father asked whether you would like to attend the estate meeting tomorrow.”

Seraphina lowered her book.

“Why?”

“He said you may find it useful.”

Seraphina looked at the garden.

“Is that a request or an instruction?”

Mira smiled.

“I believe it is an invitation.”

“Can I decline?”

“You can.”

Seraphina waited.

Mira added, “Your father may ask again.”

Seraphina sighed.

“Then I shall attend.”

She returned to her book.

For several minutes, she read without interruption.

Then she looked up.

“Mira?”

“Yes, my lady?”

“Do you think I am becoming too involved in the estate?”

Mira considered this.

“You have attended two meetings, reviewed several documents, and asked the accountant a few questions.”

“That sounds like quite a lot.”

“It is less than your father does in a morning.”

“That is not reassuring.”

Mira smiled.

Seraphina looked down at her book.

She had wanted a quiet life.

She still wanted one.

But she was beginning to understand that a quiet life did not necessarily mean a life without responsibilities.

Perhaps it meant choosing which responsibilities were worth carrying.

She turned a page.

The garden was peaceful.

The tea was warm.

The book was interesting.

For the next hour, she intended to remain exactly where she was.

She would not investigate the maintenance allocation.

She would not request another ledger.

She would not ask another question.

She would simply read.

It was a perfect plan.

Unfortunately, the plan lasted only forty-three minutes.

A servant approached with a sealed envelope.

“My lady, a message from Master Ashford.”

Seraphina looked at it.

The seal belonged to the financial office.

She opened the envelope.

Inside was a brief note.

*Lady Seraphina,*

*I have located the original authorization connected to the northern maintenance allocation. The matter requires further examination. I would appreciate a brief meeting at your convenience.*

*Respectfully,*

*Roland Ashford*

Seraphina stared at the note.

Then she looked at Mira.

“Do you know what this means?”

Mira shook her head.

“It means Master Ashford has found something.”

“It means I am going to have to read more documents.”

Mira said nothing.

Seraphina folded the letter.

She looked toward the peaceful garden.

Then she looked at the letter again.

“Why,” she whispered, “is accounting so determined to ruin my life?”

In the financial office, Roland Ashford sat before a newly opened archive box.

Inside were the original authorization, several supporting records, and a correspondence file that had not been included in the regional summary.

He carefully removed the first document.

The paper was old.

The ink had faded slightly.

But the signature was clear.

Roland compared it with the copied authorization attached to the expenditure report.

They were not identical.

He placed the two documents side by side.

Then he called for his senior assistant.

“Edwin.”

“Yes, sir?”

“Bring me the property office's full correspondence record for this allocation.”

“Certainly.”

“And do not use the summary.”

Edwin hesitated.

“Sir?”

“I want the original letters.”

The clerk hurried away.

Roland looked at the documents once more.

Lady Seraphina had asked a simple question.

Now he was beginning to wonder whether the estate had been asking the wrong questions for years.
