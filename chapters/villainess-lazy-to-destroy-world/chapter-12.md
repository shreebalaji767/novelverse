# **Chapter 12 — The Price of a Bag of Grain**

Seraphina Veyr had developed a new appreciation for merchants.

Not because she particularly enjoyed negotiating with them.

She did not.

Negotiations involved sitting upright for extended periods, listening to people explain why their prices were reasonable, and pretending that the phrase *market conditions* was a complete explanation.

But merchants possessed one quality she increasingly admired.

They knew exactly how much money they were losing.

Unfortunately, the ducal administration did not always share that knowledge.

This discovery became particularly irritating when Seraphina learned that the Veyr estate had been purchasing grain at three different prices from three different suppliers, all within the same region and during the same purchasing period.

She discovered this at breakfast.

Which was becoming an increasingly dangerous place to discover things.

“Cedric,” she said, looking at the document beside her plate.

Her cousin, who had been attempting to enjoy his tea, looked up.

“Yes?”

“Why are we paying three different prices for the same grain?”

Cedric slowly put down his cup.

“Good morning to you too.”

“Good morning. Why?”

He reached for the document.

“Where did you get this?”

“Roland sent it.”

“When?”

“Yesterday.”

“Why didn't you ask him yesterday?”

“Because I was trying to have dinner.”

Cedric examined the page.

“The prices are not necessarily for the same grain.”

“They are all listed as northern wheat.”

“The quality may differ.”

“Then why are they all listed under the same category?”

Cedric looked at the document again.

Seraphina waited.

He turned the page over.

Then turned it back.

“Roland may have an explanation.”

“Excellent. We should ask him.”

Cedric closed his eyes.

“You are aware that he is currently preparing the revised procurement procedure?”

“Yes.”

“And reviewing the historical reserve records?”

“Yes.”

“And examining the disputed maintenance authorization?”

“Yes.”

Seraphina considered the list.

“Perhaps we should hire another accountant.”

Cedric looked at her.

“We have accountants.”

“Then perhaps we should give them less work.”

“That would require us to stop discovering things.”

Seraphina returned to her breakfast.

It was an unpleasantly reasonable suggestion.

The three prices were as follows:

| Supplier                  | Price per standard measure | Recorded quality |
| ------------------------- | -------------------------- | ---------------- |
| Brant Trading House       | 6 silver crowns, 20 copper | Northern wheat   |
| Dalen Agricultural Supply | 6 silver crowns, 05 copper | Northern wheat   |
| Whitmore Grain Company    | 6 silver crowns, 55 copper | Northern wheat   |

Seraphina studied the table again in her office.

The prices were close enough to suggest ordinary market variation.

They were also different enough to make her curious.

She had learned not to assume that a difference in records meant wrongdoing.

Sometimes the explanation was mundane.

Sometimes the explanation was complicated.

Sometimes the explanation was that someone had created a system that made a simple question impossible to answer.

She suspected this was the third situation.

Cedric entered carrying a second folder.

“I spoke with Roland.”

“And?”

“He says the prices reflect different contract terms.”

Seraphina looked at him.

“Such as?”

“Transport responsibility, delivery location, payment timing, and storage arrangements.”

“Then we are not comparing the same thing.”

“Correct.”

She leaned back.

“Why did the report put them in one table?”

“Because the report compares the recorded price of northern wheat.”

“That is technically correct.”

“Yes.”

“But not particularly useful.”

“Also correct.”

Seraphina rubbed her forehead.

“Who prepared it?”

“The regional procurement clerk.”

“Does he know the contracts are different?”

“Probably.”

“Then why?”

Cedric placed the second folder on the desk.

“Because he was instructed to prepare a summary of purchase prices, not a comparison of total procurement costs.”

Seraphina stared at him.

“That sounds like a distinction someone invented to avoid answering a question.”

Cedric shrugged.

“It may simply be the distinction he was given.”

She sighed.

“Fine. What do we actually know?”

Cedric opened the folder.

“Brant Trading House supplies grain to the northern warehouses. Its quoted price includes transport to the receiving point, but payment is due within twenty days of verified delivery.”

“Dalen?”

“Dalen supplies grain from farms closer to the central road. Its price is lower, but transport beyond the first warehouse is charged separately.”

“And Whitmore?”

“Whitmore's price includes transport and temporary storage. Payment is due within forty-five days.”

Seraphina looked at the figures.

“So Brant is more expensive than Dalen, but Dalen's transport costs are additional. Whitmore is more expensive, but it allows us longer to pay.”

“Exactly.”

“Do we know which arrangement is cheapest?”

“Not yet.”

“Why?”

“Because the regional office records transport expenses separately, but not always against the original purchase contract.”

Seraphina closed her eyes.

She had expected this.

She had still hoped otherwise.

“Very well,” she said. “We need a comparison that includes the full cost.”

Cedric nodded.

“I thought you might say that.”

“Then why did you bring me the folder?”

“To tell you that I had already asked Roland to prepare one.”

Seraphina opened her eyes.

She looked at Cedric.

Cedric looked back.

“That,” she said, “was very considerate.”

“I am learning.”

The comparison took two days.

Seraphina did not spend those two days sitting over the accounts.

She had learned that watching people work did not necessarily make the work happen faster.

Instead, she attended her lessons, reviewed household correspondence, and tried to avoid opening the Royal Academy letter.

She failed on the second afternoon.

The letter contained a formal invitation to a preliminary discussion concerning the Academy's regional infrastructure programme.

The language was polite.

The intention was not particularly clear.

The Academy wished to discuss the maintenance of magical installations in provincial territories, especially those connected to transport, storage, and public facilities.

Seraphina read it twice.

Then she placed it inside a drawer.

She had no desire to become involved in magical infrastructure.

Her previous memories of the novel were incomplete, but they contained enough to make her cautious.

The Royal Academy was involved in the original catastrophe.

She did not know precisely how.

She only remembered that a magical research project had been connected to political conflict, the saintess, and the eventual destruction of a large part of the capital.

The details were hazy.

She could not remember whether the problem had been negligence, deliberate interference, or a combination of both.

She did remember one thing.

The original Seraphina had become involved.

That was enough.

Unfortunately, the Academy had sent another letter.

She placed the second envelope beside the first.

Then closed the drawer.

There were limits to how much trouble a person should be allowed to encounter in a single week.

On the third morning, Roland arrived with the procurement comparison.

He looked slightly less exhausted than usual.

Seraphina suspected this was because he had managed to sleep.

A rare achievement.

Cedric joined them in the small financial office.

The report was considerably longer than the original table.

It included grain prices, transport charges, storage costs, delivery schedules, payment terms, and estimated losses caused by delays.

Roland placed it on the desk.

“We have compared the three suppliers using the available records.”

“Good.”

“There are some limitations.”

“Of course.”

“Several transport charges were recorded as general regional expenses. We have allocated them where the supporting documents identify the relevant deliveries. Some amounts remain unassigned.”

“Can we still compare the contracts?”

“Yes, but the result should be treated as an estimate rather than a final calculation.”

Seraphina nodded.

“Please explain.”

Roland pointed to the first entry.

“Brant Trading House offers the highest grain price among the three, but its transport charges are included. It also delivers directly to the designated warehouses.”

“And Dalen?”

“Dalen offers the lowest initial price. However, the ducal office pays separately for transport from the collection point to the warehouse. Once those charges are included, the total cost is slightly higher than Brant's.”

“How much higher?”

“Approximately twelve copper pieces per standard measure, based on the available records.”

Seraphina looked at the figure.

“And Whitmore?”

“Whitmore's price remains the highest. Its advantage is the longer payment period and temporary storage arrangement. However, the storage is not always necessary, and the contract does not guarantee that the grain will be delivered within the requested schedule.”

“So we are paying for an advantage we do not always use.”

“Yes.”

Seraphina studied the table.

“Which supplier performed best?”

Roland looked at the delivery records.

“Brant had the most consistent delivery schedule. Dalen was reliable for smaller quantities. Whitmore had several delays.”

“Why?”

“Road conditions, according to its reports.”

“Were those reports verified?”

“Not independently.”

Seraphina nodded.

“Then we cannot treat the explanation as established.”

“Correct.”

Cedric leaned forward.

“What would you recommend?”

Roland adjusted the papers.

“I would not recommend selecting one supplier for all requirements. Brant is suitable for larger deliveries to the main warehouses. Dalen may be more economical for nearby locations. Whitmore's longer payment period could be useful in certain circumstances, but we should not pay for temporary storage unless we need it.”

Seraphina looked at him.

“That sounds sensible.”

Roland appeared faintly surprised.

“Thank you, my lady.”

“Why do you look surprised?”

“Because I expected you to ask for a single answer.”

“I am beginning to understand that the world is inconveniently full of different situations.”

Cedric laughed quietly.

Seraphina ignored him.

“Do we have a record of which villages need grain most urgently?”

Roland produced another sheet.

“Yes. The regional office has prepared a list of current requests.”

She read through it.

Westmere was listed.

So were three other villages.

Two required grain for local distribution.

One had requested additional seed grain before the next planting period.

Seraphina paused.

“Why is seed grain included in the same procurement report?”

Roland examined the page.

“It should not be.”

“Is it purchased under the same contract?”

“No. Seed grain has separate quality requirements.”

“Then why is it here?”

“The regional clerk appears to have included all grain-related requests in one summary.”

Seraphina put the paper down.

“Please separate them.”

Roland nodded.

Cedric examined the village list.

“Westmere's request is relatively small.”

“How small?”

“Forty standard measures.”

“Do they have enough storage space?”

“The existing village storehouse is being repaired.”

“Then where will the grain go?”

Cedric looked at the document.

“I do not know.”

Seraphina looked at Roland.

He did not know either.

She sighed.

“Before we arrange a delivery, we should confirm where the grain will be stored.”

Cedric nodded.

“I will ask Harold.”

“Thank you.”

Seraphina looked at the table again.

There was something she found increasingly frustrating about the entire system.

People were not necessarily failing to do their jobs.

They were completing individual tasks without always knowing what happened before or after them.

The purchasing office bought grain.

The transport company moved it.

The warehouse recorded it.

The payment office issued money.

The village requested it.

And somewhere between these steps, the actual purpose of the grain became difficult to identify.

She was beginning to think the problem was not simply that records were incomplete.

It was that each office had a different idea of what the records were supposed to prove.

Mistress Brant returned to the estate two days later.

This time, she came with a revised proposal.

Seraphina was not originally scheduled to attend the meeting.

She had planned to spend the morning in the library.

That plan lasted until Cedric informed her that the merchant had requested her presence.

“Why?” she asked.

“Because she wants to discuss the revised receiving procedure.”

“Can she discuss it with Roland?”

“She can.”

“Then why does she need me?”

Cedric looked at her.

“She asked for you.”

Seraphina stared at him.

“Why?”

“I believe she finds your questions useful.”

“That is unfortunate.”

“Why?”

“Because I do not.”

Cedric smiled.

“You should come anyway.”

Seraphina stood.

“Fine.”

She took her notebook.

Then she put it down.

She was not going to write anything.

She was only observing.

That was still her official role.

Mistress Brant had brought a new contract draft.

It was shorter than the original.

The receiving procedure had been revised to include three requirements:

- The dispatch quantity would be recorded and signed by the merchant's representative.
- The receiving quantity would be measured at the designated warehouse, with both the receiving clerk and the merchant's representative present where practical.
- Any difference would be recorded before the delivery certificate was finalized.

Seraphina read the clauses.

“They are clear.”

“That was the intention,” Mistress Brant said.

“Who prepared this?”

“Our legal clerk.”

“He did a good job.”

“He will be pleased to hear that.”

Roland examined the payment section.

“The contract still states that payment is due within twenty days of verified delivery.”

“Yes,” Mistress Brant said.

“Would your company accept payment based on the verified quantity if a dispute remains over the difference?”

“Provided the undisputed quantity is paid on schedule.”

Roland nodded.

“That can be arranged.”

Harold Fenwick, who had joined the meeting, looked uneasy.

“What happens if the merchant's representative is not present?”

Mistress Brant answered.

“The receiving clerk records the quantity, and the warehouse retains a copy of the weighing record. The merchant may request an independent review if the difference exceeds the agreed tolerance.”

“What tolerance?”

“That would need to be negotiated.”

Harold turned to Roland.

“We cannot allow every minor difference to delay payment.”

“Agreed,” Roland said. “A reasonable tolerance can be specified.”

Seraphina examined the clause.

“What happens if the weighing equipment is inaccurate?”

Mistress Brant looked at her.

“Then both parties have a problem.”

“Who checks it?”

“That depends on the equipment.”

“Can the contract require periodic inspection?”

Roland considered.

“Yes. We could include a maintenance and calibration record.”

Harold looked at the duke.

“Your Grace, this is becoming excessively complicated.”

Alaric did not respond immediately.

Seraphina looked at Harold.

“Is it more complicated than arguing over missing quantities after every delivery?”

Harold opened his mouth.

Then closed it.

Roland turned a page.

“There is another issue. We should standardize the units used in the regional reports.”

“Yes,” Seraphina said. “But only after confirming that the standard measure is understood by every warehouse.”

Harold frowned.

“They all use the standard measure.”

“Then confirming it should be easy.”

Mistress Brant gave a quiet laugh.

The discussion continued.

They agreed to test the revised procedure with a limited number of deliveries before applying it across the region.

The initial trial would include two main warehouses and one smaller receiving point.

Brant Trading House would participate.

The regional office would provide the weighing records.

Roland would compare the results with dispatch documents.

No one would change the price agreement until the trial established whether the revised process was workable.

It was not a dramatic solution.

It did not expose a conspiracy.

It did not recover missing treasure.

It did not even guarantee that the merchants would lower their prices.

But it offered a way to determine what was actually happening.

Seraphina considered that a satisfactory outcome.

After the meeting, Mistress Brant requested a private conversation.

Not a secret conversation.

Just one without the entire administrative department listening.

They sat in a small adjoining room with tea.

Seraphina chose the chair nearest the window.

Mistress Brant chose the chair nearest the door.

Both positions suggested experience.

“You have been examining the old reserve records,” the merchant said.

Seraphina looked at her.

“Yes.”

“Does that include the northern storehouses?”

“N-14 and N-09.”

Mistress Brant's expression changed slightly.

“You found N-14?”

“We inspected the building.”

“And the grain?”

“Under examination.”

The merchant was quiet for a moment.

“Then I should tell you something.”

Seraphina waited.

“My company did not supply grain to N-14. But my predecessor handled several contracts involving the old reserve system.”

“Your predecessor?”

“My father. He managed the company before me.”

“Do you have those contracts?”

“Some.”

“Could we see them?”

“Perhaps. I would need to examine the archive.”

Seraphina nodded.

“Do you remember anything about the reserve programme?”

Mistress Brant looked into her tea.

“Only what my father told me. It was established after a severe winter. The ducal administration purchased grain from several merchants and distributed it among the northern villages.”

“That was twenty-six years ago.”

“Yes.”

“Do you know whether the reserve was replenished?”

Mistress Brant did not answer immediately.

“I remember my father complaining that several payments had been delayed.”

“Which payments?”

“I do not know.”

“Were they connected to N-14?”

“I cannot say.”

Seraphina considered this.

“Do you know anyone who might remember?”

“There was an old purchasing clerk. His name was Martin Hale. He worked with my father during that period.”

Seraphina kept her expression neutral.

“Is he still alive?”

“I believe so. He retired to a village near the eastern trade road.”

“Would you be able to contact him?”

“I can try.”

“Please do not suggest that we already know something is wrong. I only want to understand how the old programme operated.”

Mistress Brant nodded.

“That is fair.”

Seraphina finished her tea.

The conversation had been useful.

It had also produced another name.

She was beginning to suspect that the kingdom contained an unlimited supply of people who had once been responsible for something and now remembered only part of it.

That evening, Seraphina went to the library.

She intended to read for half an hour.

Instead, she found herself sitting at a large table with the procurement report, the old reserve allocation list, and a map of the northern territories.

She placed the documents side by side.

N-14.

N-09.

N-03.

Three locations.

Three references.

One old reserve programme.

The records did not yet prove that anything had been stolen.

The differences could have resulted from emergency distribution, delayed replenishment, changes in measurement, or ordinary administrative errors.

But the references appeared in payment records long after the original programme.

That was the part she could not explain.

She studied the map.

N-14 was near Westmere.

N-09 was farther north, near an old road that no longer appeared on the current transport map.

N-03 was listed as Southmere Reserve Depot, in the southern district.

Why were payments connected to these locations?

Were they actual deliveries?

Were they maintenance allocations?

Were they references copied from older documents?

She did not know.

And she was not going to guess.

She reached for her notebook.

**16. A difference in price does not automatically mean someone is dishonest.**

She paused.

Then wrote:

**17. A record can be accurate and still fail to answer the question being asked.**

She closed the notebook.

At that moment, Mira entered carrying a small tray.

“Your mother asked me to remind you that dinner will be served in fifteen minutes.”

Seraphina looked at the clock.

“How long have I been here?”

“Nearly two hours.”

“That is impossible.”

“I am afraid it is not.”

Seraphina looked at the documents.

She had not noticed the time passing.

This was becoming a problem.

She gathered the papers into a folder.

“Did my mother say anything else?”

“She asked whether you would attend the spring reception preparations tomorrow.”

Seraphina stopped.

“Preparations?”

“Lady Evelyne has invited several members of the household staff to discuss the arrangements.”

“Why?”

“Because the reception will be held at the estate, my lady.”

Seraphina looked at the ceiling.

Of course.

The spring reception.

She had agreed to attend.

She had not considered that attending might involve preparations.

“Very well,” she said.

Mira nodded.

“And the Royal Academy has confirmed that its delegation will attend the reception.”

Seraphina slowly turned towards her.

“The Academy?”

“Yes, my lady.”

“Who confirmed this?”

“Their secretary.”

“Why?”

“They are expected to meet several regional administrators during the event.”

Seraphina stared at the folder in her hands.

The Academy was coming to the estate.

The saintess might also attend, depending on the guest list.

The original novel had begun its central political conflict around a spring gathering.

She could not remember whether it had been this reception.

She could not remember the exact sequence of events.

But she remembered enough to feel deeply uncomfortable.

“Has the guest list been finalized?” she asked.

“Not yet.”

“Good.”

Mira waited.

Seraphina placed the folder on the table.

“Tomorrow,” she said.

Mira nodded.

Then, after a brief pause, she asked, “Would you like me to remind you to eat dinner?”

Seraphina looked at her.

“That would be appreciated.”

Dinner was peaceful.

For approximately twelve minutes.

Then Duke Alaric asked about the merchant meeting.

Seraphina explained the revised receiving procedure.

Evelyne listened carefully.

Cedric described the procurement comparison.

Roland's preliminary report was summarized.

Alaric approved the limited trial.

Evelyne asked whether the household could assist with temporary storage arrangements if necessary.

Cedric said it could, provided the storage facilities were inspected first.

Seraphina ate her meal.

Then her father asked, “What did you learn from the merchant?”

She considered.

“That the price of grain is not the same as the cost of supplying it.”

Alaric nodded.

“And?”

“That a lower price can become more expensive when transport, storage, and payment conditions are included.”

“Good.”

“And that merchants are not necessarily being unreasonable when they refuse to lower their prices.”

Evelyne smiled.

“That is an important lesson.”

Seraphina took another bite.

“I also learned that the old reserve records may be connected to historical procurement contracts.”

Alaric looked at her.

“Connected how?”

“I do not know yet. Mistress Brant mentioned that her father handled contracts during the emergency reserve programme twenty-six years ago. She may have old documents.”

Roland, who had joined them for dinner, looked up.

“That could be useful.”

“Only if the records are relevant,” Seraphina said.

Alaric nodded.

“Ask for copies. Do not make assumptions.”

“I know.”

He looked at her for a moment.

Then he smiled slightly.

“I believe you do.”

Seraphina returned to her meal.

For a brief moment, she felt something warm.

Not because she had accomplished anything extraordinary.

But because her father had listened.

She was beginning to understand that being treated as a member of the family did not require her to solve every problem in the duchy.

It only required her to be present.

She could manage that.

Probably.

Later that night, a messenger arrived at the regional office.

He carried a sealed packet from the northern property administration.

The packet contained a copy of an old procurement register.

The clerk who received it checked the seal, entered the date, and placed it in the incoming documents cabinet.

He did not notice the faded reference written in the lower corner.

He did not recognize the name of the retired purchasing officer.

And he did not know that the register contained a delivery entry dated three months after the final recorded replenishment of the northern reserve.

The entry was brief.

**Destination: N-14.**

**Quantity: 80 standard measures.**

**Receiving officer: Martin Hale.**

**Payment status: Settled.**

The clerk placed the packet beneath two newer reports.

Then he returned to his work.

The next morning, Roland would find it.

And Seraphina would discover that the old reserve programme had not ended when the records said it had.

**End of Chapter 12**
