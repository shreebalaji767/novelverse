# Chapter 68 — The Two Thousand Four Hundred Crowns That Nobody Had Claimed

Seraphina disliked unpaid debts.

She disliked imaginary debts more.

Unfortunately, the distinction was becoming difficult to establish.

The old guarantee had been examined by the legal adviser, Roland, and the Treasury records administrator. All three agreed that the instrument was genuine.

All three also agreed that the remaining 2,400 crowns could not yet be treated as an outstanding debt.

The guarantee had a maximum value of 4,000 crowns.

A contribution of 1,600 crowns appeared in the attached schedule.

The remaining amount was conditional upon final certification.

The certification had not been found.

The project had been partially completed.

And the handwritten note stating that the balance should remain pending had no identifiable author.

It was a remarkably effective way to create a problem without actually establishing one.

Seraphina sat at her desk while Cedric reviewed the latest correspondence.

"Have we received the Treasury inspection report?"

"Not yet."

"The final accounts?"

"Requested."

"The original project completion records?"

"Also requested."

Seraphina looked at him.

"Then what have we received?"

"Acknowledgment of the requests."

She closed her eyes.

Cedric waited.

"Excellent," she said.

He gave her a sympathetic look.

"Would you like me to arrange another cup of tea?"

"Would it come with the final accounts?"

"Probably not."

"Then no."

The legal adviser arrived that afternoon.

He carried a copy of the guarantee and several notes.

"I have reviewed the instrument," he said. "There is no immediate basis for a payment demand."

"Because the final certification is missing?"

"Correct. The guarantee specifies a maximum amount, not an unconditional obligation. It also identifies the circumstances under which the contribution becomes payable."

He placed the document on the table.

"Section four is particularly important. It states that the guarantee applies only to expenditure certified under the approved schedule."

Seraphina looked at the clause.

"And section seven?"

"That provides for the release of the guarantor when the committee certifies that the project has been completed, abandoned, or formally closed."

"Has any such certification been found?"

"No."

"Then the guarantee remains unresolved."

"Administratively, yes. But unresolved does not mean payable."

Seraphina nodded.

The distinction needed to remain clear.

A financial obligation could remain open in a historical register without being due.

It could also have been discharged without the correct document being transferred to the estate.

Neither possibility justified treating the full amount as an outstanding debt.

The legal adviser continued.

"There is another issue. The guarantee was issued to the Northern Development Committee, not directly to the Royal Infrastructure Allocation Office."

"Does that matter?"

"It may. The Allocation Office later assumed responsibility for reviewing historical commitments, but we need to establish whether it also received the committee's legal rights and obligations."

Seraphina frowned.

"So the office may have inherited the records without inheriting the guarantee."

"Precisely."

Cedric looked at the document.

"Could the committee have transferred its authority when it was dissolved?"

"It could have. But we need the dissolution order and the transfer schedule."

Seraphina looked at the legal adviser.

"Can we request both?"

"Yes."

"Then do so."

He nodded.

"One more recommendation. House Veyr should not issue any statement recognizing a current liability until the matter is resolved."

Seraphina almost smiled.

"That was not our intention."

"I thought not."

The Treasury inspection report arrived two days later.

It was fourteen pages long.

Northgate Historical Services had been granted supervised access to the historical commitments collection for a period of five working days.

The inspection had been authorized by the deputy director of the Central Treasury Records Division.

The company's stated purpose was to prepare a historical reference catalogue of regional development guarantees.

The report listed thirty-eight documents examined.

Twenty-one were classified as completed or discharged.

Nine were classified as incomplete.

Five were marked as requiring further verification.

Three were listed as having no surviving final status.

House Veyr's guarantee appeared in the last category.

The report described the instrument accurately.

It recorded the maximum amount of 4,000 crowns.

It also recorded the 1,600-crown contribution.

But the summary column contained a phrase that Seraphina did not like.

**Potential remaining exposure: 2,400 crowns.**

She read it twice.

Then she turned to the notes.

The report stated that the final certification had not been located.

It did not state that the amount was due.

It did not state that a demand had been made.

It did not state that the project had been completed.

The phrase *potential remaining exposure* was technically cautious.

But it was easy to misunderstand.

Seraphina placed the report beside the original instrument.

"Who wrote this?"

Cedric checked the signature.

"Prepared by Northgate Historical Services. Reviewed by the Treasury receiving officer."

"Did the Treasury approve the findings?"

"The authorization says the company was permitted to submit an archival report. It does not say the Treasury certified the contents."

Seraphina nodded.

"So this is a private historical assessment."

"Yes."

"Not a government liability register."

"Correct."

Roland entered the room.

He had been examining the old financial schedule.

"There's another problem."

Seraphina looked up.

"What?"

"The 1,600 crowns."

He placed a separate ledger extract on the desk.

"The Treasury's historical account records a payment of 1,600 crowns to the Northern Development Committee. But the payment description does not identify House Veyr as the source."

Seraphina frowned.

"Then why is it recorded against our guarantee?"

"Because the attached schedule lists the contribution under House Veyr."

"Could the payment have come from the estate?"

"It could have. But the Treasury entry records only the committee's receipt. We need the originating account."

Roland opened another page.

"House Veyr's account book for that year contains an entry for 1,600 crowns."

Seraphina leaned forward.

"Paid to whom?"

"Regional development contribution."

"Is there a receipt?"

"No."

"Then we have a matching amount but no direct payment confirmation."

Roland nodded.

"It may be the same transaction."

"Or another contribution."

"Yes."

Seraphina looked at the two records.

A 1,600-crown payment appeared in the Veyr estate accounts.

A 1,600-crown receipt appeared in the committee's accounts.

The schedule connected them.

But the payment trail was incomplete.

She looked at Roland.

"Can we trace the transfer?"

"The estate used a regional banking house at the time. Its surviving records may identify the recipient."

"Which banking house?"

"Meridian Northern Bank."

Seraphina stared at him.

"Does it still exist?"

"Yes."

"Then perhaps we can ask them."

Roland smiled.

"That is my intention."

The bank's archival department responded after four days.

It had retained a limited collection of historical transaction ledgers.

The relevant year was available.

The account number was not.

The bank requested the estate's original account reference and proof of succession to the account.

Cedric supplied both.

The bank then located the transaction.

The payment of 1,600 crowns had been made from House Veyr's account.

The recipient was the Northern Development Committee.

The date matched the committee's receipt.

The transfer reference also matched the old financial schedule.

For the first time, the 1,600-crown contribution was confirmed.

Seraphina read the bank's letter.

"Good."

Roland nodded.

"So that part is settled."

"Yes."

"And the remaining 2,400?"

"Still conditional."

Roland sighed.

He had hoped for more.

Seraphina placed the letter beside the guarantee.

The payment had been made.

The committee had received it.

But the guarantee had been issued for a maximum of 4,000 crowns.

The remaining 2,400 had never been paid.

That did not establish that it was owed.

It simply established that the original contribution was real.

She looked at the payment description.

**Northern Development Committee — Regional Improvement Contribution.**

The bank had also provided the account's original payment instruction.

It contained an additional line.

*Contribution to be credited against Veyr estate guarantee upon receipt of certified project schedule.*

Seraphina looked at Roland.

"That means the 1,600 was credited against the guarantee."

"Yes."

"Then the remaining amount was never transferred."

"Correct."

The legal adviser, who had joined them, examined the instruction.

"It strengthens the evidence that the contribution was intended to satisfy part of the guarantee."

"But not the rest."

"Correct."

Seraphina nodded.

The payment history was becoming clearer.

The legal status was not.

The Royal Agricultural Council responded to the inquiry about Northgate Historical Services.

Its letter was polite and surprisingly direct.

The council confirmed that Northgate had submitted a consultation paper under the name Northern Development Historical Review Committee.

The company had been listed as a supporting archival organization.

However, the council had not appointed it as a representative.

It had not authorized the company to collect private estate documents.

It had not asked the company to conduct liability assessments.

The consultation paper had proposed the creation of a voluntary historical records service.

The council had not accepted the proposal.

The letter also stated that the council had received no formal request from Northgate to act on its behalf.

Seraphina placed the letter down.

"That answers one question."

Cedric nodded.

"Northgate was involved in the consultation, but it was not authorized to represent the council."

"Correct."

"Should we send this to the other estates?"

"Only the families that received the letters. They should know the company was not acting for the council."

Cedric made a note.

Seraphina continued.

"Do not call it fraud. We do not know whether the letters were intended to mislead anyone."

"Understood."

The distinction was important.

Northgate was a legitimate registered company.

It had been granted permission to inspect historical records.

It had submitted a consultation paper.

But it had also sent letters to estates requesting private documents under an unverified association name.

Its conduct deserved scrutiny.

It did not yet establish criminal intent.

A meeting with Northgate was arranged for the following week.

The company agreed to send two representatives.

The first was **Mr Adrian North**, a former regional legal clerk and one of the company's directors.

The second was **Mrs Lydia Voss**, an archival consultant responsible for the historical records project.

They arrived at the Veyr administrative hall on Tuesday morning.

Cedric received them.

Seraphina attended with the legal adviser and Roland.

Mr North was in his early sixties, carefully dressed, and visibly uncomfortable.

Mrs Voss appeared more relaxed.

After the introductions, the legal adviser placed the correspondence on the table.

"We would like to clarify the purpose of your letters to House Veyr and several neighboring estates."

Mr North nodded.

"Of course."

The legal adviser continued.

"Your company requested copies of historical guarantees and described the work as a regional liability assessment."

"That is correct."

"Who authorized the assessment?"

Mrs Voss answered.

"We were conducting a historical verification project."

"On whose behalf?"

"Our company."

"Not the Royal Agricultural Council?"

"No."

"Not the Treasury?"

"No."

"Not the regional administration?"

"No."

The legal adviser nodded.

"Then why did your letters refer to a Northern Development Records Association?"

Mr North glanced at Mrs Voss.

She answered.

"It was the working name of the historical review project."

"Was it a registered organization?"

"No. It was a project title."

"Why did you use a seal?"

Mrs Voss folded her hands.

"The seal was intended to distinguish the correspondence from ordinary company letters."

Seraphina looked at her.

"Did you consider that recipients might believe it was an official body?"

Mrs Voss paused.

"That was not our intention."

"But it was a foreseeable interpretation."

Mr North nodded slowly.

"Yes. In retrospect, the wording could have been clearer."

The room remained quiet.

Then Mrs Voss spoke.

"We should have used our company name and explained that the request was voluntary. We did not intend to imply government authority."

The legal adviser made a note.

Seraphina asked the next question.

"Have you contacted other estates beyond the three identified in the report?"

Mrs Voss consulted her folder.

"Yes. We sent requests to twelve estates."

"Twelve?"

"Across the northern region."

"How many replied?"

"Five."

"How many provided documents?"

"Two provided copies. One provided a summary. The other two declined."

"Did you make any payment demands?"

"No."

"Did you publish any liability figures?"

"No."

"Did you send the figures to the newspapers?"

Mrs Voss looked at Mr North.

"No," he said. "We have not provided our report to any newspaper."

Seraphina considered that.

The articles had described the northern estates as divided over central records.

Northgate's work concerned historical guarantees.

The two subjects might be related through the council's consultation.

But there was no evidence that the company had supplied information to the newspapers.

"Who has access to your inspection report?" she asked.

Mrs Voss listed the company's two directors, the project consultant, and the Treasury officer who had supervised the inspection.

"Anyone else?"

"No."

"Has the report been submitted to the Royal Agricultural Council?"

"No. It was prepared for our internal research."

"Then why was it referenced in your correspondence?"

"Because we wanted to verify the figures before completing it."

Seraphina nodded.

That answer was plausible.

It was not entirely satisfying.

But it was a beginning.

The meeting continued for another hour.

Northgate explained that its project had begun after the Royal Agricultural Council published its consultation notice.

The company believed that historical guarantees were poorly documented.

It wanted to demonstrate that a private archival service could help estates locate and preserve their old agreements.

The consultation paper proposed that participating estates could voluntarily submit document references.

Northgate would then identify missing records, compare surviving copies, and prepare summaries.

The company hoped to receive a contract if the council eventually established such a service.

That was the commercial interest.

It was not illegal.

But it explained why Northgate had approached the estates before any formal programme existed.

Seraphina listened carefully.

"Did you disclose that commercial interest in the consultation paper?"

Mr North opened the document.

It stated that Northgate Historical Services was a supporting archival organization.

It did not explicitly state that the company hoped to obtain a future contract.

The legal adviser noticed the omission.

"Did the council know?"

"We mentioned it during an informal discussion," Mr North said.

"Is there a written record?"

"Not that I know of."

Seraphina looked at the consultation paper.

The company had a legitimate reason to research historical guarantees.

It had also been positioning itself to benefit from a future public contract.

The problem was not that it had a commercial interest.

The problem was that its correspondence had not made the nature of that interest clear.

Nor had it distinguished private research from official assessment.

Seraphina leaned forward.

"Will you correct the letters?"

Mrs Voss nodded.

"Yes."

"To all twelve estates?"

"Yes."

"Will you clarify that your company was not acting for the Royal Agricultural Council or the Treasury?"

"Certainly."

"And will you stop describing the work as a regional liability assessment unless you have authority to do so?"

Mr North nodded.

"Agreed."

The legal adviser added that the company should provide a complete list of the documents it had examined and identify any copies it retained.

Mr North agreed.

The meeting ended without accusations.

It also ended without a resolution of the Veyr guarantee.

That evening, Cedric brought Seraphina the draft letter from Northgate.

It apologized for the unclear wording.

It confirmed that the company had not been authorized by the Crown or the Royal Agricultural Council.

It also stated that the 2,400-crown figure represented an unverified potential balance, not a confirmed debt.

Seraphina read it.

"Good."

Cedric nodded.

"They will send the correction to all twelve estates."

"Make sure we receive confirmation."

"Yes."

He placed another document beside it.

"The Treasury has found the final payment schedule."

Seraphina looked up.

"Already?"

"Yes. It was stored separately from the original guarantee."

She opened the document.

The schedule listed the original project.

The two roads had been completed.

The bridge had passed inspection.

The water-control structure had been suspended.

The final accounts showed that the project had been formally divided into completed and incomplete sections.

A closing statement had been issued for the completed works.

The remaining funds had been returned to the Treasury.

The schedule also recorded that the uncompleted water-control structure had been removed from the development programme.

Seraphina looked at the final line.

**Outstanding estate contributions to be reconciled against certified expenditure.**

There was no figure.

No list of guarantors.

No release certificate.

But the project had been formally divided.

The legal adviser examined the document the following morning.

"This may be sufficient to establish that the original guarantee was no longer capable of being called for the uncompleted section," he said. "However, I would like to examine the formal closure order."

"Why?"

"Because the guarantee refers to the Northern Development Committee's final certification. The Treasury's closing statement is important, but it may not be the same as the committee's legal release."

Seraphina nodded.

"So we are close."

"Closer."

She looked at Cedric.

He was smiling.

She did not trust the expression.

"Do not celebrate."

"I have said nothing."

"Your face has."

Cedric looked offended.

The formal closure order arrived two days later.

It was dated thirty-nine years earlier.

The Northern Development Committee had formally closed the original programme after the water-control structure was removed from the approved works.

The order confirmed that the completed sections had been certified.

It also directed the Treasury to reconcile all estate contributions against the final expenditure.

A separate schedule recorded the contributions received.

House Veyr's 1,600 crowns were listed.

The final expenditure allocated to the Veyr guarantee was also listed.

It amounted to 1,600 crowns.

The guarantee had been fully satisfied.

The remaining 2,400 crowns had never become payable.

The order stated that all conditional guarantees associated with the abandoned water-control structure were released.

Seraphina read the final paragraph.

Then she read it again.

The legal adviser smiled.

"That settles the question."

Roland examined the schedule.

"House Veyr paid exactly what was required."

"Yes."

"And the remaining amount?"

"Never due."

Cedric let out a long breath.

"Then we owe nothing."

The legal adviser nodded.

"Correct."

Seraphina looked at the closure order.

A forty-one-year-old guarantee had been found.

Its payment had been traced.

Its final accounts had been located.

Its legal release had been confirmed.

The matter was finally settled.

She felt a degree of satisfaction.

Then she noticed the date on the closure order.

It had been issued thirty-nine years earlier.

Yet the Treasury's historical commitments register had never been updated.

The original guarantee remained listed as unresolved.

The private company had found it and calculated a possible remaining exposure.

The company had not found the closure order.

Neither had the Treasury's own records system.

Seraphina looked at the legal adviser.

"Can we request that the historical register be corrected?"

"Certainly."

"With the closure order attached."

"Yes."

"And the original guarantee should be marked as released, not deleted."

He nodded.

"That is the correct approach. The original record should remain, with the later closure attached."

Seraphina turned toward Cedric.

"Send copies to the Treasury and the Allocation Office."

"Yes."

"And notify Lord Bellmont and Lord Marlowe that the Veyr guarantee is resolved."

Cedric wrote it down.

The legal adviser gathered his papers.

"There is one final matter."

Seraphina looked at him.

"What?"

"The Northern Development Committee's closure order lists five other estate guarantees."

She stared at him.

"Five?"

"Yes."

"Are they all unresolved?"

"Not according to the closure order. They were released or reconciled."

"Then why mention them?"

"Because the historical register lists three of them as incomplete."

Seraphina slowly leaned back.

The original guarantee had been resolved.

But the larger collection still contained records whose status had not been updated.

Northgate had not invented the problem.

It had found a real administrative gap.

It had simply described that gap in a way that could mislead people into believing they owed money.

Seraphina looked at the closure order.

"How many estates are affected?"

"Three."

She closed her eyes.

Cedric made no attempt to smile.

He had learned.

The correction was issued the following week.

Northgate sent its revised letters to all twelve estates.

The company clarified that it had no official authority and that its historical assessment did not establish legal debts.

The Royal Agricultural Council also issued a notice stating that no private organization had been appointed to collect historical estate documents on its behalf.

The Treasury corrected the Veyr guarantee's status.

The historical record remained intact, but the final closure order was attached.

The 2,400 crowns were marked as never payable.

Lord Bellmont confirmed that his own guarantee had been discharged.

Lord Marlowe's legal adviser found the final release document for the old drainage project.

Two other estates discovered similar closure records.

One had been formally released.

The other had been reconciled against a completed project.

The regional office began reviewing the remaining historical entries.

The matter did not become a scandal.

It did, however, expose a weakness in the way old obligations were recorded.

The Treasury's register had preserved original guarantees but had not consistently attached their final closure documents.

The result was a collection of unresolved entries that looked more alarming than they were.

Northgate had noticed the problem.

Its commercial ambitions had influenced how it approached the estates.

Its correspondence had been careless.

But the underlying records problem was real.

Seraphina found that distinction important.

It was possible for a company to behave irresponsibly while identifying a genuine administrative weakness.

It was also possible for an administrative weakness to exist without anyone deliberately creating it.

Neither conclusion required a villain.

Unfortunately, the newspapers were unlikely to find that particularly interesting.

The *Aurelion Morning Chronicle* published a short article two days later.

**NORTHERN GUARANTEE REVIEW FINDS NO OUTSTANDING VEYR DEBT**

The article correctly stated that House Veyr had paid its required contribution and that the remaining amount had never become payable.

It also reported that the Treasury was reviewing several historical entries.

However, it described Northgate Historical Services as a private organization that had uncovered a possible liability dispute.

Seraphina read the article at breakfast.

"At least this one is accurate."

Evelyne looked at the page.

"Mostly."

"What is wrong with it?"

"It makes the company sound more important than it was."

Seraphina glanced at the article again.

That was true.

The company had not uncovered the final closure order.

It had identified an unresolved entry and approached the estate.

The decisive document had been found through the Treasury archive.

Evelyne placed the newspaper down.

"Will you request a correction?"

"No."

"Why not?"

"Because the important facts are correct. I don't need every sentence to describe our work exactly."

Evelyne smiled.

"That is a sensible answer."

Seraphina returned to her breakfast.

For once, the matter appeared to be ending.

Then a messenger arrived.

He carried a letter from the Royal Agricultural Council.

It was addressed to the Duke.

Alaric opened it after breakfast.

The council thanked House Veyr for providing the closure order and the supporting records.

It also requested permission to examine the remaining historical guarantees identified in the Northern Development Committee's final schedule.

The purpose was to determine whether the Treasury's historical register required wider correction.

The request was voluntary.

It did not ask House Veyr to lead the review.

It did not ask the estate to collect documents.

It simply requested permission to use the Veyr guarantee as a redacted example of how an original instrument and final closure order should be recorded together.

Seraphina read the letter.

"That seems reasonable."

Alaric nodded.

"Would you object?"

"No. Provided the private financial details are removed and the original documents remain under the estate's control."

"Then we can approve it."

Seraphina handed the letter back.

The review of the old guarantee had ended.

The administrative consequences were only beginning.

But they were consequences that belonged to the proper offices.

She had no intention of becoming responsible for all of them.

She stood.

"Where are you going?" Evelyne asked.

"To my room."

"You have a meeting with Lady Bellmont this afternoon."

Seraphina stopped.

She had forgotten.

Evelyne smiled.

"And dinner next week."

Seraphina looked toward her father.

Alaric suddenly became very interested in the newspaper.

"Father."

He turned a page.

"Yes?"

"Did you know about this?"

"I was informed."

"And you did not tell me?"

"I believed your mother had already done so."

Evelyne rose.

"I did."

Seraphina looked between them.

The historical guarantee had been resolved.

The Treasury was correcting its records.

Northgate had withdrawn its misleading correspondence.

The council had requested a redacted example.

And somehow, her parents had arranged two social obligations without requiring a single written authorization.

She returned to her chair.

"Fine."

Evelyne smiled.

"Wonderful."

Seraphina picked up her tea.

It was still warm.

For the first time in several days, she managed to finish it before another document arrived.
