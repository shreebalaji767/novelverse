# **Chapter 93 — The Night the Reserve Could Not Keep Up**

The first warning arrived at six minutes past seven in the evening.

It was not an alarm.

It was a discrepancy.

In the Royal Academy's monitoring room, a narrow brass indicator moved one division beyond the range recorded during the previous observation period. The technician responsible for the western distribution zone checked the reading, adjusted the measuring instrument, and checked it again.

The indicator remained where it was.

"Record the time," he said.

His assistant wrote it down.

"Any change in the reserve?"

"Within normal response."

"Central assembly?"

"Still responding slowly."

The technician looked at the observation board.

Seven zones were represented by seven columns. Each contained readings from the current monitoring period, with separate spaces for pressure, temperature, circuit response and reserve contribution.

The board had become increasingly crowded.

There were no red marks.

There were, however, more corrections than there had been the previous evening.

"Notify Master Arkwright," the technician said.

His assistant hesitated.

"Immediately?"

"Would you prefer to wait until the indicator becomes more interesting?"

"No, Master."

"Then immediately."

Seraphina was in the royal administrative building when Lucien found her.

She had been reading a summary of the Academy's observational assessment. It contained six pages of measurements, three pages of explanations and one page explaining why the measurements should not be interpreted as evidence of a fault.

She had read the last page first.

It had not improved her mood.

Lucien entered without knocking.

"Something has changed."

Seraphina lowered the report.

"Has something broken?"

"Not yet."

"That is a remarkably reassuring answer."

"The western zone has registered a deviation beyond its usual observation range. The northern and southern zones have also begun responding more slowly under increased demand."

"Is the reserve compensating?"

"Yes."

"Then what has changed?"

Lucien placed a folded sheet on the table.

"The reserve is taking longer to compensate."

Seraphina looked at the figures.

The difference was small. The reserve had responded within its permitted operating range, but the response had begun later than expected. The central assembly had also taken longer to stabilize the distribution.

She read the recorded times twice.

"How long?"

"Several seconds beyond the recent average."

"That doesn't sound catastrophic."

"It isn't."

"Then why are you here?"

"Because the readings are becoming less consistent."

Lucien pulled out a chair.

"The network was designed to tolerate ordinary fluctuations. The reserve exists partly for that reason. But the reserve is not supposed to spend every evening correcting the same irregularities."

Seraphina looked at the observation sheet again.

"Have you changed anything?"

"No."

"Has anyone?"

"No adjustments have been made since the order to maintain the existing configuration."

"Good."

Lucien seemed almost offended by the question.

Seraphina placed the sheet beside the report.

"Then tell me what you need."

"Nothing from you technically."

"Excellent."

"But I need the Royal Infrastructure Department to confirm whether the evening demand schedule has changed."

"Why?"

"Because the variations began during the same period in which the capital's evening demand increased."

"That sounds like an ordinary explanation."

"It might be."

"You're saying it as though ordinary explanations are personally insulting."

Lucien ignored that.

"The capital has had unusually high demand for three evenings. Public buildings, hospitals, the administrative district and the southern residential quarter are all drawing from the network."

"Why?"

"The cold."

Seraphina glanced toward the window.

The weather had deteriorated during the afternoon. Rain had turned to sleet, and the wind had begun pushing against the palace windows.

"People are using more heating circuits."

"Yes."

"Then the network is under greater demand."

"Yes."

"And the network is responding more slowly."

"Yes."

Seraphina folded her hands.

"That is not a complicated mystery."

"No," Lucien said. "The complicated part is that we cannot yet determine whether the demand is exposing a maintenance problem, an inadequate operating margin, or both."

"Then don't decide until you know."

He gave her a tired look.

"That is what I have been telling everyone."

"Perhaps you should say it more loudly."

The Crown Prince arrived twenty minutes later.

Theodore had already received the Academy's preliminary notification. He listened while Lucien explained the latest observations, then examined the readings himself.

"Has the reserve reached its maximum contribution?"

"No," Lucien replied.

"How close?"

"Not close enough to justify an emergency shutdown. But closer than it should be at this stage of demand."

"Can the network continue operating?"

"Under the present configuration, yes. We need to monitor it continuously."

Theodore turned to the Royal Infrastructure Department coordinator.

"Are there any scheduled changes tonight?"

"None, Your Highness."

"Any planned maintenance?"

"Only routine inspection of the southern distribution chamber. It has been postponed."

"Good. Nothing is to be isolated without the Academy's approval."

He turned to Lucien.

"Can the affected zones be monitored independently?"

"Yes. We have additional measuring teams."

"Arrange it."

Then he looked at Seraphina.

"Do you see anything in the administrative records that could explain the timing?"

Seraphina did not answer immediately.

She had not been asked to investigate the network. Her role was still limited to reviewing records and identifying inconsistencies. She had no intention of turning a narrow administrative assignment into an unofficial position of responsibility.

"I can compare the demand schedule with the maintenance records," she said. "But I cannot tell you whether a technical explanation is correct."

"I understand."

"And I will not authorize any changes."

"I am not asking you to."

"Good. I would prefer not to become responsible for something I cannot repair."

Theodore looked at the observation board.

"At present, I would prefer that nobody attempt to repair anything without knowing what is wrong."

That was sensible.

Unfortunately, sensible instructions did not always survive contact with frightened people.

At eight minutes past eight, the first interruption occurred.

It lasted eleven seconds.

The northern administrative quarter reported a brief reduction in protective illumination along two streets. The lamps dimmed, recovered, and remained stable.

No buildings lost their protective barriers.

No one was injured.

The city watch initially attributed the incident to a local lighting fault. A second report, received four minutes later, indicated that the same disturbance had occurred in a small section of the western residential quarter.

This time, the Academy recognized the pattern.

The reserve had compensated for the northern variation.

It had not compensated quickly enough for the western one.

The protective network had not failed completely. Instead, two local sections had experienced brief reductions in their protective output before the reserve restored their readings.

Lucien read the reports.

"Both incidents occurred during the same demand increase."

"Can you isolate the cause?" Theodore asked.

"Not yet."

"Is the network safe?"

"The majority of the system remains within its approved limits. The affected sections have recovered."

"Then keep the reserve active."

"I intend to."

A messenger entered with another report.

This one concerned the southern quarter.

A protective lamp above the entrance of a public infirmary had gone dark. The local ward remained functional, but the outer protective line had fluctuated twice.

Theodore stood.

"Notify the city watch. Have the infirmary checked."

Lucien was already moving.

Seraphina looked at the report.

"How many people are in that infirmary?"

The messenger consulted the attached note.

"Thirty-one patients, Your Ladyship. Including several children."

"Are they being moved?"

"No, my lady. The building itself is unaffected."

"Then don't move them unnecessarily. Have the infirmary staff informed, and ensure the entrances remain accessible."

Theodore nodded.

"Send an officer to confirm that."

The messenger bowed and left.

For a moment, nobody spoke.

Then Lucien said, "I need to return to the Academy."

"Go," Theodore replied.

The prince turned to the coordinator.

"Prepare an emergency communication channel between the Academy, the city watch, the infirmaries and the district offices. I want reports sent directly, not summarized through three departments."

The coordinator hurried away.

Seraphina watched the room empty.

She had the uncomfortable impression that the kingdom had spent decades constructing a system in which every office knew one part of the truth, and now the entire capital was waiting for those offices to learn how to speak to one another.

The next hour was not dramatic.

It was worse.

It was busy.

The city watch began checking the affected streets. The Royal Academy sent technical teams to the nearest observation points. The Infrastructure Department instructed local maintenance crews to avoid touching any component connected to the central network.

The hospitals and infirmaries received instructions to keep ordinary lighting and non-magical heating available wherever possible.

The Church offered additional rooms in its southern charitable buildings as temporary shelter.

Liora arrived at the administrative building shortly before nine.

Her cloak was damp, and Nessa followed her carrying a bundle of reports.

"I was told the southern infirmary had experienced a disruption," Liora said.

"It experienced a brief reduction in its outer protective line," Seraphina replied. "The building remains functional."

"I have already spoken with Sister Agnes. She is checking the patients and arranging additional lamps."

"Good."

"Several elderly patients are frightened. They remember the previous winter's circuit interruptions."

Seraphina nodded.

"Can the infirmary continue operating normally?"

"Yes. Its medical rooms are not dependent on the protective circuit for ordinary lighting. They have reserve lamps and manual equipment."

"Then keep the patients where they are unless the staff decide otherwise."

Liora looked at the report in her hands.

"The Church can provide two additional shelter rooms if the city needs them."

"Tell the prince's office directly."

"I have."

"Then you're ahead of me."

Liora gave her a small, tired smile.

"That happens more often than you think."

Nessa placed another document on the table.

"These are the local reports. The western district has requested additional lamps. The northern district has asked whether the protective lines will be restored before midnight."

"Has anyone promised them that?"

"No."

"Good."

Nessa looked relieved.

Seraphina read the reports.

The western district's request included a list of public buildings, street sections and households with elderly residents. The northern district had submitted a map showing the locations of the dimmed lamps.

The reports were useful.

They were also incomplete.

"Who is coordinating the distribution of ordinary lamps?"

"The city stores, I believe."

"Do they have enough?"

Nessa checked her notes.

"That information has not been confirmed."

Seraphina looked at the coordinator's empty desk.

"Then we should confirm it."

Liora glanced toward the prince's office.

"I can ask the Church to provide some."

"Do that. But don't send supplies without recording where they go."

Liora raised an eyebrow.

"Even during an emergency?"

"Especially during an emergency. Otherwise tomorrow we will have three lists claiming the same lamps were delivered to three different places."

Nessa quietly wrote something down.

Liora sighed.

"I suppose that is fair."

"It is not a particularly attractive quality of mine."

"No," Liora agreed. "But it is useful."

At half past nine, the capital's protective network experienced its first sustained disturbance.

The southern zone's output began to decline.

The western zone followed.

The central assembly responded, but its stabilization time increased beyond the range recorded during the earlier assessment.

The reserve contribution rose.

For thirty-two seconds, the network operated close to the reserve's permitted response margin.

Then the readings stabilized.

The Academy's monitoring room became silent.

Master Alistair Fenwick stood beside the main observation board.

"Do not adjust anything," he said.

Nobody moved.

"Confirm the readings independently."

Two technicians checked the central measurements. A third compared the reserve's contribution against the secondary instrument.

The figures matched.

Lucien stood at the end of the table, his expression unusually still.

"That was not a local fluctuation," he said.

"No," Fenwick replied.

"Three zones."

"Yes."

"The central response was delayed."

"Yes."

"And the reserve corrected it."

"For now."

Lucien looked at the board.

The word *now* seemed to occupy more space than it deserved.

A technician approached.

"Master Fenwick, we have a request from the southern maintenance team."

"What request?"

"They want permission to inspect the replacement stabilizing assembly."

"Not yet."

"They believe the assembly may be retaining residue."

Fenwick considered the report.

"We have already identified the maintenance gap. That does not mean the assembly is the cause of tonight's disturbance."

"They say the inspection can be performed under isolation."

"Then they can prepare the isolation plan. They will not carry it out until the Academy confirms that the affected section can be safely separated."

The technician bowed.

Lucien looked at Fenwick.

"If the residue is accumulating across multiple components, we may have a common maintenance problem."

"Possibly."

"And if the components are all responding more slowly—"

"We still need evidence that they share the same failure mechanism."

Lucien stopped.

Fenwick was correct.

The three affected zones did not have identical components, installation histories or maintenance conditions. Treating them as one problem might save time, but it could also lead to a repair that addressed only the most visible symptom.

"Prepare a comparative inspection," Fenwick ordered. "Central, western and southern assemblies. Record the measurements before touching anything."

The technician departed.

Lucien remained at the board.

"How long can the reserve continue at this rate?"

Fenwick did not answer immediately.

"That depends on the demand and the response of the central assembly."

"That isn't a duration."

"It is the only honest answer I have."

At ten o'clock, the King convened an emergency council.

He did not call every noble in the capital.

He called the officials responsible for the network, the city, public safety and the available resources.

Theodore presented the situation.

Three zones had experienced irregular responses. The central assembly was slower than expected. The reserve remained functional but had approached its permitted response margin during the latest disturbance.

The protective network was still operating.

The affected districts had been notified.

The hospitals and infirmaries remained open.

The King listened without interruption.

Then he asked the Academy director, "Can you guarantee that the network will remain stable through the night?"

Fenwick answered carefully.

"No, Your Majesty. We can confirm that it remains within its approved limits at present. We cannot guarantee how it will respond to a further increase in demand."

The King nodded.

"Then what do you recommend?"

"Maintain the present configuration. Reduce unnecessary demand where possible. Keep the reserve active. Continue independent monitoring of all seven zones."

"Can demand be reduced without affecting hospitals or public safety?"

"Yes. Nonessential ceremonial buildings, unused administrative wings and several public demonstration facilities can reduce their consumption."

Theodore looked toward the Treasury minister.

"Can the city issue those instructions?"

Minister Garrick nodded.

"Through the appropriate district authorities."

"Do it."

The King turned to the Royal Infrastructure Department.

"Can you provide the necessary maintenance materials?"

The department's representative hesitated.

"Some materials are available in central stores. Others are held under separate project allocations."

"How long to assemble the required quantities?"

"We need to confirm the stock records."

The King looked at him.

"Confirm them tonight."

The representative bowed.

Seraphina sat at the far end of the table.

She had not intended to attend the council. Theodore had asked her to bring the administrative comparison she had prepared, and she had been unable to find a convincing reason to refuse without wasting everyone's time.

She placed the document before the Treasury minister.

"This is the current schedule of maintenance materials and replacement components."

Garrick looked through it.

"You have separated the materials by zone."

"By zone, supplier, recorded quantity and maintenance requirement."

"Why?"

"Because the previous summary combined them into one total."

"Was that incorrect?"

"It was mathematically correct."

Garrick waited.

"It was also useless."

The King glanced at the document.

Seraphina continued.

"The central and western assemblies have different replacement histories. The southern assembly has a separate supplier note. The northern component has a complete maintenance schedule. If the stores office treats all four as identical, it may report sufficient stock while lacking the specific material required for a particular assembly."

The Infrastructure Department representative leaned forward.

"Are you suggesting the inventory is inaccurate?"

"I'm suggesting the summary doesn't distinguish between different materials."

"That is not the same thing."

"Exactly."

The man looked at the document again.

The King tapped the table once.

"Then have the stores office provide a separate inventory by component type and intended use."

The representative bowed.

Seraphina returned to her seat.

She had not discovered anything.

She had simply made it harder for the wrong quantity to look reassuring.

At eleven minutes past eleven, the central stores report arrived.

It contained a problem.

Not a dramatic one.

A missing quantity.

The stores office had recorded a supply of stabilizing catalyst for the central protective network eighteen months earlier. The original dispatch record listed forty-eight sealed containers.

The central receipt listed forty-eight.

The current inventory listed thirty-one.

The maintenance schedule recorded fourteen containers used during scheduled work.

That left three containers unaccounted for.

The stores clerk had classified the difference as a stock adjustment.

The adjustment had been entered in the general materials ledger without a separate movement reference.

Seraphina read the figures.

"Three containers."

Theodore looked at the report.

"Could that explain the disturbance?"

"I don't know."

Lucien took the page from her.

"Which catalyst?"

The stores representative checked the attached description.

"Grade C-4 stabilizing compound."

Lucien's expression changed.

"That is used in the central assembly and two of the regional replacement components."

"Is it used in the western and southern zones?" Theodore asked.

"Potentially. It depends on the assembly specification."

Fenwick took the report.

"Do not assume the missing quantity was used there."

"I wasn't."

Lucien looked at the stores representative.

"Do you have the original issue records?"

"Only the summary."

"Then find the original."

The representative nodded.

"Tonight?"

"Yes."

The man looked as though he had hoped the answer might be different.

The King spoke before anyone else could.

"Find it."

The stores investigation did not immediately reveal theft.

It revealed three different descriptions of the same material.

The original procurement record described forty-eight containers of C-4 stabilizing compound.

The central receipt described forty-eight sealed catalyst containers.

A later maintenance schedule described fourteen units of stabilizing material consumed.

The stores ledger described the remaining quantity as thirty-one containers.

A separate project note referred to three containers transferred for technical evaluation.

There was no corresponding return entry.

There was also no evidence that the three containers had been installed in the network.

The transfer reference was incomplete.

"Where were they sent?" Theodore asked.

The clerk examined the paper.

"Technical Evaluation Store, Your Highness."

"Where is that?"

The clerk looked at the Infrastructure Department representative.

The representative looked at the Academy director.

Fenwick closed his eyes briefly.

"That store was reorganized."

"Then where are its records?"

"We will have to establish which office inherited them."

Seraphina stared at the transfer line.

Three containers had been removed from the inventory.

The ledger had recorded the adjustment.

The project note had recorded a transfer.

But the destination had not been reconciled.

It was not proof that the material was missing in the ordinary sense.

It was proof that nobody in the room could presently say where it was.

"Could the transfer have been for the current maintenance programme?" she asked.

Lucien examined the date.

"Possibly. The evaluation was conducted before the central assembly's replacement lining was approved."

"Then it may be related to the material that is retaining residue."

"Possibly," Lucien repeated.

He looked at the Academy director.

"We need to identify the evaluation batch."

Fenwick nodded.

"Without assuming it was used."

"Agreed."

The King turned toward the Treasury minister.

"Can the stores investigation proceed separately from the technical assessment?"

"Yes, Your Majesty."

"Then keep them separate. I want both reports, and I want the distinction preserved."

Garrick bowed.

Seraphina looked down at the document again.

Three containers.

A transfer.

An incomplete reference.

She had seen enough administrative problems to know that a missing entry could be more dangerous than a missing object.

At least an object could be found.

Near midnight, the city received another report.

The southern protective line had stabilized.

The western zone remained under observation.

The central assembly continued responding more slowly than its earlier operating standard, but it had not crossed its permitted limit.

The reserve remained active.

The northern zone was stable.

The Academy recommended maintaining the reduced demand schedule until morning.

The King approved it.

The council began to disperse.

Lucien remained behind, reading the latest measurements.

Seraphina approached the table.

"Will the network last until morning?"

"I don't know."

"That seems to be everyone's answer tonight."

"It is better than pretending."

She considered the observation board.

"Do you think the catalyst is responsible?"

"I think the missing material deserves investigation. I also think the central assembly's residue deserves attention. But we have not established that either explains the variations across all three zones."

"Could they be separate problems?"

"Yes."

"Could they be connected?"

"Yes."

Seraphina sighed.

"You're making this very difficult to summarize."

Lucien looked at her.

"Would you prefer that I choose one explanation?"

"No. I would prefer that the kingdom had fewer complicated problems."

"That would be convenient."

"Extremely."

A quiet knock sounded at the door.

A young Academy assistant entered carrying a narrow envelope.

"Master Arkwright. The Technical Evaluation Store has located an older transfer index."

Lucien took it.

The envelope contained a single sheet.

The assistant explained, "The index refers to three containers transferred from central stores to the former Eastern Materials Laboratory. The transfer was recorded under an evaluation number, not the catalyst's procurement number."

"Is the laboratory still operating?" Lucien asked.

"No, Master. It was closed six years ago."

"Where are its materials?"

"The closure inventory says they were transferred to the Academy's secondary stores."

"Then why were they not returned to central inventory?"

The assistant hesitated.

"The closure inventory lists them as technical samples."

Lucien looked at the sheet.

"Does it identify the batch?"

"Not on this page."

"Find the laboratory's original sample register."

"Yes, Master."

The assistant bowed and left.

Lucien read the index again.

Seraphina looked over his shoulder.

The transfer had occurred eighteen months earlier.

The laboratory had received three containers for evaluation.

Its closure inventory had listed the material as technical samples.

There was no evidence of installation.

There was no evidence of disposal.

There was no evidence that the material had been returned to general stores.

It was an ordinary administrative explanation.

It was also incomplete.

"At least the containers may still exist," Seraphina said.

Lucien folded the sheet.

"That is what I hope."

"Don't hope too much. It makes the disappointment expensive."

He gave her a tired look.

She picked up her report.

"Good night, Master Arkwright."

"Lady Seraphina."

She paused at the doorway.

"Yes?"

"If the network experiences another disturbance, we may need additional assistance with the records and supply coordination."

Seraphina looked at him.

"Ask the prince."

"I intend to."

"Then why are you telling me?"

"Because the stores records are already in your hands."

She considered this.

"That is not an appointment."

"No."

"Or an agreement to supervise anything."

"No."

"Good."

She left.

At the palace's eastern entrance, the rain had turned to snow.

The city beyond the gates was still illuminated, though several streets had fewer protective lamps than usual. Ordinary lanterns had been placed outside public buildings. Watchmen stood at intersections, directing pedestrians away from the affected sections.

Nothing looked like the end of the world.

That was perhaps the most unsettling part.

The capital was still functioning.

People were still going home. Bakers were preparing their morning ovens. Hospital staff were changing shifts. The city watch was checking doors and windows.

The protective network had not collapsed.

But it had shown that the margin between normal operation and an uncomfortable interruption was narrower than anyone had believed.

Seraphina pulled her cloak closer.

A carriage waited beyond the gate.

Mira, who had remained awake despite Seraphina's repeated instructions to go home, opened the door.

"Your Ladyship."

"You're still here."

"Yes."

"I told you to rest."

"You also told me that you would be finished before ten."

Seraphina climbed inside.

"That was an optimistic estimate."

Mira closed the door.

"Should I tell the household to prepare for an emergency?"

"No. Tell them the capital's protective network is under observation. Nothing more."

"And if there is another interruption?"

"Then the relevant authorities will issue instructions."

Mira nodded.

The carriage began moving.

For several minutes, neither spoke.

Then Mira asked, "Will the city be safe?"

Seraphina looked out through the window.

"I hope so."

Mira turned toward her.

"That isn't a very comforting answer."

"It is an honest one."

The carriage passed a public building where two watchmen were replacing a dimmed protective lamp with an ordinary lantern.

A small crowd had gathered nearby.

Nobody was shouting.

Nobody was running.

The people were simply waiting for the light to return.

Seraphina watched until the carriage turned away.

At the Academy, seven columns of measurements continued to fill with numbers.

At the central stores, three containers of catalyst material remained somewhere in the records of a closed laboratory.

And beneath the administrative district, the capital's central assembly continued to respond a little more slowly than it had been designed to.

The reserve was still holding.

For tonight, that was enough.

It was not a solution.

It was only time.
