# Chapter 66 — The Guarantee That Had Outlived Its Signatories

The first difficulty with an old financial guarantee was discovering whether it had ever been a financial guarantee.

The second difficulty was discovering who had been expected to pay.

The third was discovering why anyone had thought it sensible to write the document in handwriting that resembled a frightened spider.

Seraphina encountered all three difficulties before breakfast.

The letter from Master Percival Dunn lay on her father's desk.

Beside it rested an old estate reference copied onto a separate sheet.

**VEY/FIN/41/08 — NORTHERN DEVELOPMENT GUARANTEE**

The reference was unfamiliar.

That was not particularly reassuring.

Seraphina had spent enough time in the Veyr archives to know that an unfamiliar reference could mean several things. It might refer to a missing document, an incorrectly indexed document, a document stored under another name, or a document that had never existed outside a clerk's imagination.

She preferred the fourth possibility.

It was usually the least expensive.

Unfortunately, the reference appeared in an old financial ledger.

That made it more difficult to dismiss.

"Do we have the ledger?" Alaric asked.

Cedric, who had arrived early, opened his notes.

"The reference appears in the northern accounts register from forty-one years ago. I found a second entry in the estate correspondence index, but it does not identify the recipient."

"What was the amount?" Seraphina asked.

"Not recorded."

"Then why call it a guarantee?"

Cedric looked at the letter.

"That is what the index calls it."

Seraphina leaned back.

"An index can call a horse a chair. It doesn't make sitting on it advisable."

Alaric looked at her.

Cedric tried not to smile.

"We should examine the original register first," Seraphina continued. "And the correspondence index. We should not assume that the reference describes a payment obligation."

Alaric nodded.

"Agreed."

He turned toward Cedric.

"Have the archive staff retrieve both volumes. Keep the documents in their original order."

"Yes, Your Grace."

"And contact Master Dunn. Ask him not to circulate the reference until we understand what it concerns."

Cedric wrote the instruction down.

Seraphina glanced at the letter again.

"Who requested the information from him?"

"That is what we need to discover," Alaric replied.

It was a sensible answer.

She disliked how many sensible answers were currently leading to more work.

The archive room was cold.

It was not particularly damp, but the stone walls retained the winter chill even during warmer months.

Seraphina arrived with Cedric and Roland Ashford, the ducal accountant.

Roland had been asked to examine the financial records because the reference appeared in an account register.

He looked at the two volumes placed on the table.

"Forty-one years old."

"Yes," Cedric replied.

"At least the bindings are intact."

Seraphina glanced at him.

"Is that important?"

"It means the pages are less likely to fall apart while we are reading them."

"An excellent beginning."

Roland ignored her.

The first volume was a large ledger with darkened pages and an uneven spine.

The second was a correspondence index, smaller but more carefully maintained.

Cedric opened the ledger to the relevant year.

He found the reference after several minutes.

The entry read:

*Northern development works — guarantee arrangement discussed with regional parties. Further correspondence to follow.*

There was no amount.

No recipient.

No payment date.

No description of the development works.

Seraphina read it again.

"That is not a guarantee."

Roland leaned closer.

"It could be a preliminary entry."

"Then it should say preliminary."

"It does say further correspondence to follow."

"It does not say what correspondence."

Roland turned the page.

The following entries concerned ordinary estate expenses.

The guarantee did not appear again in that volume.

Cedric opened the correspondence index.

The same reference appeared under a different heading.

**Regional Infrastructure and Waterworks.**

The entry was more detailed.

*Correspondence received from Northern Development Committee. House Veyr to provide limited guarantee subject to approved schedule and final agreement.*

Seraphina tapped the page.

"That is better."

Roland frowned.

"Not much."

"No. But it establishes that someone intended to discuss a guarantee."

Cedric checked the next line.

*Draft schedule referred to legal office.*

Then another entry:

*Execution copy requested.*

And finally:

*Original instrument to be retained by regional committee. Estate copy pending.*

The three of them looked at one another.

Roland spoke first.

"Do we have an estate copy?"

Cedric checked the following year's index.

"No."

"Then perhaps it was never completed."

"Perhaps," Seraphina said.

She looked at the wording.

"Or the estate copy was never delivered."

Roland sighed.

"Which means we cannot determine whether House Veyr guaranteed anything."

"Correct."

"Yet Master Dunn has received a request concerning it."

"Yes."

Roland closed the ledger carefully.

"That is unfortunate."

Seraphina looked at him.

"Why?"

"Because it suggests someone has found another document."

She considered the possibility.

That was true.

But it did not necessarily mean the document was enforceable.

A draft could be circulated for decades without becoming a contract.

A signed agreement could expire.

A guarantee could be limited to a specific project or amount.

And a reference could survive long after the obligation itself had ended.

The archive had established only one thing.

House Veyr had discussed providing a limited guarantee.

It had not established that the guarantee had been executed.

The first useful response came from Master Dunn that afternoon.

He had written a short letter.

The request he received had been delivered by post approximately three weeks earlier.

It had asked him to locate the original instrument associated with the reference.

The sender had identified himself as a representative of the Northern Development Records Association.

The letter carried no personal signature.

Instead, it bore an official-looking seal.

Master Dunn had not recognized the association.

He had also not received payment for the search.

He included a copy of the original request.

Cedric placed it on the table.

Seraphina read it.

The letter was polite.

It explained that the association was reviewing historical regional development agreements and wished to verify the current status of several guarantees.

It listed four references.

One belonged to House Veyr.

The other three belonged to neighboring estates.

There was no accusation.

No demand for payment.

No threat of legal action.

The writer simply asked for assistance in locating the relevant documents.

Seraphina turned the page.

"Who is the Northern Development Records Association?"

Cedric checked the seal.

"I do not know."

Roland examined the letter.

"The name sounds official."

"That is not the same as being official," Seraphina replied.

He nodded.

"Quite."

The letter contained a return address in Aurelion.

It also included a postal box number.

There was no street address.

Seraphina looked at Cedric.

"Ask the capital registry whether the association is registered."

"Under what category?"

"Any category. Legal association, charitable organization, commercial company, public body. We need to know whether it exists."

Cedric wrote it down.

"And the other references?"

"Ask Master Dunn whether he knows which estates they concern. Do not contact those estates yet."

Roland raised an eyebrow.

"Why not?"

"Because we do not know whether the request is legitimate. If we start asking around, we may spread an unnecessary rumor."

Roland considered that.

"Reasonable."

Seraphina returned to the letter.

There was something else.

The request had not asked whether the guarantees remained enforceable.

It had asked for their current status.

That suggested the sender might already possess copies of the original instruments.

Or might simply be conducting an archival review.

Neither possibility could be established from the letter alone.

She set it aside.

"Let's find out whether the association exists."

The capital registry replied two days later.

It had no record of an organization under the name Northern Development Records Association.

However, the registry clerk had identified a similarly named private correspondence group that had operated nearly twenty years earlier.

It had been called the Northern Development Historical Correspondence Society.

The society had been formed by several retired surveyors, former regional administrators, and private legal clerks.

Its purpose had been to preserve records relating to northern infrastructure projects.

It had dissolved eleven years earlier.

There was no evidence that the current letter had originated from it.

The registry also noted that the old society had never possessed authority to request official documents.

Seraphina read the reply.

"At least we know the name is not registered."

Cedric nodded.

"But it could be an informal group."

"Yes."

"Or someone using the old society's name."

"Yes."

Roland looked up from his notes.

"Or someone who simply made a mistake."

Seraphina nodded.

"That too."

She was becoming increasingly tired of possibilities.

The next question concerned the three other references.

Master Dunn replied that he had recognized two of them.

One belonged to Lord Benedict Marlowe.

The other belonged to the Bellmont estate.

The third was unfamiliar.

Cedric checked the references.

"Lord Marlowe's agreement appears to concern the old lower drainage works."

"That is not the same agreement we reviewed last year?"

"No. It predates it."

"And Bellmont?"

"An old transport guarantee."

Seraphina looked at him.

"Transport?"

"Possibly a road or bridge project. The reference does not provide details."

Roland frowned.

"That makes three separate estates and three separate types of work."

"Yes," Seraphina said.

"Which suggests the sender is reviewing a collection of regional guarantees rather than one particular dispute."

"Possibly."

Cedric looked at her.

"Should we contact Lord Bellmont?"

"Yes. But only to ask whether he has received the same request."

"Understood."

"Do not suggest that we suspect anything."

Cedric nodded.

The distinction was important.

A question was not an accusation.

And an old document was not evidence of a crime merely because someone had asked to see it.

Lord Bellmont responded the following morning.

He had received a similar letter.

His estate had been asked to verify a transport guarantee issued thirty-eight years earlier.

The letter was almost identical to the one sent to Master Dunn.

It included the same seal.

The same postal box.

The same description of the association.

Lord Bellmont had not replied.

He had instead sent the letter to his legal adviser.

The adviser had found an old contract reference in Bellmont's accounts.

Unlike the Veyr record, Bellmont had a copy of the executed agreement.

It concerned a bridge construction project shared by three estates.

The guarantee had been limited to the cost of construction.

The agreement had also included a completion date and a clause releasing the guarantors once the project had passed its final inspection.

The bridge had been completed.

The final inspection had been recorded.

The guarantee had been formally discharged thirty-four years earlier.

Lord Bellmont's letter ended with a simple question.

*Why would anyone be reviewing an obligation that had already expired?*

Seraphina read the sentence twice.

She could think of several reasons.

Historical research.

Legal verification.

An attempt to establish whether an old obligation had been discharged.

A clerical review.

Or something less innocent.

But the document itself did not suggest wrongdoing.

She wrote a reply.

She explained that House Veyr had found a preliminary reference but no executed instrument.

She also mentioned that the sender's claimed association did not appear in the current registry.

She recommended that both estates preserve the correspondence and seek legal advice before providing any private documents.

Cedric read the draft.

"Should we mention the Marlowe reference?"

"Only if Lord Bellmont already knows about it."

"He does not."

"Then no."

Cedric nodded.

Seraphina folded the letter.

There was no reason to turn three separate inquiries into a single public incident.

Not yet.

Lord Benedict Marlowe arrived at the Veyr estate two days later.

He was accompanied by his steward, Arthur Bell.

Seraphina received them in the smaller reception room.

Lord Marlowe looked tired.

"I assume you have heard about the letter."

"Yes."

He placed a folded document on the table.

"I received it yesterday."

Seraphina opened it.

The wording was identical.

The same request.

The same reference format.

The same seal.

Lord Marlowe sat down.

"My steward found the original agreement."

Arthur Bell placed a leather folder beside the letter.

"It concerns the construction of a southern drainage channel," he explained. "The agreement was made between three estates. Lord Marlowe's predecessor provided a limited guarantee for construction costs."

Seraphina looked at the document.

The agreement had been signed by the landowners.

It included an amount.

A schedule of work.

A completion requirement.

And a clause stating that the guarantee would expire once the final accounts had been settled.

The final accounts were attached.

They showed that the work had been completed and the costs reconciled.

There was also a discharge certificate.

Seraphina read it carefully.

"Then this guarantee was closed."

"Yes," Arthur Bell replied.

Lord Marlowe leaned forward.

"Why would someone ask for its current status?"

"That is what we are trying to understand."

"Do you think someone is attempting to revive old obligations?"

"We have no evidence of that."

Lord Marlowe frowned.

"Then why ask?"

"Because someone may be reviewing historical records."

"Why these records?"

Seraphina looked at the list.

Three estates.

Three different agreements.

All concerning development projects.

"That is the question."

Arthur Bell opened another folder.

"There is something else."

He produced a second letter.

This one was older.

It had been sent to the Marlowe estate approximately six months earlier.

The sender was not identified.

The letter asked whether Lord Marlowe's estate still recognized an obligation to maintain a section of the old southern channel.

Seraphina recognized the subject.

It concerned the lower channel arrangement reviewed in the previous year.

Lord Marlowe had answered that the estate maintained only the responsibilities set out in the current agreement.

The unknown correspondent had replied with a copy of an older document.

The copy was incomplete.

It showed an earlier maintenance arrangement but omitted the amendment that had later changed the responsibilities.

Lord Marlowe had asked his solicitor to examine it.

The solicitor had confirmed that the document was outdated.

Seraphina looked at the two letters.

"Did the second correspondent identify where the copy came from?"

"No," Arthur Bell replied.

"Did you ask?"

"Yes. No answer."

Lord Marlowe looked at Seraphina.

"I am beginning to dislike people who write letters without names."

"That is understandable."

"Can we do anything?"

"Preserve the correspondence. Keep the original agreements together with the amendments. If the sender makes a claim, ask them to identify the legal basis."

Lord Marlowe nodded.

"And the current request?"

"Do not provide the original document. A certified summary may be appropriate if your legal adviser agrees, but there is no reason to release private records to an unidentified organization."

Lord Marlowe leaned back.

"That is what my solicitor said."

"Then your solicitor is sensible."

Arthur Bell smiled faintly.

Lord Marlowe did not.

"I would prefer to know who is asking."

"So would I."

That evening, Cedric brought another document to Seraphina.

It was a copy of the original request sent to Master Dunn.

There was a small detail they had overlooked.

At the bottom of the letter, beneath the association's seal, was a reference number.

**NDR/REVIEW/06.**

Cedric placed another page beside it.

"This is a copy of a public notice from the Royal Agricultural Council."

Seraphina read the heading.

**PRELIMINARY CONSULTATION — HISTORICAL PROPERTY AND INFRASTRUCTURE RECORDS**

The notice had been published four months earlier.

It invited comments from legal offices, regional administrators, landowners, and historical societies about the preservation of old infrastructure agreements.

The purpose was to assess whether the council should recommend a voluntary system for indexing historical records.

The notice had not established a programme.

It had not requested private documents.

It had not created an organization.

Seraphina looked at the reference number again.

"Could NDR stand for Northern Development Records?"

"Possibly."

"Could this be connected to the consultation?"

"Possibly."

Cedric placed another page on the table.

It was a list of correspondence received by the council.

One entry caught Seraphina's attention.

**Submission 06 — Historical Guarantee Verification and Regional Record Preservation.**

The submission had been received from a private group calling itself the Northern Development Historical Review Committee.

The name was different from the association on Master Dunn's letter.

But the reference number was the same.

Seraphina read the description.

The committee had suggested that old regional guarantees be reviewed before any central index was established.

It argued that unresolved obligations could create uncertainty for landowners.

It also proposed that a private archival office should be permitted to collect copies of historical agreements for verification.

Seraphina placed the page down.

"Who submitted it?"

"The council record does not identify an individual."

"Was it accepted?"

"No. It was listed as a consultation submission."

"Did the council respond?"

"Not yet."

She leaned back.

The pieces were beginning to form a picture.

Not a complete one.

But enough to establish that the correspondence was not necessarily random.

Someone had submitted a proposal to the Royal Agricultural Council.

Someone had also sent letters to estates and retired legal clerks using a similar name.

The proposal concerned historical guarantees.

The letters requested information about historical guarantees.

The connection was plausible.

It was not proven.

Seraphina looked at Cedric.

"Does the council know that someone is contacting private estates?"

"I do not think so."

"Then they should."

Cedric nodded.

"Should we contact the committee directly?"

"Not until we know who its members are."

He looked at the submission again.

"There's a list of supporting organizations."

Seraphina took the page.

Three names were listed.

A retired surveyors' society.

A private legal archive.

And the Northern Landowners' Correspondence Group.

She stopped at the third name.

"Have you heard of this?"

"No."

"Neither have I."

Cedric examined the page.

"The name is similar to the old correspondence society."

"Yes."

He turned to the final paragraph.

"There's a contact address."

Seraphina read it.

The address was not in Aurelion.

It was in a northern market town.

The same town where the old regional development committee had once maintained its records.

She looked up.

"Do we know who owns the building?"

Cedric shook his head.

"No."

"Then ask the regional registry."

"Yes."

She returned to the submission.

The committee's proposal was not inherently unreasonable.

Historical guarantees could remain unclear.

Old obligations could be misrepresented.

A voluntary index might help preserve information.

But the letters were another matter.

They had been sent under an organization name that could not be verified.

They requested private records.

And at least one had referred to an outdated agreement.

Seraphina was not prepared to accuse anyone.

But she was no longer comfortable treating the letters as ordinary historical inquiries.

The following morning, Alaric convened a small meeting.

Present were Seraphina, Cedric, Roland, and the ducal legal adviser.

The purpose was to decide how House Veyr should respond.

The legal adviser examined the correspondence.

"I would not provide the original documents," he said.

"Agreed," Alaric replied.

"Nor would I make a public accusation. The sender may be connected to a legitimate consultation submission, even if the method of correspondence is questionable."

Seraphina nodded.

"Can we ask the council to verify whether the committee exists?"

"Yes. We can also ask whether the council authorized any private organization to collect records."

"Did it?"

"Not according to the consultation notice."

Roland opened his notes.

"We should also examine the old guarantee itself. If there was an executed agreement, the estate should know its terms."

Alaric looked at Seraphina.

"That is sensible."

The legal adviser agreed.

"Even if the guarantee was never completed, the estate should retain a record of the preliminary discussions."

Cedric wrote down the instructions.

The meeting concluded with three actions.

First, House Veyr would continue searching its archives for the original instrument.

Second, it would ask the Royal Agricultural Council to clarify the identity and status of the submitting committee.

Third, it would preserve the letters and refrain from distributing private documents until the sender's authority was established.

No public statement would be issued.

No accusations would be made.

For once, Seraphina felt that the matter had been handled without anyone attempting to turn it into a kingdom-wide crisis.

She was almost grateful.

The next day, the regional registry sent its reply.

The building listed as the committee's contact address belonged to a private records company.

It had previously been occupied by a legal archive that closed seven years earlier.

The current tenant was **Northgate Historical Services**.

The company had been registered two years ago.

Its stated business activities included document preservation, historical research, and private archival consulting.

The registry provided the names of two directors.

One was a former regional legal clerk.

The other was a retired administrative officer.

Neither name was familiar to Seraphina.

However, the second director had previously worked in the Royal Agricultural Council's regional correspondence office.

Cedric placed the registry report on her desk.

"That is our first identifiable connection."

Seraphina examined the name.

"Did this officer work on the old guarantee records?"

"We do not know."

"Did they receive the consultation submission?"

"We do not know."

"Are they authorized to collect private documents?"

"No."

Cedric paused.

"But the company is legitimate."

"Yes."

"Then the letters may have been sent by the company."

"Possibly."

Seraphina looked at the report.

A registered company was not automatically entitled to private estate records.

But its existence made the correspondence less mysterious.

There was now a plausible explanation.

A private archival company might be trying to gather historical documents in support of a proposal to establish a regional records service.

That could be legitimate research.

It could also be an attempt to create a commercial role for itself.

The difference mattered.

Especially if estates were being encouraged to hand over documents without knowing who would control them.

Seraphina folded the report.

"Ask the council whether Northgate Historical Services is connected to Submission 06."

Cedric nodded.

"And if it is?"

"Then we ask whether the committee authorized the letters."

"And if it isn't?"

"Then someone is using the committee's name without permission."

Cedric looked at the document.

"That could become serious."

"Yes."

Seraphina stood.

"Which is why we should find out before anyone announces it in a newspaper."

That afternoon, another letter arrived.

This one was addressed directly to House Veyr.

It carried the same seal.

The sender thanked the estate for its presumed cooperation and requested a certified copy of the original northern development guarantee.

It also stated that the document was required to complete a preliminary regional liability assessment.

Seraphina read the sentence.

Then she looked at Cedric.

"Presumed cooperation?"

"Yes."

"We have not cooperated."

"No."

"Have we even replied to them?"

"Not directly."

She placed the letter down.

The request had become more confident.

The sender now appeared to believe that House Veyr would provide the document.

Perhaps it was simply an administrative follow-up.

Perhaps the writer had assumed that the earlier request had been accepted.

But the phrase *presumed cooperation* was troubling.

It suggested that the sender was treating silence as consent.

Seraphina turned to the legal adviser, who had arrived to review the correspondence.

"Can we send a formal refusal?"

"Certainly."

"State that the sender has not established authority to request private estate records."

"Yes."

"And ask them to identify the legal basis for the liability assessment."

The adviser nodded.

"I will prepare the reply."

Seraphina looked again at the old reference.

**VEY/FIN/41/08.**

A preliminary guarantee.

An unfinished schedule.

An original instrument supposedly retained by a regional committee.

A private company collecting historical records.

And a liability assessment whose authority had not been established.

The original document might prove nothing more than an abandoned proposal.

But until they found it, House Veyr could not be certain.

Seraphina had wanted a quiet week.

Instead, someone had discovered a forty-one-year-old financial reference and decided that it deserved a second life.

She closed the folder.

"Find the original committee archive," she told Cedric.

"Where?"

"The old regional development office. Start with its transfer records."

Cedric nodded.

"Understood."

"And Cedric?"

"Yes?"

"Do not let anyone send them our accounts."

He gave her a solemn look.

"I had no intention of doing so."

"Good."

She considered the letter one final time.

The sender had asked for a guarantee.

House Veyr had not yet found one.

And until it did, Seraphina had no intention of guaranteeing anything.
