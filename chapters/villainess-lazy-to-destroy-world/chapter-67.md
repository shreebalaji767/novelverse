# Chapter 67 — The Archive That Had Been Closed Twice

The old Northern Development Committee had apparently possessed three archives.

This was inconvenient because the committee itself had possessed only one office.

Seraphina learned this from Cedric on Thursday morning.

He had placed four documents on her breakfast table, along with a small cup of tea and a look that suggested he had already spent several hours regretting the existence of historical administration.

"Three archives?" she asked.

"Three recorded storage locations."

"How many actual storage locations?"

"That is what I am trying to establish."

Seraphina picked up the first document.

It was a transfer schedule from the former Northern Development Committee, dated forty years earlier.

The committee had been dissolved after a regional administrative reorganization. Its active correspondence had been transferred to the Northern Regional Office.

Its technical records had been transferred to the Regional Engineering Archive.

Its financial records had supposedly been transferred to the Treasury's Northern Accounts Repository.

The schedule included a final note:

*Remaining historical material to be retained under consolidated custody pending identification and allocation.*

Seraphina looked at Cedric.

"Where?"

"It does not say."

"Of course."

Cedric placed another document beside it.

A later inventory, prepared twenty-three years earlier, listed a collection of boxes under the heading:

**Northern Development Committee — Unallocated Historical Records.**

There were seventeen boxes.

The inventory recorded that they had been moved to a temporary storage facility.

The facility's address was missing.

"Perhaps they were transferred to the regional archive," Seraphina said.

"That is possible."

"Perhaps they were transferred to the Treasury."

"Also possible."

"Perhaps someone put them in a cellar and forgot."

Cedric considered the suggestion.

"That is possible too."

Seraphina lowered the document.

"Then we have made excellent progress."

Cedric sat down.

"The regional office has confirmed that it does not hold the boxes. The Treasury has no entry for the seventeen-box transfer. The engineering archive has only the technical collection."

"Then the temporary storage facility is our next lead."

"Yes."

"Do we know who operated it?"

"Not yet."

Seraphina looked at the fourth document.

It was a handwritten list of box references.

One entry had been underlined.

**Box 08 — Guarantees, Northern Development Works, Estate Contributions.**

She tapped it.

"That is the one we need."

Cedric nodded.

"Possibly."

"Do not sound so disappointed."

"I am trying to avoid promising that a box exists merely because someone wrote its number on a list."

Seraphina considered that.

It was a reasonable precaution.

She disliked reasonable precautions when they delayed her breakfast.

The first inquiry went to the regional records office.

The second went to the Treasury.

The third went to the former staff registry.

The regional office replied that the temporary storage facility had been operated by the Northern Administrative Services Department, which had ceased to exist thirty-one years earlier.

Its functions had been divided between the regional records office and the Royal Infrastructure Department.

The Treasury replied that it had no record of receiving seventeen boxes.

The former staff registry identified the person responsible for the temporary storage facility's inventory.

His name was **Edwin Marsh**.

He was retired.

He had worked as a depot manager for the Northern Administrative Services Department before transferring to the Western Storage Annex.

Cedric read the personnel summary.

"That is the same Edwin Marsh who managed the Western Storage Annex?"

"Yes."

Seraphina looked up.

"Do we know him?"

"Not personally. He appears in several old transfer records."

"Then ask whether he remembers the boxes."

Cedric wrote the instruction down.

Seraphina returned to the breakfast tray.

Her tea had gone cold.

She looked at it with quiet disappointment.

The archive had already cost her one cup.

It was developing a reputation.

Edwin Marsh agreed to meet them the following week.

He lived in a modest house outside the northern market town.

The former depot manager was seventy-two, broad-faced, and hard of hearing in his left ear.

He welcomed Cedric and Seraphina into a sitting room crowded with books, old maps, and a collection of wooden model ships.

"Records," he said after Cedric explained their purpose.

"I spent half my life moving them."

Seraphina looked at the model ships.

"Did you keep any?"

"Ships?"

"Records."

He laughed.

"No. My wife would have thrown me out."

His wife, sitting near the window, gave him a look.

"I considered it."

Edwin smiled.

Cedric explained the transfer schedule.

The old manager listened carefully.

"Seventeen boxes," he repeated.

"Yes," Cedric said.

"That was a temporary collection."

"Do you remember where it went?"

"I remember receiving it."

"Where?"

"North Depot Three."

Cedric looked at Seraphina.

"Is that the temporary storage facility?"

"It was one of them."

Edwin frowned.

"There were two buildings at the time. Depot Three was the old grain administration storehouse. Depot Four was the transport office."

"Which one held the boxes?"

"Three."

"Do you remember the transfer date?"

"Late autumn. The year before the department was reorganized."

Cedric checked his documents.

The date matched.

"Who received the boxes?"

Edwin leaned back.

"I did."

"Did anyone collect them afterward?"

"Yes."

"Who?"

He thought for a moment.

"Records consolidation team."

"Do you remember the officer's name?"

"No."

"Do you remember where the boxes were sent?"

Edwin shook his head.

"They were supposed to go to the central records store."

Cedric opened his notes.

"Which central records store?"

"That was the problem."

Seraphina looked at him.

"What problem?"

Edwin rubbed his chin.

"The department had two places called the central records store. One belonged to the regional administration. The other belonged to the infrastructure office."

Cedric closed his notebook.

"That is unfortunate."

Edwin nodded.

"Very."

Seraphina looked toward the old manager.

"Did you receive a collection receipt?"

"Yes."

"Do you still have it?"

Edwin smiled.

"I might."

His wife looked up.

"You said you threw those things away."

"I said I threw most of them away."

"That is not the same thing."

Edwin rose slowly.

"Wait here."

He disappeared into the adjoining room.

Several minutes later, he returned carrying a small wooden box.

It contained old letters, receipts, a pocket watch, and a collection of papers tied with string.

Edwin searched through them.

Then he found a folded sheet.

He opened it carefully.

The receipt was faded but readable.

**Northern Development Committee — Seventeen Boxes Received at North Depot Three.**

The receiving date matched the transfer schedule.

The lower section recorded a later collection.

**Collected by: Central Records Consolidation Team.**

There was a signature.

The name was partially smudged.

Seraphina leaned closer.

"Can you read it?"

Edwin adjusted his spectacles.

"Looks like H. Mercer."

Cedric copied the name.

"Do you remember him?"

"No."

"Was he an employee?"

"Probably. I signed for the boxes when they arrived. I signed again when they left. The collection team had its own paperwork."

He looked at the receipt.

"That was the rule."

Seraphina nodded.

"Did you see the destination written anywhere?"

Edwin turned the paper over.

There was a faint stamp on the back.

**CENTRAL RECORDS — RECEIVING BAY 4.**

The address was missing.

But the receiving bay number was useful.

Cedric carefully copied the stamp.

For the first time that morning, the search had produced something concrete.

Not the boxes.

Not the guarantee.

But a transfer destination that could be checked.

Seraphina stood.

"Thank you, Master Marsh."

He smiled.

"Glad to be of help."

His wife looked at the wooden box.

"Perhaps now you can stop telling people that I threw away your papers."

Edwin coughed.

Seraphina politely pretended not to hear.

The Central Records Office confirmed the existence of a Receiving Bay Four.

Unfortunately, it had belonged to the Royal Infrastructure Department.

The department's old building had been demolished twenty-six years earlier.

Its records had been divided between two successor offices.

One held technical project files.

The other held administrative correspondence.

Neither had an inventory for seventeen boxes from the Northern Development Committee.

Cedric read the replies in silence.

Then he placed them on Seraphina's desk.

"Another transfer."

"Yes."

"Another missing receipt."

"Yes."

"Do you think we will ever find the boxes?"

Seraphina looked at him.

"I think the boxes have become very good at avoiding us."

Cedric sighed.

The next step was to identify H. Mercer.

The former staff registry produced three possible names.

One had worked in the Royal Infrastructure Department's receiving office.

One had been a temporary clerk in the regional transport authority.

The third had been an accounts assistant.

Only the first had a connection to Receiving Bay Four.

His name was **Harold Mercer**.

He had retired twenty-eight years earlier.

The registry did not have a current address, but a former colleague had listed a forwarding address in a town south of the capital.

Cedric sent a letter.

They waited.

The reply arrived nine days later.

Harold Mercer was alive.

He remembered the transfer.

He also remembered that the boxes had been received under a temporary classification because their contents had not been reviewed.

He had not personally examined them.

The receiving record listed them as:

**Historical Regional Development — Pending Departmental Allocation.**

He also recalled a dispute over which office should hold the collection.

The engineering department considered the records historical.

The regional administration considered them technical.

The Treasury had declined to accept them because they were not financial ledgers.

The boxes had remained in Receiving Bay Four for several weeks.

Then they had been moved.

The destination was recorded in a secondary register.

Harold Mercer did not have a copy.

But he remembered the name of the person who had prepared it.

**Master Colin Reeve.**

Cedric stared at the letter.

"Master Reeve?"

Seraphina looked up.

"The regional surveyor?"

"Yes. He was involved in the old technical review group."

She remembered the name.

Colin Reeve had helped examine the western infrastructure projects years earlier.

He had also contributed to the report listing the unfinished stations.

He was not the same person as the estate surveyor.

But his name had appeared in several of the old infrastructure records.

"Contact him," Seraphina said.

Cedric nodded.

"Immediately?"

"Yes. Ask whether he remembers the transfer. Do not suggest that he misplaced anything."

Cedric looked mildly offended.

"I would not."

Seraphina returned to her notes.

"Good."

Colin Reeve replied the following afternoon.

He remembered the collection.

He had been asked to review several boxes because the records contained maps and engineering schedules.

He had prepared a short inventory.

The collection had then been sent to the Northern Project Review Office.

The office had existed for less than three years.

Its purpose had been to review incomplete regional infrastructure projects and determine whether they should be continued, suspended, or formally closed.

It had been dissolved after a change in departmental structure.

The inventory reference was included in his letter.

**NPRO/ARCH/17-4.**

Seraphina read it.

"That sounds promising."

Cedric nodded.

"The office's successor was the Central Project Review Office."

"The same office Henry Cole worked for?"

"Yes."

Seraphina looked at him.

"Then we should contact Henry Cole."

Cedric smiled.

"We have finally reached someone who might know where the records went."

"Do not celebrate yet."

He stopped smiling.

She continued.

"We have only reached another office."

Henry Cole responded promptly.

He remembered the Northern Project Review Office.

He had been transferred there during its final year.

He also remembered the historical collection.

The seventeen boxes had been divided into smaller groups after Colin Reeve's inventory.

Some technical maps had been transferred to the Academy.

Several administrative files had been retained by the Central Project Review Office.

The remaining material had been sent to the Royal Infrastructure Allocation Office.

Seraphina looked at the letter.

"That office?"

Cedric nodded.

"It appears so."

"The same office that reviewed the old W-2 settlement?"

"Yes."

Seraphina considered the possibility.

The Northern Development Committee records had passed through several departments.

Some had eventually reached the Royal Infrastructure Allocation Office.

That did not mean the office had retained them.

It did, however, provide a new location to investigate.

Henry Cole's letter included another reference.

**NPRO/ARCH/17-4/TRANSFER/FINAL.**

He had a copy of the transfer summary.

Cedric requested it.

The document arrived two days later.

It contained a list of the remaining boxes.

The original seventeen had been divided into three collections.

Seraphina read the categories aloud.

"Technical maps and surveys. Administrative correspondence. Financial guarantees and estate contributions."

Cedric looked at the final category.

"That is ours."

"Yes."

The transfer summary listed four boxes under it.

Box 06.

Box 08.

Box 11.

Box 14.

Box 08 was described as:

**Estate Guarantees — Northern Development Works — Original Instruments and Execution Copies.**

Seraphina sat very still.

There it was.

The box existed.

Or at least, it had existed at the time of transfer.

The destination column contained a department code.

**RIAO — Historical Commitments Review.**

Cedric read it twice.

"The Royal Infrastructure Allocation Office."

Seraphina nodded.

"Now we need to know whether it received the box."

The Allocation Office confirmed that it had received the four boxes.

However, it had later transferred its historical commitments collection to the Central Treasury Records Division.

The transfer had taken place twenty-two years earlier.

The office had retained only summary schedules.

The Treasury's receiving register listed the collection.

Box 08 had been received.

The receipt was signed.

The inventory was complete.

For the first time in several weeks, a record had not vanished between departments.

Cedric almost looked disappointed.

The Treasury's records administrator, a woman named **Eleanor Price**, was assigned to supervise the retrieval.

She confirmed that the collection was stored in the historical commitments archive.

The box could be retrieved within three working days.

Seraphina read the confirmation.

"Three days."

Cedric nodded.

"Three days."

"That seems reasonable."

"It does."

She looked at the ceiling.

"Perhaps the kingdom is improving."

Cedric laughed.

Then he noticed the final paragraph.

His expression changed.

"What?"

"The box has been opened previously."

Seraphina looked down.

"By whom?"

"The register lists a review in the previous year."

"Who requested it?"

Cedric read the entry.

**External Records Verification — Reference NDR/06.**

Seraphina's expression became still.

The same reference.

The same one appearing on the letters sent to Master Dunn and the northern estates.

The Treasury had released Box 08 for inspection twelve months earlier.

The request had been submitted by a private records company.

The name was listed.

**Northgate Historical Services.**

Seraphina placed the letter down.

"Did they receive permission to inspect the original documents?"

Cedric checked the attached authorization.

"They were permitted to examine the collection under supervision. They were not permitted to remove documents or make copies without approval."

"Who approved the request?"

He turned the page.

The signature belonged to the deputy director of the Central Treasury Records Division.

Seraphina read the authorization carefully.

It was valid.

The company had not stolen access.

It had been granted supervised access.

But the scope of the inspection was not entirely clear.

The authorization described the work as a historical records survey.

It did not mention collecting liability information.

It did not authorize the company to contact estates.

It did not authorize the company to describe itself as a representative of the Royal Agricultural Council.

Seraphina looked at Cedric.

"Ask the Treasury for the inspection report."

"Yes."

"And the list of documents examined."

"Yes."

"Also ask whether Northgate submitted a copy of its findings."

Cedric nodded.

The letter had one final attachment.

A handwritten note from the records administrator.

It stated that the box had been returned to storage after inspection.

However, one document had been marked for further verification.

**VEY/FIN/41/08 — Northern Development Guarantee.**

Seraphina looked at the reference.

It had been found.

The original instrument existed.

And someone had examined it twelve months earlier.

That evening, Alaric joined Seraphina in the study.

She explained the chain of transfers.

He listened without interruption.

When she finished, he looked at the Treasury letter.

"So the guarantee exists."

"The original instrument exists. We have not seen its terms."

"But it was examined."

"Yes."

"By a private company."

"Under Treasury authorization."

Alaric nodded.

"That is important."

"Yes."

He read the inspection reference.

"Why did they contact Master Dunn if they already had access to the original?"

Seraphina had been asking herself the same question.

Perhaps the company had needed a second copy.

Perhaps it had been trying to verify the identity of the signatories.

Perhaps the original document was incomplete.

Or perhaps its inspection had revealed an obligation that the company wished to investigate.

There was no way to know yet.

Alaric folded the letter.

"Have the Treasury send us the inspection report."

"They have been asked."

"And the original guarantee?"

"Three working days."

He nodded.

"Good."

Then he looked at his daughter.

"Do you think this concerns the old financial arrangements we reviewed last year?"

"I don't know."

"The historical obligations?"

"Possibly. But we should not connect them without evidence."

Alaric smiled faintly.

"You have become very careful."

Seraphina looked at him.

"I have become tired."

He laughed.

It was a small sound, but it eased the tension in the room.

Alaric stood.

"Then we will examine the document when it arrives. Until then, no assumptions."

Seraphina nodded.

"Agreed."

He reached the door.

Then he stopped.

"One more thing."

"What?"

"Your mother has arranged dinner with the Bellmonts next week."

Seraphina stared at him.

"Why?"

"Because Lord Bellmont asked to discuss the assembly resolution."

"Could that not be done by letter?"

"It could."

"Then why dinner?"

Alaric considered the question.

"Because your mother believes it would be socially beneficial."

Seraphina closed her eyes.

The old guarantee had been found.

The missing archive had been located.

The private company had been identified.

The next step was clear.

And yet dinner with the Bellmonts had somehow become the most immediate threat to her peace.

She opened her eyes.

"Will there be speeches?"

"Probably not."

"How many people?"

"I do not know."

"Will there be musicians?"

Alaric hesitated.

Seraphina sighed.

"That means yes."

Three days later, the Treasury delivered the box.

It was smaller than Seraphina expected.

The wooden case was dark with age, and its brass fittings had dulled.

A paper seal had been attached to the lid.

The seal was intact.

Eleanor Price supervised the opening.

Cedric attended with Roland and the ducal legal adviser.

Seraphina stood beside the table.

The box contained twenty-three documents.

There were maps, correspondence, financial schedules, and several signed instruments.

The first twelve documents concerned different regional development projects.

The next six concerned estate contributions.

The remaining five were correspondence about incomplete or disputed guarantees.

Roland began sorting the papers according to the original inventory.

Seraphina searched for the reference.

She found it near the bottom.

**VEY/FIN/41/08.**

The document was folded inside a separate protective cover.

It was titled:

**Northern Water and Transport Improvement Scheme — Limited Estate Guarantee.**

Seraphina opened it.

The first page contained the names of three parties.

The Northern Development Committee.

The Royal Infrastructure Allocation Office.

And House Veyr.

The guarantee had been signed by Duke Alaric's grandfather.

It covered a maximum contribution of 4,000 crowns toward a specific regional development programme.

The obligation was conditional.

Payment would become due only if the project reached its final construction stage and the committee issued a certified expenditure statement.

The guarantee would expire if the project was abandoned before construction began.

Seraphina read the final page.

There was a signature.

A date.

And a seal.

The document was genuine.

The legal adviser examined it.

"It appears to be an executed instrument."

Roland looked up.

"Then House Veyr did guarantee the amount."

"Yes," the adviser replied. "But we need the accompanying schedule and completion records to determine whether the obligation was ever activated."

Seraphina turned to the next page.

It was the project schedule.

The Northern Water and Transport Improvement Scheme had been divided into four sections.

Two roads.

A bridge.

And a regional water-control structure.

The estate contributions were intended to cover part of the construction costs.

The schedule listed several participating families.

House Veyr was responsible for a maximum guarantee of 4,000 crowns.

Bellmont had guaranteed 2,500.

Marlowe had guaranteed 1,800.

Two other estates had smaller obligations.

Seraphina looked at the document.

"Was the project completed?"

Roland began checking the attached correspondence.

The first section had been completed.

The second had been delayed.

The bridge had been built.

The water-control structure had been suspended.

The final accounts were incomplete.

The legal adviser examined the completion certificate.

It covered the bridge and the first road.

It did not cover the water-control structure.

The guarantee had not automatically expired.

But neither did the document establish that the full amount was payable.

Roland turned to the financial schedule.

"Here."

He pointed to a line.

**House Veyr — Maximum Guarantee: 4,000 Crowns.**

Below it was another entry.

**Certified Contribution Received: 1,600 Crowns.**

Seraphina looked at him.

"Paid?"

"Possibly. We need the Treasury account."

The legal adviser examined the notation.

"It appears to record a contribution already received."

"Then the remaining 2,400?"

"Not necessarily due. The guarantee was conditional."

Seraphina turned the page.

There was a handwritten note in the margin.

*Balance to remain pending final certification.*

The handwriting was different from the original document.

The note had no signature.

No date.

No official stamp.

She read it again.

"Who wrote this?"

Roland examined the ink.

"I cannot tell."

The legal adviser frowned.

"We should not treat this note as part of the executed instrument without verifying its source."

Seraphina nodded.

The original guarantee was real.

The obligation was conditional.

A portion had apparently been paid.

The remaining amount had been left pending.

But the final certification was missing.

And someone had recently examined the document.

She looked at the inspection record.

The Treasury's note identified the documents inspected by Northgate Historical Services.

The company had examined the original guarantee.

It had also examined the attached schedule.

And one more document.

A separate summary sheet.

Its reference was unfamiliar.

**NDR/LIABILITY/06-A.**

Seraphina looked at Cedric.

"Do we have that sheet?"

He searched the box.

There was no matching document.

Eleanor Price checked the inventory.

It had not been listed as part of the original collection.

"It may have been produced by the inspection team," she said.

"Was a copy retained?"

"I will need to check the inspection file."

Seraphina nodded.

"Please do."

Eleanor wrote down the reference.

The original guarantee was returned to its protective cover.

The box was not closed.

There were still other documents to examine.

But the most important question had changed.

They were no longer searching for a guarantee that might not exist.

They were investigating a genuine historical obligation whose final status had never been properly established.

And a private company had already examined it.

Seraphina looked at the handwritten note.

*Balance to remain pending final certification.*

It was a small sentence.

It might have meant nothing more than an administrative reminder.

But if someone had copied the guarantee without the condition, or had presented the maximum amount as an outstanding debt, the consequences could be considerable.

She closed the folder.

"Before anyone makes a claim about this guarantee," she said, "we need the final accounts, the completion records, and the inspection report."

The legal adviser nodded.

Roland agreed.

Cedric wrote down the instructions.

Eleanor Price prepared the box for temporary resealing.

Outside the archive room, the afternoon bells rang.

Seraphina glanced at the clock.

She had missed lunch.

Again.

She looked at the old guarantee.

Forty-one years of uncertainty had finally produced a document.

Unfortunately, the document had produced several more questions.

And one of them concerned 2,400 crowns that House Veyr might owe.

Or might not.

For the moment, Seraphina was unwilling to pay anyone simply because they had discovered an old piece of paper.
