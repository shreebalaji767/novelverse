# Chapter 14 — The Man Who Kept the Old Receipts

Martin Hale had kept the wooden chest for twenty-six years.

His wife had once suggested throwing it away.

He had refused.

She had asked why a retired purchasing clerk needed three decades of old receipts, warehouse maps, and correspondence from an administration that had already replaced half its offices.

He had answered that old papers were useful.

She had told him that old papers were useful only when someone remembered where they were.

He had not disagreed.

His wife had died seven years ago.

The chest remained.

Now, at the age of seventy-one, Martin found himself sitting beside it with a letter from Mistress Elinor Brant in one hand and an old transfer register in the other.

He read the letter again.

It was polite and carefully worded.

It asked whether he remembered a delivery involving N-14, a temporary depot, and a final reconciliation that might never have been completed.

Martin remembered.

He remembered more than he wanted to.

But memory was not evidence.

He had spent most of his working life insisting on that distinction.

It seemed foolish to abandon it now.

He opened a small writing desk and began drafting a reply.

He stopped after the first sentence.

Then he tore the page in half.

He tried again.

This time, he wrote:

*I remember the transaction. I also remember that the records were incomplete at the time. I have retained several documents that may help establish what occurred. However, I cannot confirm the final destination of every delivery without examining the original maps.*

He paused.

Then he added:

*There is one name in the instruction register that I believe should be verified before any conclusions are drawn.*

Martin looked at the name.

He had not written it in the letter.

Not yet.

At the Veyr estate, Seraphina was being measured for the spring reception dress.

She had already endured two fittings.

This was the third.

She was beginning to suspect that aristocratic clothing was designed to prevent its wearer from moving comfortably.

“Could this sleeve be slightly looser?” she asked.

The dressmaker adjusted the fabric.

“Like this, my lady?”

“Better.”

Mira stood nearby, holding a tray of accessories.

Duchess Evelyne examined the dress from a distance.

“The color suits you.”

“Thank you.”

“The embroidery is subtle.”

“That is good.”

“And the neckline is appropriate.”

Seraphina glanced at the mirror.

“Can I sit down?”

The dressmaker looked alarmed.

“Of course, my lady.”

Seraphina carefully lowered herself onto a chair.

She immediately felt better.

Evelyne watched her.

“You dislike formal clothing.”

“I dislike clothing that makes sitting feel like a complicated negotiation.”

“That is a very specific complaint.”

“It is also accurate.”

Mira looked down at the accessories.

The duchess smiled.

“Fortunately, you will not need to remain in the reception hall for the entire evening.”

Seraphina looked at her mother.

“Can I leave early?”

“You may.”

“Before the Academy delegation arrives?”

“No.”

Seraphina sighed.

Evelyne sat opposite her.

“I know you are uncomfortable about their presence.”

“I am uncomfortable about being asked to discuss subjects I do not understand.”

“Then say so.”

“That sounds simple.”

“It is.”

Seraphina considered this.

“Will the Academy accept that?”

“They may be disappointed. They may also explain their work. Neither outcome requires you to agree to anything.”

Seraphina looked at the mirror.

The woman reflected there was seventeen, dressed in an elegant gown, with long black hair and crimson eyes.

She looked like someone who should be attending a royal ball and plotting someone's downfall.

Instead, she was worried about the quality of grain storage and whether a magical infrastructure meeting would take place during dinner.

She had to admit that her life had become strange.

“Very well,” she said.

Evelyne nodded.

“Good. Now let the dressmaker finish.”

Seraphina looked at the sleeve.

“Can she finish while I am sitting?”

The dressmaker gave a nervous laugh.

Evelyne did not answer.

Martin Hale sent his letter two days later.

He also sent copies of three documents.

He did not send the original transfer register.

He had learned, over a long career, that important documents should not be entrusted to ordinary messengers without a proper receipt.

The packet arrived at the Veyr estate shortly after noon.

Roland Ashford received it.

He checked the seal.

Then he checked the contents.

Then he called Cedric.

“Three documents?” Seraphina asked.

Cedric nodded.

“Three copies and a letter.”

“From Martin Hale?”

“Yes.”

“Does he confirm the delivery?”

“He confirms that the temporary transfer register existed. He does not confirm that every quantity reached its destination.”

Seraphina sat down.

“Show me.”

Cedric placed the documents on the table.

The first was a copy of an old warehouse transfer schedule.

The second was a map.

The third was a handwritten instruction.

Seraphina examined the transfer schedule first.

It listed three locations:

- N-14 — eighty measures.
- N-09 — one hundred and twenty measures.
- Southmere N-03 — sixty measures.

The total was two hundred and sixty standard measures.

She looked at the map.

The Northern Consolidated Depot was marked near an old junction road between the northern agricultural district and the western trade route.

It was not a permanent warehouse.

It appeared to have been a temporary storage facility.

A collection of buildings, perhaps.

Seraphina studied the markings.

“Is this place still there?”

Cedric shook his head.

“The map is too old to establish that. The road has since been altered.”

“Was it on ducal land?”

“Partly. The northern boundary crossed land belonging to a merchant family.”

“Which family?”

“The old map does not say.”

She looked at the third document.

It was an instruction issued during the emergency winter.

The handwriting was neat, formal, and unfamiliar.

It directed that the listed grain quantities be transferred to the temporary depot for redistribution.

At the bottom was a signature.

Seraphina could not read the name clearly.

“Who signed this?”

Cedric leaned closer.

“Martin Hale has written a note beside it.”

He turned the paper.

A short explanation had been added.

*The instruction was delivered to the purchasing office as an order from the central emergency committee. I did not receive it directly from the named official. It was brought by a messenger carrying the committee seal.*

Seraphina read the note again.

“So Martin did not personally verify the signature.”

“No.”

“Did he verify the seal?”

“He says the seal appeared genuine, but he did not retain the envelope.”

Seraphina placed the document down.

“That is not enough to establish who issued it.”

“No.”

“Does he say anything about the person who delivered it?”

Cedric read the letter.

“He remembers the messenger was a junior clerk from the central administration. He believes the man's name was Edmund Vale.”

Seraphina looked at him.

“Professor Edmund Vale?”

“No. The name is common. We have not established a connection.”

“Good.”

She was relieved.

The last thing she needed was for every person named in an old record to turn out to be part of some enormous conspiracy.

Cedric turned the letter over.

“There is something else.”

“What?”

“Martin says he remembers the final reconciliation being assigned to the central office. He does not remember receiving confirmation that it was completed.”

Seraphina looked at the transfer schedule.

Two hundred and sixty measures.

Three locations.

One temporary depot.

No final reconciliation.

“Then the missing records may concern all three deliveries.”

“Yes.”

“Not just N-14.”

“Correct.”

She nodded slowly.

That changed the scope of the problem.

The eighty measures at N-14 might not have been an isolated discrepancy.

They might have been part of a larger transfer involving several locations.

But that did not mean the grain had disappeared.

It could have been distributed.

It could have been stored.

It could have been transferred again.

The problem was that no one had yet established what happened.

“Has Roland seen this?”

“He has.”

“What does he think?”

“He wants the original register.”

“Can Martin provide it?”

“He says he will, but only after receiving confirmation that it will be formally recorded and returned or preserved securely.”

Seraphina nodded.

“That is reasonable.”

Cedric gave her a faint smile.

“You are becoming very careful.”

“I am becoming very tired.”

“That too.”

The duke called a meeting that afternoon.

Roland presented the new documents.

Alaric examined the map for several minutes.

Then he looked at Cedric.

“Can we identify the location of the temporary depot?”

“Not yet. The surveyor believes it may have been near the old junction road.”

“Has the land ownership been established?”

“No.”

“Find the old property register.”

Roland made a note.

Alaric turned to Seraphina.

“What do you make of this?”

She considered her answer.

“I think the old reserve programme involved a larger transfer than the surviving records currently show. I think the grain was intended to be consolidated at a temporary depot. I think the final reconciliation was supposed to happen centrally.”

“And?”

“We do not know whether the depot received all the grain. We do not know whether the grain was distributed from there. We do not know whether the payment records correspond to the final quantities.”

Alaric nodded.

“Good.”

He looked at Roland.

“Request the original register from Martin Hale. Arrange for an official representative to collect it and issue a receipt.”

“Yes, Your Grace.”

“Cedric, have the surveyor examine the old map and identify the likely site.”

“Understood.”

“Harold Fenwick is to obtain the remaining regional records. No one is to make accusations against the former officials or merchants without evidence.”

Seraphina looked at her father.

He was taking the matter seriously.

But he was not treating it as proof of wrongdoing.

That was reassuring.

“May I ask something?” she said.

“Of course.”

“Do we know why the old reserve programme was closed?”

Alaric looked at Roland.

Roland checked his notes.

“The official record states that the emergency distribution was completed and the reserve programme returned to normal operation.”

“Does it explain how?”

“No.”

“Does it say whether the temporary depot was dismantled?”

“No.”

“Does it say whether the remaining grain was returned to permanent storage?”

“No.”

Seraphina leaned back.

“Then we should not call it completed.”

Alaric looked at her.

“What would you call it?”

“Closed administratively. Not necessarily reconciled.”

Roland wrote that down.

Cedric looked at her.

“That is an important distinction.”

Seraphina shrugged.

“It is also an annoying one.”

The following morning, the regional surveyor arrived with an initial map comparison.

The temporary depot had likely been situated near a junction that no longer existed.

The old road connected three agricultural settlements to a former military supply route.

The buildings might have been constructed during the emergency winter and dismantled afterward.

The surveyor could not establish their precise location.

However, he had identified an old land record.

The site had been leased from a private landholder named Lord Armand Bellweather.

Seraphina read the name.

“Is that family still active?”

Cedric checked the register.

“The current head is Baroness Bellweather. Her family owns several properties in the northern district.”

“Would she know anything about the old depot?”

“Possibly.”

“Can we ask?”

Cedric nodded.

“I will arrange a formal inquiry.”

Seraphina looked at the map.

“Was the land returned to the family after the emergency?”

“The lease record says it was.”

“Is there a return inspection?”

“Not in this file.”

“Then ask for that too.”

Cedric made a note.

Seraphina was quiet for a moment.

“Does the family have an archive?”

“Most old aristocratic families do.”

“Then perhaps they kept a copy of the lease.”

“Perhaps.”

She nodded.

It was a reasonable possibility.

She was beginning to feel that the investigation was becoming a search for old documents rather than a search for missing grain.

Which was probably what it needed to be.

That afternoon, Seraphina visited the northern agricultural office with Cedric.

She had been invited to observe the review of the procurement records.

The office was smaller than the main ducal administration building.

Its rooms were crowded with cabinets, desks, and bundles of old correspondence.

Several clerks worked at long tables.

A large map of the northern district occupied one wall.

Harold Fenwick greeted them.

“My lady.”

“Good afternoon.”

“Lord Cedric.”

Harold led them into a meeting room.

Roland was already there.

He had brought copies of the historical records.

Two senior clerks were waiting.

The first was a woman named Agnes Whitlock, who had served in the regional office for nearly thirty years.

The second was a younger clerk named Edwin Clarke, responsible for maintaining the current property registers.

Seraphina recognized the name from the receiving records.

She took a seat.

Harold began.

“We have reviewed the surviving documents relating to the Northern Consolidated Depot. There are several references to the site, but no complete inventory.”

“Was it used only during the emergency winter?” Cedric asked.

Agnes Whitlock answered.

“According to the records I have found, yes. It was intended as a temporary collection point.”

“Who supervised it?”

“The central emergency committee appointed a temporary supervisor. His name appears in one of the old registers.”

“Who?”

Agnes checked her notes.

“Sir Thomas Wycliffe.”

Seraphina did not recognize the name.

Neither did Cedric.

Roland asked, “Was he a member of the ducal administration?”

“No. He was a royal logistics officer assigned to the northern relief operation.”

“Is there a final report?”

“Not in the regional archive.”

“Does the central administration have one?”

“We have requested copies. They have not yet responded.”

Seraphina looked at the map.

“Do we know how many buildings were at the depot?”

Agnes shook her head.

“No confirmed number.”

“Was it a single warehouse?”

“We do not know.”

“Then the word depot may refer to several temporary storage buildings.”

“Yes, my lady.”

Seraphina studied the map again.

The marked location was near the old junction.

There were several structures drawn around it, but the symbols were too small to identify.

“Can we compare this with the old land lease?”

Edwin Clarke opened a folder.

“We have a partial copy.”

He placed it on the table.

The lease had been issued for a period of one year.

It granted the emergency committee permission to use a section of land for temporary storage.

The agreement required the land to be returned in its original condition after the emergency.

Seraphina read the final clause.

“Who inspected the land before it was returned?”

Edwin checked.

“The record lists an inspection by the temporary supervisor.”

“Sir Thomas Wycliffe?”

“Yes.”

“Was there a second inspection by the landowner?”

“No.”

“Was there a signed acknowledgment from the family?”

Edwin shook his head.

“No.”

Cedric looked at the document.

“Then we cannot establish that the land was formally returned.”

Harold frowned.

“The lease expired. That should have been sufficient.”

“Not if the agreement required an inspection,” Roland said.

Agnes Whitlock turned to another page.

“There is a note here.”

She read it aloud.

“*Land returned to owner. Temporary facilities removed. Final reconciliation pending central confirmation.*”

Seraphina looked up.

“Who wrote that?”

Agnes examined the handwriting.

“It appears to be the temporary supervisor.”

“Then the same person who inspected the land also recorded that the final reconciliation was pending.”

“Yes.”

“Did the central office ever confirm it?”

“No confirmation has been found.”

The room fell silent.

Seraphina looked at the old map.

The temporary depot had been dismantled.

The land had supposedly been returned.

But the final reconciliation had never been confirmed.

The grain might have been distributed.

It might have been transferred.

It might have been accounted for elsewhere.

The records did not establish which.

“Could we find Sir Thomas Wycliffe?” she asked.

Harold shook his head.

“He died many years ago.”

“His family?”

“Possibly.”

“His papers?”

“Unknown.”

Seraphina sighed.

Another dead official.

Another archive.

Another person whose memory could no longer be consulted.

She had a growing suspicion that history was mostly the study of people who had failed to keep their paperwork in one place.

On the way back to the estate, Cedric spoke.

“You have been quiet.”

“I am thinking.”

“About the depot?”

“Yes.”

“What do you think happened?”

Seraphina watched the fields pass outside the carriage window.

“I think the temporary depot was used during the emergency. I think grain was moved there from at least three reserve locations. I think the administration intended to reconcile the quantities afterward.”

“And?”

“And I think the process was interrupted or never completed.”

Cedric nodded.

“Could the grain have been distributed without records?”

“Possibly. But I would expect some local records to survive.”

“Could it have been sold?”

“Possibly. But we have no evidence of that.”

“Could it have been transferred to another reserve?”

“Possibly.”

Cedric looked at her.

“You are not going to choose one explanation.”

“No.”

“Even though it would be convenient?”

“Especially because it would be convenient.”

He laughed quietly.

“You sound like Roland.”

Seraphina looked offended.

“That is a terrible thing to say.”

Cedric smiled.

She returned her attention to the window.

The northern fields stretched towards the distant hills.

The roads were uneven.

The irrigation channels were narrow.

A farmer was guiding a cart along a muddy path.

The land looked ordinary.

The problems hidden within its records did not.

When they returned to the estate, a message was waiting.

Baroness Bellweather had replied to the formal inquiry.

She confirmed that her family retained a partial archive of the old land lease.

She also remembered that the temporary depot had included more than one building.

Her family had not received a complete handover report.

However, she was willing to permit the ducal administration to examine the surviving documents.

Seraphina read the message.

“That is helpful.”

Cedric nodded.

“It may identify the buildings.”

“Or at least establish how many there were.”

He placed the letter on the desk.

“There is another matter.”

Seraphina looked up.

“What?”

“The Royal Academy has sent its final guest list for the spring reception.”

She stared at him.

“Why?”

“Because the reception is approaching.”

“I know that.”

“Then why ask?”

“Because I was hoping the Academy would forget.”

Cedric handed her the list.

Seraphina scanned the names.

Several Academy officials were attending.

A senior researcher.

Two administrative representatives.

A regional infrastructure examiner.

And one name that made her stop.

**Liora Everwyn — Representative of the Holy Church.**

Seraphina stared at the page.

The saintess.

She remembered her from the original novel.

Liora Everwyn had been described as kind, hardworking, and unusually powerful in healing magic.

The original Seraphina had despised her.

That much was clear.

But Seraphina's memories of the actual relationship were incomplete.

She could not remember whether Liora had attended the spring reception.

She could not remember whether their first meeting had been hostile.

She only remembered that their conflict eventually became a central part of the villainess's downfall.

Seraphina slowly placed the list on the desk.

Cedric noticed her expression.

“Do you know her?”

“Not personally.”

“She is well regarded by the Church.”

“I have heard.”

“She is expected to attend with the delegation.”

Seraphina looked at the name again.

The saintess was coming.

The Royal Academy was coming.

The reception was approaching.

And somewhere in the northern records, two hundred and sixty measures of grain remained unaccounted for.

She looked at Cedric.

“Can we move the reception?”

He blinked.

“No.”

“Can we cancel it?”

“No.”

“Can I pretend to be ill?”

Cedric gave her a flat look.

“Your mother would know.”

Seraphina sighed.

“Of course she would.”

That evening, Evelyne found her daughter sitting beside the window.

The guest list lay on the table.

“You have seen the names,” she said.

Seraphina nodded.

“Liora Everwyn.”

“Yes.”

“Have you met her?”

“No.”

Evelyne sat beside her.

“She is young, but she has already taken on considerable responsibilities. The Church has asked her to represent several charitable and medical programmes.”

Seraphina remembered that much.

In the original novel, Liora's charitable work had been used to gain influence.

But she had no idea how much of that description was true.

“Do you think she is difficult?” Seraphina asked.

Evelyne considered the question.

“I have heard that she is sincere. I have also heard that she is overworked.”

“That sounds unfortunate.”

“It is.”

Seraphina looked at her mother.

“Will I have to speak with her?”

“You may.”

“What should I say?”

Evelyne smiled.

“Good evening would be a reasonable beginning.”

Seraphina looked away.

“That is not what I meant.”

“I know.”

Evelyne placed a hand over hers.

“You do not need to decide what sort of person she is before meeting her. And you do not need to compete with her.”

Seraphina went still.

Her mother had said it casually.

But the words landed heavily.

“You think I would?”

“I think you have spent too much time worrying about what other people expect of you.”

Seraphina lowered her eyes.

“I do not want to make a mistake.”

“Then listen before you speak. If you disagree, be polite. If you do not understand something, ask. You are not required to impress anyone.”

Seraphina looked at her mother.

“That sounds manageable.”

“It is.”

Evelyne stood.

“Now, please come downstairs. Your father has requested your company at dinner.”

“Is it another meeting?”

“No.”

“Are you certain?”

“I am certain.”

Seraphina rose.

Then paused.

“Mother?”

“Yes?”

“Thank you.”

Evelyne smiled.

“You are welcome.”

Later that night, Martin Hale received another letter.

This one came from the Veyr administration.

It confirmed that an official representative would visit him to inspect the original register and provide a formal receipt.

Martin read the letter.

Then he opened the wooden chest.

He removed the original transfer register.

Beneath it was a smaller envelope.

He had not opened it in more than twenty years.

It contained a copy of the emergency instruction that had authorized the transfer of the grain.

The signature was clearly visible.

So was the name.

Martin held the paper close to the lamp.

He had always believed that the instruction had come from the central emergency committee.

But now, looking at the signature, he remembered something.

The official named on the document had been away from the capital during that winter.

Martin had seen the travel notice himself.

He had even entered it into the correspondence register.

Which meant that the instruction could not have been issued in the ordinary way.

He placed the document beside the old transfer schedule.

Then he wrote a new letter.

This time, he included the name.

He also included one sentence:

*I do not know who prepared the instruction, but I can confirm that the person whose name appears beneath it was not in the capital when it was delivered.*

He sealed the envelope.

For the first time in twenty-six years, Martin decided that the old receipts were no longer his responsibility alone.
