# Chapter 69 — The Statement That Nobody Wanted to Sign

The Royal Agricultural Council had a problem.

It had requested a redacted example of an old financial guarantee and its final closure order.

House Veyr had agreed to provide one.

The Treasury had agreed to review its historical register.

Northgate Historical Services had corrected its correspondence.

Several estates had confirmed that their old obligations were no longer outstanding.

The problem was that the council now wanted to explain all of this publicly.

And nobody could agree on how.

Seraphina discovered this when the first draft of the council's statement arrived at the Veyr estate.

It was six pages long.

She read the first paragraph.

Then the second.

Then she stopped.

"Who wrote this?"

Cedric, seated opposite her, consulted the accompanying letter.

"The council's communications office."

"Did anyone from the legal department review it?"

"Apparently."

"Did anyone from the Treasury review it?"

"Yes."

"Did anyone from the regional office review it?"

"Yes."

Seraphina looked at the document again.

"Then why does it say House Veyr initiated a regional review of historical financial obligations?"

Cedric sighed.

"I was hoping you would tell me."

The draft continued to describe the Veyr guarantee as an example of the need for centralized historical records.

It suggested that the council's proposed index would prevent similar uncertainty in the future.

It also described Northgate Historical Services as an external research partner.

Seraphina placed the document down.

"Northgate was not appointed by the council."

"No."

"It was not authorized to collect records."

"No."

"It was not a government research partner."

"No."

"Then why is it described as one?"

Cedric examined the paragraph.

"Perhaps they mean that it participated in the consultation."

"Then they should write that."

He nodded.

Seraphina turned to the final page.

The statement concluded:

*The Northern estates have demonstrated the importance of coordinated historical oversight and have welcomed the council's efforts to establish a common framework.*

She stared at the sentence.

"Did the Northern Noble Assembly approve this?"

"No."

"Did the estates approve it?"

"No."

"Then who is speaking for them?"

Cedric had no answer.

Seraphina closed the document.

"Send it back."

"What should I say?"

"That House Veyr cannot approve a statement that attributes positions to other estates without their consent."

Cedric wrote it down.

"And ask them to distinguish the Treasury's register review from the council's proposed index."

"Understood."

She pushed the draft away.

"Also, Northgate should not be called a partner."

Cedric nodded.

"Anything else?"

"Yes."

She looked at the six pages.

"Tell them to use fewer words."

The council replied the following morning.

It thanked House Veyr for its comments and explained that the draft had been intended as a preliminary summary.

A revised version would be circulated.

The council also asked whether Seraphina would attend a meeting to discuss the wording.

She declined.

She was not a member of the council.

She had no authority to approve its public statements.

She suggested that the Treasury, legal office, and regional administration agree on the factual language.

The council accepted.

Seraphina considered the matter closed.

It was not.

Two days later, the revised statement arrived.

It was shorter.

Unfortunately, it had acquired a different problem.

The first paragraph now read:

*Following concerns raised by House Veyr, the Royal Agricultural Council has initiated a review of historical regional guarantees.*

Seraphina read it.

"Still wrong."

Cedric nodded.

"Yes."

"House Veyr did not raise concerns about the regional guarantees."

"Correct."

"We found an old document while responding to an inquiry."

"Correct."

"The Treasury reviewed the records."

"Correct."

"Then why are we still at the beginning of the sentence?"

Cedric looked at the draft.

"Perhaps they want to acknowledge our involvement."

"They can acknowledge our involvement without making us responsible for the review."

He took the paper.

"I'll send another correction."

Seraphina leaned back.

"At this rate, the statement will be finished by the time the next generation inherits the archive."

Cedric smiled.

"At least they will know where it is."

The next development came from Lord Bellmont.

He requested a private meeting with Alaric.

Seraphina attended because the subject concerned the Northern Noble Assembly.

Lord Bellmont arrived with his steward and a document case.

He greeted them, then placed a letter on the table.

"I have received this from the Royal Agricultural Council."

Alaric opened it.

The letter requested that Bellmont confirm its support for the proposed voluntary historical index.

It described the Northern Noble Assembly's recent resolution as evidence of regional agreement.

Lord Bellmont looked irritated.

"We agreed to retain original documents and record amendments. We did not approve a central index."

Alaric nodded.

"The resolution did not establish that."

"Exactly."

Seraphina examined the letter.

The council had not directly claimed that the assembly approved the index.

It had said the assembly's resolution demonstrated support for the principles underlying it.

That was technically defensible.

But it was misleading.

The assembly had agreed to general recordkeeping principles.

It had not agreed to the council's proposed system.

Lord Bellmont continued.

"I have no objection to the index itself. It may be useful. But I do not want our resolution used to suggest that we have approved something we have not examined."

Seraphina looked at him.

"Then you should reply with that distinction."

"I intend to."

Alaric placed the letter down.

"The council may simply be trying to demonstrate that the proposal has regional support."

"Then it should ask for support rather than imply it."

Seraphina nodded.

Lord Bellmont looked at her.

"Would House Veyr sign a joint clarification?"

She considered it.

A joint clarification could be useful.

But it should not become another declaration of opposition or support.

"We can confirm what the assembly actually approved," she said. "Nothing more."

Lord Bellmont smiled.

"That is precisely what I had in mind."

The joint clarification was prepared by the legal advisers of both estates.

It was brief.

It stated that the Northern Noble Assembly had agreed on several voluntary principles concerning the preservation of historical property agreements.

It had not approved the council's proposed central index.

It had not authorized any estate to submit private records.

It had not granted the regional office authority to revise existing property agreements.

The statement also confirmed that participating estates remained free to retain their own records and seek independent legal advice.

Lord Bellmont signed it.

Alaric signed it.

Seraphina did not.

She had attended the assembly as a House Veyr representative, but she was not the legal head of the estate.

Her father signed on behalf of House Veyr.

Cedric sent the document to the council.

The following day, three other estates asked to be included.

One wanted to add a sentence clarifying that its participation in the assembly did not constitute approval of the index.

Another wanted to emphasize that historical agreements remained subject to their original legal terms.

The third asked whether the clarification could be distributed to all participating families.

The joint statement was revised.

Five estates signed.

Two declined to sign but confirmed that they agreed with the factual description.

One estate requested a separate statement because its legal adviser believed the wording was too broad.

Seraphina approved of the result.

It was not unanimous.

It did not need to be.

The important thing was that nobody's position had been invented.

The newspapers reacted within two days.

The *Crown and Country Gazette* published a short report.

**NORTHERN ESTATES CLARIFY SUPPORT FOR RECORDKEEPING PRINCIPLES**

The article accurately described the joint clarification.

It also noted that the estates had not endorsed the proposed central index.

The *Aurelion Morning Chronicle* took a different approach.

Its headline read:

**HOUSE VEYR AND ALLIES CHALLENGE COUNCIL'S RECORDS PLAN**

Seraphina placed the newspaper on the breakfast table.

Evelyne looked at it.

"At least they have stopped calling you the leader of a regional movement."

"They have replaced it with a rebellion."

"An improvement, perhaps."

Seraphina looked at her mother.

"How?"

"The word rebellion is less administrative."

Seraphina almost smiled.

The article described the five signatories as a group of influential northern estates.

It also suggested that the council's proposal had met organized resistance.

But the article contained no evidence that the estates opposed the index.

The estates had simply asked the council not to misrepresent their agreement.

Seraphina read the final paragraph.

It quoted an unnamed official who said that the council would continue its work despite the objections.

She looked up.

"Who is the official?"

Evelyne shrugged.

"The newspaper does not say."

"Was the official from the council?"

"Possibly."

"Did the council confirm the statement?"

"Not yet."

Seraphina folded the newspaper.

She was beginning to dislike anonymous officials almost as much as anonymous landowners.

The council meeting took place in Aurelion the following week.

Seraphina did not intend to attend.

The invitation, however, was addressed to Duke Alaric.

He decided to send Cedric as the estate's administrative representative.

Seraphina was relieved.

She had already attended enough meetings to understand that some discussions could proceed without her.

Cedric left on Monday morning.

He returned two days later with a report.

The council had reviewed the wording of its public statement.

The legal office had insisted that the distinction between the Northern Noble Assembly's resolution and the proposed index be preserved.

The Treasury had also clarified that its historical register review was separate from the council's consultation.

The council had agreed to revise the statement.

But another issue had emerged.

The council's communications office had received a private memorandum from the Northern Development Historical Review Committee.

It claimed that several estates had expressed interest in a centralized archival service.

The memorandum included a list of twelve estates.

The same twelve estates Northgate had contacted.

Seraphina looked at Cedric.

"Did the council verify the list?"

"No."

"Did the estates confirm their interest?"

"Not individually."

"Did Northgate submit it?"

"The memorandum was submitted under the committee's name. The council believes Northgate prepared it."

Seraphina leaned forward.

"Then the council should ask Northgate to identify the basis for each entry."

Cedric nodded.

"That was proposed."

"Was it accepted?"

"Yes. The committee has been asked to provide supporting correspondence."

"Good."

Cedric opened his notes.

"There is another detail."

Seraphina waited.

"The memorandum was not submitted through the council's public consultation channel."

"How was it submitted?"

"By a private letter to the chairman's office."

"Who delivered it?"

"The council's correspondence register lists a messenger from the Office of Regional Liaison."

Seraphina frowned.

"Which office?"

Cedric checked his notes.

"The Northern Administrative Liaison Office."

She considered the name.

"Is that a public office?"

"It is part of the Royal Council Secretariat."

"Then why was a private memorandum delivered through it?"

"The council does not know."

Seraphina looked at him.

"Did the liaison office explain?"

"It says the document was forwarded for consideration after an informal meeting."

"With whom?"

"That is not recorded."

The room became quiet.

It was the first concrete indication that the private committee's memorandum might have been given access through a government office.

It did not prove improper influence.

The liaison office might have forwarded it as ordinary correspondence.

But the missing details mattered.

"Can the council request the original forwarding note?" Seraphina asked.

"Yes. It has already done so."

"Good."

Cedric placed another document on the table.

"The council also received a copy of the memorandum's supporting list."

Seraphina examined it.

The twelve estates were listed by name.

Five had received Northgate's letters.

Three had provided documents.

Two had declined.

The remaining two had not replied.

The memorandum described all twelve as having expressed interest in the proposed archival service.

Seraphina looked at the list.

"That is not accurate."

"No."

"Did any of the five estates explicitly agree to participate?"

"One had said it would consider a voluntary service if access rules were established."

"And the others?"

"They had only acknowledged receipt of the letters."

Seraphina put the list down.

"Then the memorandum has turned silence into support."

Cedric nodded.

"Yes."

Lord Bellmont was informed.

He was not pleased.

Neither was Lord Marlowe.

The two men agreed that the council should be asked to correct the list before relying on it.

They also wanted the Northern Noble Assembly to be informed.

Seraphina supported that.

But she advised against issuing another public statement until the council had examined the forwarding record.

The estates needed to know what had happened.

They did not yet need to accuse anyone.

The council agreed to send a verification request to all twelve families.

Each estate would be asked to confirm whether it had:

- Expressed support for a central archival service.
- Agreed to provide private records.
- Authorized another organization to represent its position.
- Received correspondence from Northgate Historical Services.

The questions were straightforward.

The answers were not.

One estate confirmed that it had expressed interest.

Two confirmed that they had agreed to consider participation, subject to legal review.

Five stated that they had never expressed support.

Four had received letters but had not replied.

Northgate's memorandum had described all twelve as interested participants.

The council now had evidence that the description was inaccurate.

It still did not know why.

The most useful reply came from the seventh estate.

It belonged to Lord Frederick Wycliffe, a landowner whose family had managed several northern transport routes.

His letter contained a copy of an earlier communication from Northgate.

Unlike the other letters, this one had been accompanied by a printed summary.

The summary described the proposed service as a way to identify unresolved historical obligations and prevent disputes.

It also stated that participating estates would be given priority access to the service if the council later approved a contract.

Seraphina read the sentence twice.

"Priority access?"

Cedric nodded.

"That is what it says."

"Was this included in the other letters?"

"No."

"Did the council approve it?"

"No."

"Did Northgate have a contract?"

"No."

Seraphina looked at the document.

The company had not merely been conducting historical research.

It had been encouraging estates to participate in a service it hoped to provide.

That was a commercial proposal.

But the promise of priority access suggested that it was trying to secure early support.

The company had also described the estates as interested participants before receiving their consent.

The problem was becoming clearer.

It might not be a conspiracy.

It might be a private company trying to establish itself as the preferred provider for a public service that did not yet exist.

But it had used the names of estates without permission.

It had presented a proposed service as if it were already being organized.

And its memorandum had reached the council through an office whose forwarding records were incomplete.

Seraphina looked at Cedric.

"Send the Wycliffe letter to the council."

"Yes."

"Ask whether Northgate has submitted any proposal for a contract."

"Understood."

"And request that the council preserve the original memorandum and its forwarding note."

Cedric nodded.

The instruction was recorded.

The council's reply arrived three days later.

Northgate had not submitted a formal contract proposal.

It had, however, requested a meeting with the council's chairman.

The meeting had taken place two months earlier.

It was described as an informal discussion about historical records.

The chairman's office had retained a short note.

The note recorded that Northgate had offered to assist with a voluntary archival pilot if one were approved.

It also stated that the company would be willing to provide an initial catalogue at its own expense.

The note did not mention priority access for estates.

It did not mention the twelve families.

It did not authorize the company to collect private records.

It did not approve a contract.

The meeting had been attended by the chairman, a council secretary, and a representative from the Northern Administrative Liaison Office.

The representative's name was **Mr Adrian Holt**.

Seraphina recognized it.

Adrian Holt had previously served as coordinator for the Royal Infrastructure Department's administrative review.

He had also helped organize the earlier infrastructure committee.

That did not establish any connection between his previous work and the current proposal.

But it meant he was familiar with the process of transferring documents between departments.

Cedric looked at her.

"Do you think he was responsible for forwarding the memorandum?"

"We do not know."

"He attended the meeting."

"Yes."

"He also works in the liaison office."

"Yes."

Cedric waited.

Seraphina closed the letter.

"We need the forwarding note."

The original note arrived the next morning.

It had been written by the Northern Administrative Liaison Office.

The document stated that the memorandum from the Northern Development Historical Review Committee was being forwarded to the council chairman following the earlier discussion with Northgate.

The sender's name was Adrian Holt.

The note contained a short sentence:

*The attached submission may assist the council in assessing regional interest in a voluntary archival service.*

There was no statement that the twelve estates had approved the service.

There was no claim that Northgate represented them.

There was no instruction to accept the memorandum.

Holt had forwarded it for consideration.

The problem lay in the memorandum itself.

It claimed that twelve estates had expressed interest.

The forwarding note did not verify that claim.

Seraphina read the two documents side by side.

The distinction was clear.

The liaison office had transmitted a private submission.

It had not certified its contents.

The council had then treated the submission as a useful indication of regional interest.

The mistake had occurred when an unverified claim became part of an official discussion.

It was not proof of corruption.

It was not proof of a coordinated political campaign.

It was a chain of assumptions.

Each office had accepted the previous document without checking what it actually established.

Seraphina looked at Cedric.

"That is what we need to explain."

He nodded.

"Should we contact Adrian Holt?"

"The council should ask him to confirm the circumstances of the forwarding."

"And Northgate?"

"The council should ask the company to correct the memorandum."

Cedric closed his notebook.

"Do you think they will?"

"I don't know."

The correction request was sent that afternoon.

Northgate responded within three days.

The company acknowledged that the twelve estates had not all expressed support.

It explained that the list had been prepared from correspondence received and interpreted as evidence of interest.

It agreed that this interpretation had been too broad.

The company also withdrew its claim that the estates were prospective participants.

It apologized for using their names without explicit consent.

The Royal Agricultural Council accepted the correction.

It also amended its internal consultation record.

The memorandum would remain in the archive, but its status would be marked:

**PRIVATE SUBMISSION — REGIONAL INTEREST CLAIM NOT VERIFIED.**

The council further stated that no decision had been made regarding the proposed archival service.

Northgate's request for a formal meeting was placed on hold until the council completed its review.

Adrian Holt provided a written explanation.

He confirmed that he had forwarded the memorandum for consideration after the informal meeting.

He had not verified the list of estates.

He had assumed that the council would assess the submission before relying on it.

The council accepted his explanation but instructed the liaison office to distinguish between forwarding a document and verifying its contents.

The matter was not closed.

But the immediate confusion had been corrected.

At the next Northern Noble Assembly, Lord Bellmont read the council's clarification aloud.

The estates listened.

Some were satisfied.

Others were not.

Lord Corven asked whether the council would formally prohibit private companies from collecting historical agreements.

The legal adviser explained that the council had no general authority to prohibit private archival work.

However, companies could not claim government authorization they did not possess.

They also could not use an estate's name to imply consent without permission.

Lord Marlowe asked whether the council intended to appoint Northgate.

The representative replied that no appointment had been made.

Another landowner asked whether the central index would proceed.

The answer was that the council was still considering the proposal.

The meeting remained calm.

But the atmosphere had changed.

The estates were no longer debating whether a central index was inherently good or bad.

They were discussing who would control it, who would have access, how private companies might participate, and what evidence would be required before any estate was described as supporting it.

Seraphina listened.

Cedric handled the factual questions.

Lord Bellmont kept the discussion focused.

For once, she did not have to explain the Veyr register.

That was a welcome development.

Near the end of the meeting, Lord Corven stood.

"I would like to make a proposal."

The assembly quieted.

"Before any further consultation is conducted, I believe the council should publish a clear statement of its own authority, the limits of any proposed index, and the conditions under which private contractors may be involved."

Several landowners nodded.

Lord Marlowe added:

"And any organization claiming support from an estate should provide written confirmation."

The proposal was accepted.

The assembly also requested that the council preserve the original submissions and their forwarding records.

It was a modest resolution.

It did not stop the central index.

It did not condemn Northgate.

It did not accuse the liaison office of misconduct.

It simply demanded that the process become clearer.

Seraphina thought it was the most useful thing the assembly had done so far.

After the meeting, Cedric approached her.

"Lord Bellmont wants to know whether House Veyr will join the request for a public hearing."

Seraphina looked at him.

"A public hearing?"

"To examine the council's proposed index, the role of private archival companies, and the handling of the Northgate memorandum."

She considered the request.

The matter had grown beyond a single historical guarantee.

The council had received an unverified claim.

The claim had been forwarded through an administrative office.

The estates had been described as supporters without consent.

And the newspaper coverage had repeatedly presented the proposal as if it already had regional backing.

A public hearing might help establish what had happened.

But it would also bring attention to House Veyr again.

"Who would attend?" she asked.

"The council, the Treasury, the regional office, Northgate, representatives of the estates, and the liaison office."

"Would the hearing examine the newspaper articles?"

"Possibly. The assembly has not decided."

Seraphina looked toward Lord Bellmont.

He was speaking with the legal adviser.

The old landowner seemed tired.

So was everyone else.

But the matter had become important enough that private correspondence and informal clarifications were no longer sufficient.

Seraphina turned back to Cedric.

"House Veyr should support a hearing."

Cedric nodded.

"Will you attend?"

"Yes."

He looked surprised.

She continued.

"But only as a representative of House Veyr. We will provide our own records and explain what happened. We will not speak for the other estates."

"Understood."

"And the hearing should distinguish between the original guarantee, Northgate's inspection, the council's consultation, and the newspaper coverage."

Cedric smiled.

"I suspect you will say that several times."

"Probably."

He laughed.

The formal request for a public hearing was submitted the following morning.

Five estates signed.

Three supported the request without signing.

Two declined to participate.

The Royal Agricultural Council agreed to hold the hearing in Aurelion.

It would be open to representatives of the participating estates and relevant public offices.

The council also invited Northgate Historical Services to provide evidence concerning its correspondence and consultation submission.

The hearing date was set for three weeks later.

Seraphina received the notice at breakfast.

She read it once.

Then she placed it beside her plate.

Evelyne looked at the letter.

"Another meeting?"

"Yes."

"Will you attend?"

"Yes."

Alaric folded his newspaper.

"What will the hearing examine?"

"The proposed index, the private archival service, and how the council received the memorandum."

"Do they expect you to give evidence?"

"Probably."

Alaric nodded.

"Then we should prepare the original records."

Seraphina looked at him.

"Yes."

Evelyne poured tea.

"At least this time, you know exactly what the documents say."

Seraphina picked up her cup.

That was true.

She had the original guarantee.

The bank's payment confirmation.

The final expenditure schedule.

The closure order.

The Treasury's inspection report.

Northgate's correspondence.

The council's consultation records.

And the forwarding note from Adrian Holt.

For once, the evidence was not scattered across forty years of missing transfers.

It was all available.

She took a sip of tea.

Then the household steward entered.

"Your Ladyship, Lady Bellmont has sent a message."

Seraphina looked up.

"What does she want?"

"She wishes to confirm your attendance at the charity luncheon next week."

Seraphina stared at him.

The public hearing was three weeks away.

The luncheon was next week.

The council had not yet finished revising its statement.

Northgate had not yet provided its final correction report.

The Treasury was still updating its historical register.

And now there was a luncheon.

She put her cup down.

"Tell Lady Bellmont that I will attend."

Evelyne smiled.

Seraphina looked at her.

"Please do not arrange anything else."

"I make no promises."

Seraphina sighed.

The hearing was approaching.

The records were ready.

And she had the unpleasant feeling that the next three weeks would be considerably longer than the previous three.

At least the 2,400 crowns were no longer anyone's problem.

She considered that a small victory.

It was enough for breakfast.
