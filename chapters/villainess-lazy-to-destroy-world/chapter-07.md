# Chapter 7 — The Problem With Having Too Many Servants

Seraphina Veyr had always believed that a large household was a luxury.

She was beginning to suspect it was a complicated form of punishment.

The realization came on a perfectly ordinary morning, when she discovered that three different people had been assigned to deliver the same message, two servants were waiting for instructions from a third servant who was currently on leave, and someone had sent a footman across the estate to retrieve a silver teapot that was already sitting in the dining room.

She stared at the teapot.

Then she stared at Mira.

Then she stared at the teapot again.

“Why?”

Mira, who had become increasingly skilled at interpreting the many meanings of her mistress's single-word questions, followed her gaze.

“The teapot, my lady?”

“Yes.”

“It was requested by the morning dining staff.”

“It is here.”

“Yes, my lady.”

“Then why did someone go to retrieve it?”

Mira paused.

“I believe the person who requested it did not know it had already been delivered.”

Seraphina looked at the teapot.

It was a perfectly respectable teapot. Silver, polished, expensive, and apparently capable of causing administrative confusion.

“I see.”

She did not see.

Not really.

But she had learned that saying so aloud often resulted in explanations involving departments, schedules, authority, and traditions.

All of which were considerably more exhausting than the teapot deserved.

“Who is responsible for the dining room?”

“The head housekeeper oversees the dining staff.”

“And who is responsible for delivering the silver?”

“The silver-room attendant.”

“And who confirms that the silver has arrived?”

“The dining-room supervisor.”

Seraphina considered this.

“Then why does the person who requested the teapot not know whether it arrived?”

Mira's expression became thoughtful.

“That is an excellent question, my lady.”

It was not the answer Seraphina wanted.

It was, however, the beginning of a very inconvenient morning.

The Veyr estate was not a small household.

It was a sprawling residence with several wings, private offices, reception rooms, guest accommodations, kitchens, storage rooms, staff quarters, gardens, stables, and a collection of service corridors that Seraphina suspected had been designed by someone who disliked straightforward travel.

The household employed more than two hundred people.

Some worked inside the main residence. Others maintained the grounds, prepared meals, managed supplies, cared for horses, cleaned guest rooms, handled correspondence, or performed the dozens of tasks that allowed a ducal family to live without noticing most of the work required to keep them comfortable.

Seraphina had known these things in a vague sense.

She had not previously considered what happened when the people responsible for those tasks failed to communicate.

Apparently, the answer was a silver teapot being retrieved twice.

She sat down at the small breakfast table in her private sitting room.

Mira poured tea.

“Is there a particular reason you are studying the household this morning?” she asked.

“I am not studying the household.”

Mira glanced at the notebook beside her mistress's plate.

Seraphina quietly closed it.

“I am observing.”

“Of course, my lady.”

“Those are different activities.”

“Certainly.”

Mira placed a plate of sliced pears beside the bread.

Seraphina took a piece.

“I want to understand how the household operates.”

“Would you like me to arrange a meeting with the head housekeeper?”

“No. I would like to understand how the household operates before I start arranging meetings with people.”

Mira nodded.

“That is probably wise.”

Seraphina looked up.

“Probably?”

“I only meant that the head housekeeper becomes very nervous when summoned unexpectedly.”

“Why?”

“She believes that a summons from you means something has gone wrong.”

Seraphina slowly lowered her pear.

“That is unfortunate.”

“It has been that way for some time.”

“Does she dislike me?”

“No, my lady.”

“Does she fear me?”

Mira considered her answer carefully.

“She respects your authority.”

“That is not what I asked.”

A brief silence followed.

Mira placed the teapot on the table.

“Some servants find it difficult to know what you expect from them.”

Seraphina looked at her.

“Because I am unpredictable?”

“Because you have high standards.”

“I also have a mouth. They could ask.”

Mira's expression remained polite.

“They do not always feel comfortable doing so.”

That answer was less pleasant than the teapot incident.

Seraphina leaned back.

She had spent the last several days telling herself that she wanted to become a better person.

She had written rules, reorganized her schedule, asked questions about the estate, and tried not to make assumptions.

Yet apparently, the people working in her own home still found it difficult to speak freely around her.

She could not blame them for that.

Not entirely.

“Would you help me arrange a conversation with the head housekeeper?”

“Certainly, my lady.”

“Not a formal inspection.”

“I understand.”

“And please tell her that she is not being summoned because she has done something wrong.”

Mira smiled faintly.

“I will make sure she understands.”

Seraphina picked up her tea.

“And perhaps someone should retrieve the teapot.”

Mira blinked.

“Why?”

“Because if it remains here, I may spend the entire morning thinking about it.”

The head housekeeper arrived shortly before eleven.

Her name was Margaret Ellison.

She was a woman in her late fifties, with greying brown hair secured in a neat bun and a posture so straight that Seraphina briefly wondered whether she had ever sat down in her life.

Margaret had served the Veyr household for twenty-six years.

She had managed domestic staff through three major renovations, several large receptions, two winters of unusually heavy snowfall, and one incident involving a visiting noblewoman's collection of exotic birds.

She was not easily intimidated.

She was, however, cautious around the duke's daughter.

“My lady.”

“Please sit, Mistress Ellison.”

Margaret hesitated before taking the offered chair.

Seraphina noticed.

She also noticed that the older woman kept her hands folded tightly in her lap.

“I wanted to ask you a few questions about the household,” Seraphina began.

“Certainly, my lady.”

“Nothing disciplinary.”

Margaret's shoulders relaxed by a fraction.

“I see.”

“I have realized that I know very little about how the household operates.”

Margaret looked momentarily uncertain.

Seraphina continued.

“I know there are departments, supervisors, attendants, and various responsibilities. But I do not understand how information moves between them.”

“That is a rather broad matter, my lady.”

“I suspected as much.”

Margaret waited.

Seraphina opened her notebook.

“Would you explain how a request for additional household supplies is handled?”

Margaret appeared relieved by the specific question.

“It depends on the department. The kitchen submits its requirements through the kitchen steward. Laundry supplies are requested by the laundry supervisor. Cleaning materials are requested by the floor attendants and consolidated by the senior housekeeper.”

“And who approves the requests?”

“Requests within the monthly allocation are generally approved by the steward's office. Anything exceeding the allocation requires additional authorization.”

“Who checks whether the supplies arrive?”

“The receiving clerk.”

“Who checks whether they are distributed?”

“The department supervisors.”

“And who confirms whether the department actually received what it requested?”

Margaret paused.

“The supervisors are responsible for that.”

“Do they sign a receipt?”

“Not always.”

“Why not?”

“Because the receiving clerk records the delivery.”

Seraphina wrote that down.

Then she stopped.

“Does the receiving clerk also distribute the supplies?”

“No, my lady. The stores attendants do that.”

“So the receiving clerk records the delivery, and the stores attendants distribute it, but the department supervisors do not always confirm what they received.”

Margaret looked at her notebook.

“That is correct.”

Seraphina tapped the page lightly.

“Is that how it has always been done?”

“For many years.”

“Does it work?”

Margaret hesitated.

“It works most of the time.”

Seraphina looked up.

“What happens when it does not?”

“The departments make additional requests.”

“And what happens to the original request?”

Margaret's expression changed.

“It remains in the records.”

“Along with the additional request?”

“Yes.”

Seraphina stared at her notebook.

There it was again.

Not necessarily fraud.

Not necessarily incompetence.

Just a process in which the same problem could be reported repeatedly without anyone being entirely certain whether the original problem had been resolved.

She put down her pen.

“I am not suggesting that anyone has done anything improperly.”

“I understand, my lady.”

“I am trying to understand whether our system makes mistakes difficult to notice.”

Margaret considered the question.

Then, slowly, she nodded.

“That may be so.”

It was the first answer that sounded entirely honest.

Seraphina leaned back.

“Do you have suggestions?”

Margaret looked surprised.

“My suggestions?”

“You manage the household. I do not.”

A faint crease appeared between the older woman's brows.

“I would need to consider it.”

“Please do.”

Seraphina closed the notebook.

“And I would like you to tell me when you disagree with something I suggest.”

Margaret's eyes lifted.

“My lady?”

“I am not particularly qualified to tell you how to manage the laundry.”

A silence followed.

Then Margaret's mouth moved slightly.

It might have been the beginning of a smile.

“No, my lady.”

“I'm glad we agree.”

The conversation lasted nearly an hour.

By the time Margaret left, Seraphina had learned several things.

The household had no single, comprehensive record of staff responsibilities.

Some duties were written into departmental schedules. Others were passed down verbally.

Certain positions had changed names over the years without corresponding changes to their written descriptions.

Temporary replacements were frequently assigned without formal updates to the relevant schedules.

And the senior staff often solved problems through personal familiarity rather than official procedures.

This was not necessarily bad.

A household that employed hundreds of people could not function through written instructions alone. Experience mattered. Familiarity mattered. A supervisor who knew which attendant could be trusted with fragile porcelain was useful.

But relying entirely on memory was dangerous.

Seraphina was beginning to understand why her previous-life memories had made her suspicious of vague administrative arrangements.

Unfortunately, understanding a problem did not mean knowing how to solve it.

She looked at the notebook.

There were now three pages of observations.

She disliked all three.

“Would you like lunch, my lady?” Mira asked.

“Yes.”

“Would you prefer to eat here?”

“Yes.”

“Shall I arrange it?”

“Yes.”

Mira turned toward the door.

Seraphina watched her leave.

Then she looked down at the notebook again.

There was something uncomfortable about discovering how much work other people performed simply to maintain her ordinary day.

She had been raised in a household where meals appeared, rooms were cleaned, clothes were prepared, and every small inconvenience seemed to disappear before she could notice it.

She had never needed to consider who carried the burden of making those things happen.

Perhaps that was why she had not noticed how often she gave instructions without asking what they required.

She closed the notebook.

Today, she decided, she would not create a new system.

Today, she would listen.

Unfortunately, the household had other plans.

At half past twelve, Seraphina was informed that the laundry department had requested additional linen.

At twelve forty, the kitchen steward reported that several dining-room tablecloths had been sent for washing but had not returned.

At twelve fifty, the senior housekeeper sent a message asking whether the laundry department had been authorized to use a different supplier for replacement cloth.

Seraphina stared at the three messages.

Then she called for Mira.

“Do you know what happened?”

“I believe there is a disagreement between the laundry department and the household stores.”

“About tablecloths?”

“Yes, my lady.”

Seraphina closed her eyes.

The silver teapot had multiplied.

“Please ask the head housekeeper to come.”

Margaret arrived ten minutes later, carrying a small folder.

“My lady, I apologize for the interruption.”

“Please don't apologize. What is happening?”

Margaret opened the folder.

“The laundry department reports that several tablecloths have not been returned. The kitchen believes they were sent for cleaning two days ago. The stores records indicate that replacement linen was issued last week.”

“Were the replacements received?”

“The receiving clerk recorded their arrival.”

“Were they distributed?”

“The stores attendant says they were.”

“Does the kitchen agree?”

“The kitchen steward says the department received fewer than the number recorded.”

Seraphina looked at the folder.

“Have the missing tablecloths been counted?”

“Not yet.”

“Then how do we know how many are missing?”

Margaret paused.

“We do not.”

Seraphina exhaled.

“All right. What would you normally do?”

“I would ask the laundry supervisor to explain the delay and request a fresh count from the stores.”

“And would that establish where the tablecloths are?”

“Not necessarily.”

“Then let's begin with the count.”

Margaret nodded.

“I have already asked the relevant supervisors to meet in the household office.”

Seraphina stared at her.

“You arranged that before coming here?”

“Yes, my lady.”

“Good.”

She stood.

“Let's go.”

The household office was smaller than Seraphina expected.

It was situated near the service corridor, away from the main reception rooms. Shelves lined the walls, filled with registers, schedules, supply records, and bundles of correspondence.

A broad table occupied the center.

Three people were waiting beside it.

The first was a narrow-faced man in his forties, wearing the dark uniform of the kitchen steward.

The second was a woman with broad shoulders and a practical expression, presumably the laundry supervisor.

The third was a younger man holding a ledger against his chest.

Margaret introduced them.

“Mr. Thomas Hale, kitchen steward. Mistress Agnes Porter, laundry supervisor. Mr. Edwin Clarke, receiving clerk.”

Seraphina greeted them.

All three bowed.

She gestured toward the chairs.

“Please sit.”

They did not.

Seraphina looked at Margaret.

Margaret looked at them.

“Please sit,” she repeated.

The three employees finally obeyed.

Seraphina took the chair at the end of the table.

“I understand there is a disagreement about linen.”

Mr. Hale spoke first.

“The kitchen requested twenty-four tablecloths for the main dining room and the east gallery. They were sent to the laundry two days ago. Only fourteen have returned.”

Mistress Porter frowned.

“That is not correct. Fourteen were sent to the laundry. The remaining ten were already in storage.”

Mr. Hale turned toward her.

“They were not.”

“They were recorded as being in storage.”

“Then the records are wrong.”

Mr. Clarke shifted uncomfortably.

“The stores register shows that ten replacement tablecloths were issued last week.”

“Which is not the same as saying they were delivered to the kitchen,” Mr. Hale replied.

Mr. Clarke's face tightened.

“They were signed for.”

“By whom?”

Mr. Clarke opened his ledger.

“The delivery was acknowledged by a kitchen attendant.”

“Which attendant?”

Mr. Clarke turned several pages.

“I will need to check.”

Seraphina raised one hand.

Everyone fell silent.

She looked at the three employees.

“I do not want to decide who is responsible for something before we know what happened.”

Mr. Hale lowered his eyes.

Mistress Porter folded her hands.

Mr. Clarke held his ledger more tightly.

“First,” Seraphina continued, “how many tablecloths does the kitchen currently have?”

“Fourteen,” Mr. Hale replied.

“How many does it require?”

“Twenty-four.”

“How many are in the laundry?”

Mistress Porter answered.

“Fourteen.”

“How many were sent?”

“Fourteen.”

Seraphina paused.

“Then why are we discussing twenty-four?”

Mr. Hale looked frustrated.

“Because the kitchen was supposed to receive twenty-four.”

“But did you send twenty-four?”

“No.”

“Why not?”

“Because the remaining ten were listed as already available in storage.”

Seraphina looked toward Mr. Clarke.

“Where were those ten?”

The receiving clerk opened another section of the ledger.

“The replacement delivery was received by the stores department and issued to the kitchen.”

“Can we identify the person who signed for it?”

“I believe so.”

“Please do.”

He began searching.

Seraphina turned toward Mistress Porter.

“How long does the laundry normally take to return tablecloths?”

“Two days, depending on the volume.”

“Were these fourteen delayed?”

“No. They were returned yesterday evening.”

“Mr. Hale frowned.

“We did not receive them.”

Mistress Porter looked genuinely irritated.

“They were delivered to the kitchen stores room.”

“Who received them?”

“The laundry attendant says a kitchen servant signed for them.”

Seraphina leaned back.

There was a moment of silence.

Then she asked, “Could we check the kitchen stores room?”

Mr. Hale looked surprised.

“Now?”

“Yes.”

“But the records—”

“The records have not helped us identify the tablecloths so far.”

Margaret cleared her throat.

“I can arrange for someone to accompany us.”

“Thank you.”

The kitchen stores room was located behind the main kitchen.

It smelled faintly of dried herbs, soap, and freshly baked bread.

Shelves were arranged along the walls. Wooden cupboards held spare utensils, folded linens, candles, and cleaning supplies.

Mr. Hale led them to a large storage cabinet.

“Only the kitchen supervisor and the senior attendants have access to this room.”

He opened the cabinet.

Inside were several neatly folded tablecloths.

Seraphina looked at them.

Then at the cabinet.

Then back at Mr. Hale.

“How many?”

He began counting.

One.

Two.

Three.

He stopped.

Counted again.

“Fourteen.”

Mistress Porter looked at him.

“Those are the ones returned yesterday.”

Mr. Hale blinked.

Seraphina glanced toward the cabinet.

“And the remaining ten?”

Mr. Clarke approached with his ledger.

“According to the delivery record, the replacement linen was issued to the east gallery stores.”

Mr. Hale looked confused.

“The east gallery?”

“Yes.”

“Why would the kitchen's tablecloths be sent there?”

Mr. Clarke searched the page.

“The request came from the household office.”

Margaret took the ledger.

She examined the entry.

Then she looked at Seraphina.

“My lady, the east gallery has its own linen allocation.”

“Was the request intended for the east gallery?”

“I will need to check the original requisition.”

Seraphina nodded.

“Then let's check it.”

The original request was found in the household office.

It had been submitted by the senior attendant responsible for guest accommodations.

The description read:

*Ten replacement tablecloths, east gallery, required before the spring reception.*

The delivery had been correct.

The issue was that the kitchen steward had assumed the linen was intended to replace the missing items from his department.

It was not.

The fourteen tablecloths sent to the laundry had been returned as expected.

The ten replacement tablecloths had gone to the east gallery, where they belonged.

No one had stolen anything.

No one had misplaced anything.

The kitchen had simply expected a delivery that had never been intended for it.

Seraphina read the requisition twice.

Then she placed it on the table.

“So the problem is not missing linen.”

“No, my lady,” Margaret said.

“It is a misunderstanding between departments.”

“Yes.”

Seraphina looked at Mr. Hale.

“Why did you believe the ten tablecloths were intended for the kitchen?”

“Because the kitchen had requested additional linen last week.”

“Was that request recorded?”

“Yes.”

“Was it approved?”

“Yes.”

“Was it fulfilled?”

Mr. Hale hesitated.

“I assumed it had been.”

Seraphina nodded.

“And you, Mr. Clarke, recorded the east gallery delivery correctly?”

“Yes, my lady.”

“And Mistress Porter returned the fourteen tablecloths?”

“Yes.”

Seraphina looked at the three employees.

“Then none of you has necessarily done anything wrong.”

Mr. Hale's expression softened.

She continued.

“But the departments are working from different assumptions. The kitchen believed it had received something that was actually assigned elsewhere. The stores office recorded a correct delivery, but the kitchen did not know it was unrelated to its request. And the laundry was blamed for a delay that did not exist.”

Mistress Porter looked down.

“I apologize for becoming impatient, my lady.”

“You were being accused of failing to return something you had already returned. I understand.”

Mr. Hale cleared his throat.

“I should have checked the original requisition.”

“Yes.”

He looked slightly uncomfortable.

Seraphina did not soften the answer.

“But it would also help if the department receiving a delivery had to confirm what it received, and which request it fulfilled.”

Mr. Clarke nodded slowly.

“That would require a separate acknowledgment.”

“Would that be difficult?”

“Not particularly, my lady.”

“Would it create additional work?”

“A little.”

Seraphina glanced at Margaret.

“Would it be useful?”

Margaret considered the matter.

“Yes. Especially if the delivery record included the department and the original request number.”

Seraphina nodded.

“Then perhaps we should try it.”

She looked at Mr. Clarke.

“Do not redesign the entire records system this afternoon.”

The clerk blinked.

“I had not intended to, my lady.”

“Excellent. I would prefer not to discover that we have created a second problem while solving the first.”

For the first time, Mr. Hale smiled.

It was brief, but unmistakable.

By the time Seraphina returned to her sitting room, it was nearly three.

Her lunch had been delayed.

Her tea had gone cold.

And she had acquired a new appreciation for tablecloths.

Mira helped her remove her gloves.

“Was the problem resolved, my lady?”

“Yes.”

“Was anything missing?”

“No.”

“Was anyone responsible?”

“Everyone was responsible for something, but nobody was particularly guilty.”

Mira looked puzzled.

Seraphina sat down.

“The kitchen expected ten tablecloths that had been delivered to another department. The laundry had returned what it was supposed to return. The receiving clerk had recorded the delivery correctly. The kitchen steward had assumed the delivery fulfilled his request.”

“I see.”

“It was a perfectly ordinary misunderstanding.”

“Then I am glad it was resolved.”

Seraphina rested her head against the chair.

“So am I.”

Mira poured fresh tea.

“Will you be making changes to the household system?”

“One small change.”

“Which is?”

“Delivery records should identify the department and the request they fulfil.”

Mira nodded.

“That sounds sensible.”

“It also sounds like more paperwork.”

“Would you prefer not to do it?”

Seraphina looked at her.

“Of course I would prefer not to do it.”

Mira's mouth twitched.

“But you will?”

“Yes.”

“Then I suppose it is fortunate that you are the duke's daughter.”

Seraphina stared at her tea.

“Sometimes I think being the duke's daughter is a very inefficient occupation.”

Mira smiled.

“I believe your father would disagree.”

“My father has an entire duchy. He has no right to complain.”

Later that afternoon, Seraphina received a short note from Margaret.

It was written in a neat hand.

The head housekeeper proposed that each department appoint one person responsible for confirming incoming supplies. The person would sign a simple receipt identifying the delivery, destination, and original request.

The receiving clerk would keep the master record.

The department would retain a copy.

The system would be tested in the kitchen, laundry, and household stores for one month before being extended elsewhere.

Seraphina read the proposal twice.

It was practical.

It was limited.

And most importantly, it had not been written by her.

She smiled.

Then she took a fresh sheet of paper and wrote a reply.

*Approved for a one-month trial. Please review the process with the department supervisors and inform me if it creates unnecessary delays or additional work.*

She paused.

Then added:

*Thank you for your suggestions.*

She handed the note to Mira.

“Please deliver this to Mistress Ellison.”

“Certainly, my lady.”

As Mira turned to leave, Seraphina spoke again.

“And Mira?”

“Yes?”

“Would you ask Margaret to send me the current staff responsibility schedules?”

Mira nodded.

“Of course.”

“Not because I intend to revise them.”

“I understand.”

“I only want to know what they contain.”

“Of course, my lady.”

Seraphina narrowed her eyes.

“You sound unconvinced.”

“I would never question your intentions.”

“That is exactly what someone who questions my intentions would say.”

Mira smiled politely and left.

Seraphina looked at the closed door.

She had a suspicion that her staff were becoming much too comfortable with her.

It was probably a good thing.

It was also dangerous.

Comfortable people asked questions.

Questions led to conversations.

Conversations led to responsibilities.

And responsibilities were precisely what she had been trying to avoid.

That evening, Duchess Evelyne found her daughter in the western sitting room.

Seraphina was reading a book on household administration.

Evelyne glanced at the title.

Then at the open notebook beside it.

Then at her daughter.

“Have you discovered something interesting?”

Seraphina looked up.

“Apparently, tablecloths are a complicated subject.”

Evelyne blinked.

“I beg your pardon?”

“There was a misunderstanding between the kitchen, laundry, and stores departments.”

“Was it serious?”

“No. The tablecloths were exactly where they were supposed to be.”

“Then why were you involved?”

Seraphina considered the question.

“I was nearby.”

Evelyne gave her a look.

Seraphina sighed.

“I was asked to help.”

“And did you?”

“I asked questions.”

“That is often a good beginning.”

“I also approved a small trial for delivery records.”

Evelyne took the chair opposite her.

“Was that necessary?”

“Possibly.”

“Was it your decision?”

“Margaret suggested it.”

Evelyne looked pleasantly surprised.

“Margaret?”

“Yes.”

“She has been managing this household for twenty-six years. Her judgment is usually excellent.”

“I noticed.”

Evelyne studied her daughter for a moment.

“You seem to be discovering that the household contains many capable people.”

Seraphina looked down at her book.

“I always knew that.”

“Did you?”

The question was gentle.

It was also difficult to answer.

Seraphina turned a page she had not read.

“I suppose I did not always pay attention.”

Evelyne nodded.

“People often become invisible when their work is reliable.”

Seraphina looked up.

Her mother continued.

“When everything functions, we rarely ask who made it function. We notice only when something goes wrong.”

“That seems unfair.”

“It can be.”

Evelyne folded her hands.

“But remember something, Seraphina. A household cannot be managed entirely through rules. People have experience, habits, pride, and personal circumstances. If you change every process simply because it can be improved, you may make the household less efficient rather than more.”

Seraphina nodded slowly.

“I know.”

“Do you?”

“I am beginning to.”

Evelyne smiled.

“That is better.”

She glanced toward the book.

“And what is the next subject?”

Seraphina looked at the title.

“Staff responsibilities.”

Evelyne raised an eyebrow.

“Are you certain you want to read that?”

“No.”

“Then why are you reading it?”

“Because I am beginning to suspect that if I do not understand how the household works, I will spend the rest of my life wondering why someone has sent a footman to retrieve a teapot.”

Evelyne laughed.

It was a quiet laugh, but genuine.

Seraphina felt a small sense of satisfaction.

Making her mother laugh was a pleasant achievement.

It was also considerably less exhausting than managing a department.

Later, when the household had settled for the night, Seraphina opened her notebook.

She reviewed the rules she had written after recovering her memories.

There were already several additions.

She turned to a blank page.

**Things I Have Learned About Managing a Household**

1. A large household is not a single machine. It is several smaller systems that depend on one another.
2. A correct record does not guarantee that everyone understands the same thing.
3. People who perform their work reliably are often the least visible.
4. Do not assume a problem means someone has done something wrong.
5. Ask the people who actually perform the work before changing how it is done.
6. Do not underestimate tablecloths.

She stared at the final entry.

Then crossed it out.

After a moment, she wrote it again.

It deserved to remain.

She closed the notebook and placed it beside her bed.

For once, she had not spent the day investigating suspicious payments, examining old documents, or worrying about the future.

She had helped resolve a disagreement involving household linen.

It was not particularly impressive.

It would not attract the attention of the royal court.

No noble would write a letter praising her administrative abilities.

And that was exactly how she preferred it.

Unfortunately, the Veyr estate was beginning to discover that its young mistress had become interested in how things worked.

The servants were noticing.

The department supervisors were noticing.

And Margaret Ellison, after twenty-six years of managing the household, had begun to wonder whether Lady Seraphina might actually listen when someone told her something was wrong.

That was a more significant change than the tablecloths.

Across the estate, in the duke's private office, Roland Ashford placed a sealed document on Alaric Veyr's desk.

Cedric stood beside the window.

The duke did not immediately open it.

“Is this the original correspondence?”

“Yes,” Roland replied. “Recovered from the central administrative archive. The reference was misfiled under a different expenditure category.”

“And the signature?”

“The issuing official's signature is genuine. The document itself is original.”

Alaric broke the seal.

He read the first page.

Then the second.

His expression did not change, but Cedric noticed the slight tightening of his jaw.

“What is it?” Cedric asked.

Alaric placed the pages on the desk.

“The instruction did not originate from the northern property office.”

Roland nodded.

“No, Your Grace.”

“Nor was it issued by Peter Hales.”

“No.”

Cedric moved closer.

“Then who issued it?”

Alaric looked at the name printed beneath the official seal.

For several seconds, he said nothing.

Then he turned the document so the others could read it.

The name belonged to an official from the central administration who had left his position nearly two years earlier.

And according to the personnel records, he had not been employed by the Crown when the disputed instruction was supposedly issued.

The room became quiet.

Roland adjusted his spectacles.

“That is the difficulty, Your Grace.”

Alaric looked at the document again.

“Find out who had access to his office seal.”

Cedric nodded.

“And the northern property office?”

“Continue the review. No accusations until we establish the chain of authorization.”

Roland gathered the remaining papers.

“What about Lady Seraphina?”

Alaric looked toward the window.

“She has already done enough.”

He folded the document carefully.

“She should not have to carry every problem this estate has accumulated.”

Outside, the evening wind moved through the gardens.

In her bedroom, Seraphina was already asleep.

She had spent the day thinking about linen.

She had no idea that someone had used the name of a former royal official to authorize an expenditure in her father's duchy.

Nor did she know that the document had been recovered.
