# Chapter 38 — The Officer Who Had Received the Warning

Seraphina read the name again.

Margaret Ellison.

It was an unfortunate coincidence.

The head housekeeper at Veyr Estate was also named Margaret Ellison.

She had worked for the Veyr household for twenty-six years, knew every servant's responsibility, and could identify an unauthorized visitor by the sound of his shoes.

The Margaret Ellison in the Royal Infrastructure Allocation Office had worked in the capital twenty-one years earlier.

They were almost certainly different people.

Seraphina checked the personnel details.

The capital officer had been thirty-one when the W-2 correspondence was assigned to her.

The Veyr housekeeper was now in her late fifties.

The ages were not impossible, but the employment histories were entirely different.

There was no reason to connect them.

Seraphina placed the document in the committee folder.

Then she called for Mira.

"Could you ask Margaret to come here?"

Mira nodded.

"Mrs Ellison?"

"Yes. I want to ask her about an old employment reference."

Margaret arrived ten minutes later.

She was carrying a household inventory.

"My lady?"

Seraphina showed her the document.

"Have you ever worked for the Royal Infrastructure Allocation Office?"

Margaret looked at the page.

"No, my lady."

"Have you ever been employed in the capital administration?"

"No. I came to the Veyr household directly after leaving my previous position."

"Where was that?"

"At the Rosenthal estate."

Seraphina nodded.

"Thank you. I wanted to make sure."

Margaret examined the name.

"Is someone else called Margaret Ellison?"

"Apparently."

"How unfortunate for her."

Seraphina almost smiled.

"Why?"

"Because if she worked in the capital administration, she probably spent her days explaining why documents were not where they were supposed to be."

Seraphina looked at the old correspondence.

"That seems possible."

Margaret returned to the inventory.

"Was there anything else?"

"No. Thank you."

The housekeeper left.

Seraphina looked at the document once more.

A name was only a name.

The investigation would continue with the capital officer, not the woman who managed the estate.

The Royal Infrastructure Allocation Office had been responsible for coordinating funding milestones for regional construction programmes.

It did not inspect buildings.

It did not supervise engineers.

It did not determine whether a magical circuit was safe.

Its responsibilities were financial.

It checked whether projects had met the conditions required for payment and whether allocated funds had been used for their intended purposes.

That made the W-2 correspondence important.

The regional maintenance authority had requested the missing commissioning review.

The request had reached the Allocation Office four months before the reduced settlement was approved.

The officer assigned to it was Margaret Ellison.

Seraphina wanted to know what happened next.

She wrote to Adrian Holt.

*Please obtain the Allocation Office's incoming and outgoing correspondence records for reference W-2/RC-17. Establish whether the request was answered, transferred or closed. Please include the name of the reviewing officer and any recorded instructions.*

She added a second paragraph.

*The current evidence establishes receipt of the request, not the contents of any subsequent decision.*

She sealed the letter.

Holt's reply arrived five days later.

The correspondence had been found.

It consisted of three documents.

The first was the regional authority's request.

The second was an internal review note.

The third was a routing slip.

There was no final response.

Seraphina examined the review note.

It stated:

*Commissioning report unavailable. Regional authority has requested confirmation of project status. Payment review may proceed on the basis of structural completion, subject to contract terms.*

The note bore Margaret Ellison's initials.

Seraphina read it again.

The officer had acknowledged that the commissioning report was unavailable.

She had also stated that payment review might proceed on the basis of structural completion.

But the note did not approve payment.

It did not waive the commissioning requirement.

It did not state that the project was operational.

It merely referred the matter to the contract terms.

The routing slip was more interesting.

It showed that the note had been sent to the Senior Expenditure Officer.

The name was missing from the surviving copy.

Seraphina placed the documents together.

The officer had received the warning.

She had recorded it.

She had referred the question to someone with greater authority.

What happened afterward remained unknown.

Theodore convened an additional committee meeting.

The new documents were distributed before the discussion began.

Minister Garrick examined the internal review note.

"Then the Allocation Office knew the commissioning report was missing."

"Yes," Seraphina replied.

"And it allowed the payment review to proceed."

"It allowed the review to proceed under the contract terms."

Garrick frowned.

"Is that not the same thing?"

"No."

She pointed to the sentence.

"The note does not approve the payment. It says the financial review may proceed, subject to the contract. The Senior Expenditure Officer may have been required to determine whether structural completion was sufficient for a particular payment milestone."

Mr Bellamy examined the wording.

"That is possible."

"Do we know whether the contract separated structural completion from commissioning?"

"We have not found the original schedule," Bellamy replied.

"Then we cannot establish whether the payment was permitted."

Theodore looked toward Holt.

"Can we identify the Senior Expenditure Officer?"

"The personnel register lists two possible officers assigned to the department during that period."

"Names?"

Holt read them.

"Mr Arthur Fenwick and Lady Catherine Mowbray."

Seraphina wrote both down.

"Can we determine which one received the routing slip?"

"Not from the surviving copy."

"Then the first task is to locate the original routing register."

Holt nodded.

Lucien had another discovery.

The Academy had found a technical specification for the western station.

It described the regulating assemblies as essential components of the final commissioning process.

The equipment could be installed before the circuit was ready, but it could not be activated until the final calibration had been completed.

Seraphina examined the document.

"Does this mean the station could not operate without the assemblies?"

"Not in its intended configuration," Lucien said.

"Could it have operated temporarily with an earlier arrangement?"

"Possibly, but that would have required a separate technical authorization."

"Do we have one?"

"No."

"Then we should not assume it was ever used."

Lucien nodded.

Professor Vale examined the specification.

"There is another detail. The three regulating assemblies were not all intended to be installed at the same time."

Seraphina looked at him.

"Why?"

"The first was used during preliminary calibration. The second and third were fitted after the initial distribution tests."

"Was that procedure recorded?"

"In the technical specification."

"Was it followed at W-2?"

"We don't know."

Seraphina made a note.

The equipment had arrived.

Some parts had been installed.

The final configuration had not been completed.

But the station's actual technical condition remained uncertain.

The committee turned to the financial question.

Mr Bellamy presented the surviving payment summary.

The Veylan Works settlement had been reduced by 320 crowns.

The deduction had been recorded as the value of equipment not returned.

However, the original contract schedule was still missing.

Minister Garrick examined the figures.

"If the equipment had been transferred to Central Stores, why was it deducted?"

Seraphina answered.

"That is what we need to establish."

"Could the deduction have been made before the transfer was recorded?"

"Yes."

"Could the company have requested payment before the equipment was returned?"

"Yes."

"Could the equipment deduction have been agreed as part of the settlement?"

"Yes."

Garrick put down the paper.

"You are making this extremely difficult."

Seraphina looked at him.

"The documents are making it difficult."

Theodore intervened.

"We should obtain the contract schedule and the original settlement calculation before discussing responsibility."

Mr Bellamy agreed.

After the meeting, Theodore asked Seraphina to remain.

He had the internal review note in front of him.

"Do you believe the Allocation Office acted properly?"

"I don't know."

"Even though the officer recorded that the commissioning report was missing?"

"That may have been exactly what she was supposed to do."

Theodore looked at her.

"Explain."

"She received a request from the regional authority. She recorded that the report was unavailable. She noted that payment review could proceed subject to the contract. Then she forwarded the matter to a senior officer."

"Would you have done the same?"

"Probably."

"Then where is the problem?"

"The problem may be with what happened after the note was forwarded."

Theodore considered this.

"Or with the contract itself."

"Yes."

He nodded.

"Then we should find the senior officer's decision."

Seraphina gathered her papers.

"And the contract."

"And the contract."

She stood.

"Good. I would prefer not to spend the next month discussing the same three sentences."

Theodore smiled.

"I suspect the next month will involve different sentences."

Seraphina did not find that reassuring.

The personnel register provided the first useful lead.

Arthur Fenwick had been the Senior Expenditure Officer responsible for regional infrastructure settlements.

He had retired eighteen years earlier.

Catherine Mowbray had been his deputy.

She had later become the director of a separate financial review department.

Both were still alive.

The original routing register was held in the Treasury's older correspondence archive.

The archive staff found the relevant volume.

It confirmed that the W-2 note had been forwarded to Arthur Fenwick.

A short decision had been recorded beneath the entry.

*Proceed with structural milestone review. Final settlement remains subject to commissioning evidence.*

Seraphina read it slowly.

The distinction was important.

The senior officer had permitted the structural payment review to continue.

He had not approved the final settlement.

The final settlement was still subject to commissioning evidence.

That raised a more specific question.

Why had the final payment later been processed?

The Treasury found another document.

It was a payment schedule attached to a separate contractor account.

The schedule divided the Veylan Works contract into three milestones.

The first covered structural work.

The second covered equipment delivery and installation.

The third covered commissioning and final acceptance.

The first payment had been released.

The second payment had been partially released.

The third payment was not shown.

Seraphina examined the figures.

The 1,520 crowns were not the full contract value.

They represented the first two milestones, less the 320-crown equipment deduction.

The remaining amount had been held.

She looked at Roland.

"Then the reduced payment may have been correct."

"Possibly."

"The company requested 1,840 crowns, but the Treasury approved 1,520. The difference was recorded as missing equipment."

"Yes."

"And the final settlement remained unpaid."

"According to this schedule."

Seraphina leaned back.

That changed the interpretation.

The company had not necessarily been denied its entire payment.

It might have received the amount due for completed work, while the final milestone remained unpaid because commissioning had not been completed.

The deduction still required explanation.

But the payment itself might have followed the contract.

She wrote to Holt.

*Please revise the committee's financial summary to distinguish the first two payment milestones from the final commissioning settlement. The available schedule indicates that the 1,520 crowns may have represented payment for structural and equipment work, not final acceptance.*

The next meeting was considerably calmer.

Mr Bellamy presented the revised payment schedule.

The committee agreed that the final settlement had not been approved in the surviving account.

The 320-crown deduction remained unresolved.

The original inventory and settlement agreement were still required.

Theodore looked at the table.

"So the Treasury may not have paid for work that was never completed."

"Correct," Seraphina said.

"But it did pay for the structural work and part of the equipment delivery."

"Yes."

"And the final payment was withheld."

"According to the surviving schedule."

Minister Garrick looked relieved.

"Then perhaps this is not the scandal some people expected."

Seraphina turned toward him.

"We have not established a scandal."

He nodded.

"Quite."

Lucien examined the technical report.

"That still leaves the question of why the station was never commissioned."

"Yes," Seraphina replied.

"And where the missing report went."

"Yes."

Theodore closed the folder.

"Then we continue."

The answer about the report arrived from an unexpected direction.

The Royal Infrastructure Department had received a reply from the former regional maintenance authority.

The authority's successor organization had located an old work-order register.

It contained a reference to W-2.

The entry recorded that corrective work had been scheduled.

The work order was assigned to a maintenance team.

But the completion field was blank.

There was no record of the team attending the site.

No record of the work being completed.

No record of the order being cancelled.

Seraphina examined the copy.

"Does it identify the team?"

"Yes," Holt replied.

He turned the page.

The team leader was listed as **Master Elias Rook**.

The work order had been issued twenty years earlier.

The station's final commissioning remained pending.

Seraphina looked at the date.

"Was Rook assigned to the western district?"

"Yes."

"Is he still alive?"

"We are checking."

"Was he employed by the regional authority or contracted privately?"

"The register identifies him as a regional maintenance engineer."

Seraphina nodded.

"Then we should locate his personnel record."

Holt made a note.

That evening, Seraphina reviewed the committee's progress report.

It now contained a clearer account of W-2.

The station had been structurally completed.

The equipment had been delivered.

Some components had been installed.

Commissioning had been deferred pending corrective work.

The engineer had requested that final settlement be withheld until commissioning was complete.

The file had been transferred to the Central Project Review Office.

The commissioning review had been assigned to the regional maintenance authority, but receipt had not been confirmed.

A regional request for the missing report had reached the Allocation Office.

The Allocation Office had forwarded it to a senior expenditure officer.

The senior officer had permitted structural payment review but had not approved final settlement.

The Treasury had paid the first two milestones, subject to an equipment deduction.

The final commissioning payment remained unresolved.

And a corrective work order had been issued but not completed in the surviving register.

Seraphina read the summary.

It was no longer a collection of unrelated fragments.

It was a sequence.

But it was still incomplete.

She closed the folder.

Mira entered with a letter.

"My lady, this arrived from the Royal Infrastructure Department."

Seraphina took it.

The letter concerned Elias Rook.

He had been located.

He was living in a village near the western road.

He had agreed to answer questions about the old work order.

But he had added one sentence.

*I remember attending the station, although the work order was never completed in the register.*

Seraphina read it again.

He remembered attending.

The official register did not record the work as completed.

Those two facts were not necessarily contradictory.

A maintenance engineer could visit a site, inspect the equipment and decide that no work should proceed.

Or he could begin the work and leave it unfinished.

Or the register might simply have been incomplete.

Seraphina placed the letter on the desk.

She wrote a reply requesting a formal interview.

Then she added one question:

*Please ask Master Rook whether he remembers why the work order was not marked complete.*

She sealed the letter.

The next person in the chain had finally been found.

And if his recollection was accurate, the station's unfinished history might soon become a little clearer.

Seraphina looked at the growing pile of documents.

She had wanted a quiet life.

Instead, she was now investigating a maintenance order from twenty years ago.

She took a deep breath.

At least the order had a number.

That was more than could be said for some of the kingdom's other problems.
