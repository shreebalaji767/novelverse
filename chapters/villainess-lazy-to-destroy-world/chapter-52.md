# Chapter 52 — The Orchard That Belonged to Three Different People

The western orchard had three problems.

The first was that the irrigation channel had been completed without a proper outlet inspection.

The second was that the lower trees were receiving almost no water.

The third was that nobody could agree who was responsible for fixing it.

Seraphina discovered the third problem before she reached the orchard.

Cedric was standing beside a wooden gate with two estate workers. One held a measuring rod. The other carried a rolled survey map.

Neither looked particularly pleased.

"Good afternoon," Seraphina said.

Cedric turned.

"You came."

"I was told the orchard was in trouble."

"It is."

"Has anyone died?"

"No."

"Has anything caught fire?"

"No."

"Then perhaps we should avoid describing it as a catastrophe."

Cedric looked toward the trees.

"The lower section is drying out."

Seraphina followed his gaze.

The western orchard covered a broad stretch of gently sloping land behind the estate's old stone wall. Rows of apple, pear and plum trees descended toward a narrow stream. The upper trees were healthy, but the lower rows looked noticeably weaker.

The new irrigation channel ran along the eastern edge.

It was full of water.

Unfortunately, the water was not reaching the lower section.

Seraphina examined the channel.

"Where does it end?"

Cedric pointed.

"At the old stone marker."

She walked over.

The channel ended at a flat stone structure partly covered by grass. A narrow opening had been built into its side, but it was blocked by packed soil and several small stones.

Seraphina crouched.

"Who inspected this?"

"The contractor inspected the channel."

"Who inspected the outlet?"

Cedric looked at the two workers.

Neither answered.

Seraphina sighed.

"Then the first problem is not mysterious."

The workers cleared the opening.

Water moved through the outlet for several seconds before stopping again.

One of them pushed his measuring rod into the channel.

"There is another obstruction below."

Cedric frowned.

"Where?"

"Near the old boundary wall."

Seraphina stood.

"Can we reach it?"

"Yes, my lady."

"Then let's look."

They followed the channel downhill.

The orchard became less orderly as they approached the boundary. Several older trees grew close together, their roots lifting the edges of the path. The stone wall was uneven, with a section that had been repaired using newer blocks.

At the bottom, the channel disappeared beneath the wall.

There was no visible outlet on the other side.

Seraphina examined the stones.

"Was this wall here before the irrigation work?"

Cedric nodded.

"Long before."

"Did the contractor know the channel crossed it?"

"He was given the estate map."

"Which estate map?"

Cedric looked at her.

She returned the look.

After a moment, he said, "The current one."

"Does it show an outlet?"

"No."

"Does it show the stream?"

"Yes."

Seraphina pointed at the wall.

"Then where was the water supposed to go?"

Cedric did not answer.

The estate surveyor arrived shortly afterward.

His name was Master Colin Armitage, and he had served House Veyr for eleven years.

He examined the wall, measured the channel and compared the work with the map.

"The channel was built according to the approved plan," he said.

Seraphina looked at the map.

"Then the approved plan was incomplete."

Armitage nodded reluctantly.

"The plan shows the channel reaching the boundary."

"It does not show what happens after that."

"No."

"Why?"

"The lower orchard was not included in the original irrigation proposal."

Seraphina looked at Cedric.

"You approved the proposal?"

"I approved the upper and central sections. The lower section was listed as a future improvement."

"Then why was the channel extended?"

Cedric took out a folded note.

"The contractor said extending it to the boundary would reduce the cost of future work."

Seraphina examined the note.

It was a reasonable proposal.

It was also not the same as supplying water to the lower trees.

"Was the extension included in the final inspection?"

Cedric checked.

"No."

Seraphina handed the note back.

"Then we have a channel that reaches the boundary and an orchard that needs water. Let us establish what is on the other side."

The answer arrived in the form of an elderly gardener.

His name was Thomas Barlow.

He had worked at the estate for thirty-four years and knew the orchard better than most people knew their own kitchens.

When Seraphina asked about the wall, he looked toward the old stone marker.

"That wall has always been there, my lady."

"Has the channel ever crossed it?"

"Not the new channel."

"What about the old one?"

Barlow hesitated.

"There used to be a small watercourse beneath it."

Cedric turned.

"A watercourse?"

"Yes. It ran from the upper stream toward the lower orchard."

"Where did it go?"

Barlow pointed toward the western boundary.

"Across the wall. There was an old stone culvert."

Seraphina looked at the surveyor.

"Is it on the current map?"

"No."

"Was it on an older map?"

Armitage thought.

"The previous survey might show it."

"Can you find it?"

"Yes, my lady."

Barlow added, "It was closed before I started working here."

"Why?"

"I was never told."

Seraphina looked at the wall again.

The channel had been extended to a boundary that concealed an older watercourse.

It was not a particularly complicated mystery.

But it was an irritating one.

The older survey was retrieved from the estate archive that afternoon.

It showed the orchard as it had existed sixty-three years earlier.

The old watercourse was marked beneath the western wall.

A small channel crossed into the neighboring property before turning south toward a stream.

Seraphina traced its route with her finger.

"Who owns the land on the other side?"

Cedric checked the property register.

"The Marlowe family."

"Do we have an agreement allowing the watercourse to cross their land?"

Cedric examined the register again.

"Not in the current property file."

"Does the old survey mention one?"

"No."

Seraphina looked at the map.

"Then we need to find out whether the watercourse was built under an agreement, whether it was simply a historical arrangement, or whether it was abandoned."

Cedric nodded.

"I will contact the neighboring estate."

"Good."

She stood.

"And in the meantime, can we water the lower trees manually?"

"Yes. It will require additional labor."

"Then arrange it."

Cedric gave her a brief smile.

"That is considerably easier than investigating an old agreement."

"Most things are."

The following morning, Seraphina found herself in the family archive.

She had intended to spend the day reading.

Instead, she was sitting at a long table with three old property maps, two survey registers and a document describing the western orchard's water rights.

Mira stood nearby.

"My lady, would you like tea?"

"Yes."

"Should I bring the archive clerk?"

"No. I only need the property records."

Mira hesitated.

"Your mother asked whether you would be joining her for lunch."

"I will."

"She also asked whether you were working."

Seraphina looked at the documents.

"Tell her I am investigating a tree."

Mira nodded with the seriousness of someone receiving a confidential instruction.

The first relevant document was a land agreement dated sixty-three years earlier.

It concerned the western orchard and the neighboring Marlowe property.

The agreement allowed House Veyr to maintain a narrow watercourse beneath the boundary wall.

In exchange, House Veyr was required to contribute to the maintenance of a small drainage channel on Marlowe land.

The arrangement was practical.

The watercourse served both properties.

The agreement also contained a clause stating that neither family could alter the flow without written notice to the other.

Seraphina read it twice.

Then she checked the date.

The agreement had been signed by her great-grandfather.

The neighboring family had signed through its estate representative.

It was a valid historical agreement.

But the current property register did not mention it.

Seraphina turned to the next page.

There was a handwritten note.

*Watercourse no longer in use. Maintenance responsibility to be reviewed following closure of lower culvert.*

No date was attached.

No signature appeared.

The note might have been written decades after the agreement.

It might have been an instruction.

It might simply have been a clerk's observation.

Seraphina placed it aside.

The second document was a maintenance register.

It showed that the culvert had been inspected regularly for several years.

Then the entries became less frequent.

The last recorded inspection had taken place thirty-one years earlier.

The entry stated:

*Lower culvert partially obstructed. Repair recommended before seasonal rainfall.*

The next page contained no repair record.

Instead, it listed a payment to a local stone contractor for boundary-wall repairs.

The amount was small.

The description was vague.

*Western boundary maintenance and watercourse closure.*

Seraphina frowned.

"Watercourse closure."

She checked the payment date.

It was thirty years earlier.

The payment had been authorized by the estate steward at the time.

There was no attached work order.

No completion certificate.

No confirmation from the Marlowe estate.

Seraphina wrote down the reference.

She was not going to assume the culvert had been deliberately closed without the other records.

But she was beginning to understand why the water no longer crossed the boundary.

Cedric joined her after lunch.

He had spoken with the Marlowe estate manager.

"They remember the old watercourse," he said.

"Do they have the agreement?"

"Not yet. Their archive is being searched."

"Do they know when it was closed?"

"The estate manager believes it happened during a boundary-wall repair."

"Does he know who authorized it?"

"No."

Seraphina showed him the maintenance register.

Cedric read the payment entry.

"Thirty years ago."

"Yes."

"That was before Father inherited the estate."

"Correct."

"Could the culvert have been closed because it was damaged?"

"Possibly."

"Could the wall have been repaired without consulting the Marlowes?"

"Possibly."

Cedric looked at her.

"You are very determined not to make assumptions."

"I have found that assumptions are expensive."

He smiled.

"That sounds familiar."

The next morning, the Marlowe estate manager sent a copy of an old letter.

It had been written by Lord Marlowe's predecessor.

The letter was addressed to Duke Alaric's father.

It concerned the western watercourse.

The Marlowe family had complained that the channel was overflowing during heavy rainfall.

They had requested repairs and a temporary reduction in water flow.

The letter did not request permanent closure.

It also did not authorize the destruction of the culvert.

Seraphina read the final paragraph.

*We would prefer to preserve the original arrangement if a proper repair can be made. Should the channel be unsuitable for continued use, we request that any alteration be discussed between our estates before work is undertaken.*

She placed the letter beside the payment record.

The dates were close.

The complaint had been made two months before the boundary-wall repairs.

That did not prove the repairs had violated the agreement.

But it raised a question.

Had the watercourse been closed as a temporary measure?

Or had the estates agreed to abandon it?

There was no written answer.

Alaric read the documents that evening.

He examined the old agreement, the maintenance register and the Marlowe letter.

"I remember hearing about the watercourse," he said.

Seraphina looked up.

"From whom?"

"My father. He said it had been closed after repeated flooding."

"Did he say the Marlowes agreed?"

"I do not remember."

"Was the closure permanent?"

"I believe so."

"Was there a formal agreement?"

Alaric was silent.

"I cannot tell you."

Seraphina nodded.

"Then we should not assume there was one."

Her father examined the payment record.

"The estate may have believed the work was routine maintenance."

"Perhaps."

"Or the culvert may have been unsafe."

"Perhaps."

Alaric looked at her.

"You have inherited a great deal of uncertainty."

Seraphina considered the documents.

"Apparently."

The next morning, she requested the original estate correspondence from the year of the closure.

The archive clerk found two additional letters.

The first was from the estate steward to the local contractor.

It instructed the contractor to repair the boundary wall and close the damaged culvert until further inspection.

The second was an invoice.

It listed stonework, drainage clearance and the placement of a new retaining block.

There was no final inspection report.

There was also no letter confirming that the Marlowe estate had been informed of the completed work.

Seraphina examined the contractor's name.

It belonged to a small local firm that no longer existed.

She did not expect to find much more.

Still, the documents established something important.

The culvert had been closed under an instruction that described the closure as temporary.

There was no evidence that it had ever been reopened.

There was no evidence that the neighboring family had agreed to a permanent alteration.

Seraphina wrote a summary.

*The historical watercourse was closed during boundary repairs approximately thirty years ago. The surviving instruction described the closure as temporary pending inspection. No record of subsequent inspection, reopening or formal abandonment has been located.*

She read the paragraph again.

It was not a dramatic discovery.

But it was enough to prevent anyone from treating the current boundary arrangement as unquestionably settled.

The Marlowe estate manager visited the following afternoon.

He was a broad-shouldered man named Mr. Arthur Bell, with a practical manner and a strong dislike of unnecessary ceremony.

He examined the documents in the estate's small meeting room.

"I am surprised these records were not preserved together," he said.

"So am I," Seraphina replied.

"Our estate has a copy of the original agreement. We did not have the maintenance correspondence."

"Then we have different parts of the same history."

Bell nodded.

"That seems to be a recurring difficulty."

Cedric looked at Seraphina.

She ignored him.

Bell continued.

"We are not opposed to restoring the old watercourse, provided the structure is safe and the work does not increase flooding."

"That would require a survey," Seraphina said.

"Yes."

"And a written agreement describing maintenance responsibilities."

"Agreed."

"We should also establish whether the old route is still suitable. The land has changed."

Bell nodded.

"Our surveyor can assist."

Cedric looked relieved.

"Then we can arrange a joint inspection."

"Certainly," Bell replied.

The meeting ended with a practical understanding.

No one had accused the other family of violating the old agreement.

No one had demanded compensation.

They had simply agreed to establish what remained of the old watercourse and whether it could be used safely.

Seraphina considered that a successful meeting.

The joint inspection took place four days later.

The old culvert was found beneath the boundary wall.

Its stone opening had been partly filled with rubble.

The inner passage remained intact for several yards before narrowing beneath a section of collapsed masonry.

The surveyors could not determine whether the culvert could be safely restored without further excavation.

They recommended a small exploratory survey.

They also discovered that the neighboring drainage channel had been altered during later agricultural work.

Restoring the old watercourse without examining that channel could send water into an area that had not received it for decades.

Seraphina listened to the report.

"So we cannot simply clear the blockage."

"No, my lady," the surveyor replied.

"We need to examine the entire route."

"Yes."

"And establish the current drainage capacity."

"Yes."

Seraphina looked toward Cedric.

"Then we do that before spending money on restoration."

Cedric nodded.

The lower orchard would continue receiving water through temporary irrigation.

It was not ideal.

But it was sufficient to prevent immediate damage.

A week later, the Marlowe estate sent a copy of an additional agreement.

It had been signed twenty-eight years earlier.

Unlike the original agreement, this one concerned the temporary suspension of the watercourse.

It allowed House Veyr to close the culvert while repairs were undertaken.

The agreement required both estates to review the arrangement within two years.

If neither family requested restoration, the watercourse could remain closed until a new agreement was reached.

Seraphina examined the signatures.

Both estates had signed.

The document was valid.

The watercourse had not been closed without the neighboring family's knowledge.

However, the required review had never taken place.

The old agreement had not been formally cancelled.

It had simply remained suspended.

Seraphina placed the document beside the maintenance register.

The situation was less troubling than it had first appeared.

It was also more complicated.

The orchard's water supply had been affected by a temporary arrangement that had quietly become permanent through neglect.

No one had needed to conspire.

No one had needed to falsify anything.

The families had simply failed to return to an unfinished decision.

Cedric read the new agreement.

"Then the Marlowes did know about the closure."

"Yes."

"And we were allowed to suspend the watercourse."

"Yes."

"But the review never happened."

"Correct."

He looked at the paper.

"Does this mean we need to restore it?"

"No."

"Why not?"

"Because the agreement gives us the right to review the arrangement. It does not require restoration."

Cedric nodded slowly.

"So we can decide whether the watercourse is still useful."

"After the survey."

"And if it is not?"

"Then we negotiate a formal replacement arrangement."

Cedric smiled.

"You make it sound simple."

"It is simpler than pretending an old agreement does not exist."

That evening, Seraphina placed the orchard documents in a new folder.

She wrote the title carefully.

**Western Orchard Watercourse — Historical Agreement and Maintenance Records.**

She included the original agreement, the suspension agreement, the Marlowe correspondence, the repair invoice and the recent survey report.

Then she added a note stating that the current arrangement remained under review.

Mira entered while she was finishing.

"My lady, your mother asked whether you have completed the orchard matter."

"Not entirely."

"Is something wrong?"

"No. The watercourse needs a proper survey before we decide what to do."

Mira nodded.

"Will that take long?"

"Probably."

"Then the orchard is still in trouble?"

"Not immediately. The temporary irrigation is working."

Mira smiled.

"That is good."

Seraphina closed the folder.

"It is."

The next morning, Cedric brought her a second document.

It was not about the orchard.

It was an old property agreement concerning a small mill near the northern road.

The document had been found while the archive staff searched for additional water rights.

Seraphina looked at him.

"Why are you bringing me another agreement?"

"Because the property register lists the mill as belonging entirely to House Veyr."

"And?"

"The agreement suggests that the mill's water rights were shared with three neighboring estates."

Seraphina slowly put down her tea.

"How old is it?"

"Seventy-one years."

"Has anyone reviewed it?"

"Not recently."

"Does the mill still operate?"

"Yes."

Seraphina looked at the document.

The estate had been using the mill for years.

Its accounts included maintenance costs, repairs and grain-processing charges.

She opened the agreement.

The first page described the original arrangement.

The second page contained a list of participating estates.

The third page had been amended.

Seraphina examined the amendment.

It had been signed by her grandfather.

But the signature was slightly different from the one on the original agreement.

She looked again.

The amendment transferred responsibility for the mill's maintenance costs to House Veyr.

In return, the neighboring estates were permitted to use the mill without paying the usual processing charge.

Seraphina read the clause twice.

Then she looked at Cedric.

"How long has this arrangement been in effect?"

"According to the property register, it ended twenty years ago."

"Do we have a termination agreement?"

Cedric shook his head.

"No."

Seraphina looked down at the signature again.

The amendment had been dated twenty-two years earlier.

The property register said the arrangement had ended twenty years ago.

There was no document explaining why.

She closed the folder.

"Find the original accounts for the mill."

Cedric nodded.

"And the maintenance payments?"

"Yes."

"And the records of grain processed for neighboring estates."

Cedric looked at her.

"Are you expecting another orchard?"

Seraphina stared at him.

"I am beginning to suspect that my family has been conducting business with half the kingdom without telling itself."

Cedric laughed.

Seraphina did not.

She had wanted to spend the month doing nothing.

Instead, she had discovered that the Veyr estate's property records contained agreements nobody had reviewed for decades.

The orchard had been a small problem.

The mill might be something else entirely.

And for the first time, Seraphina wondered how many of her family's responsibilities existed only because an old document had never been properly closed.
