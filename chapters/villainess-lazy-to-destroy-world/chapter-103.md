# Chapter 103 — The People Who Had Other Work to Do

The first letter came from Liora.

It was written on ordinary paper, without the Church's formal seal.

Seraphina found it beneath the morning correspondence.

Mira had placed it beside a list of estate repairs and a request for approval of the winter market's temporary stalls.

The letter was short.

Liora wrote that the capital infirmary had returned to its ordinary schedule. The emergency shelters had been closed gradually as families returned to their homes or found permanent accommodation. The Church had completed its accounting of donated supplies and transferred the remaining usable materials to its regular distribution programme.

She also mentioned that the Academy had invited her to speak about coordinating medical assistance during infrastructure emergencies.

She had accepted.

At the bottom, she added:

*I am beginning to understand that helping people during an emergency and preparing institutions to help them afterward are two different responsibilities. I would like to become better at both.*

Seraphina read the letter once more.

Then she set it aside.

"Will you reply, my lady?" Mira asked.

"Yes."

"Shall I prepare formal stationery?"

"No. She wrote on ordinary paper."

Mira brought a plain sheet.

Seraphina considered her response.

She did not write a speech about courage or duty. Liora had heard enough speeches in the capital.

Instead, she wrote that the estate's winter infirmary had received its new medical supplies, that the northern villages had completed their first round of winter health inspections, and that the temporary assistance arrangements would remain available if needed.

She wished Liora well with the Academy lecture.

Then she added one final sentence.

*Remember to eat before your lectures. An empty stomach makes even sensible people sound unreasonable.*

Mira read the sentence while folding the letter.

"Is that advice?"

"It is an observation."

"Will she understand?"

"She knows me."

The second visitor was Theodore.

He arrived without a royal carriage.

That did not mean he had come without official responsibilities. He carried a small document case and looked as though he had spent the morning reviewing reports.

Seraphina received him in the estate's eastern sitting room.

"You look tired," she said.

"The capital has been busy."

"Has something failed?"

"No."

"Then why do you look disappointed?"

Theodore gave her a dry look.

"Not every visit concerns a failure."

"Experience suggests otherwise."

He placed the document case on the table.

"The final determination has been circulated. The Directorate has accepted the revised records. The Treasury has completed the emergency expenditure schedule."

"And the loan?"

"The repayment terms have been entered into the ordinary accounts. House Veyr will receive the agreed installments."

"Good."

"The ornamental bridge project can resume when your estate accounts permit it."

"It was postponed, not cancelled."

"I remember."

"Then there is no need to mention it as though I have lost something."

Theodore looked at her.

"You are unusually protective of that bridge."

"It is a useful bridge."

"It was originally designed as an ornamental approach."

"That was the architect's mistake."

He laughed.

For a moment, neither spoke.

Then Theodore removed a single sheet from his case.

"The temporary committee has been formally dissolved. Its records are now part of the permanent administrative index. No further appointment is required."

"I received the notice."

"I wanted to tell you personally."

"Why?"

"Because you spent thirty days reviewing a matter that had remained confused for years. It seemed appropriate to acknowledge the end of it."

Seraphina regarded him.

"Thank you."

Theodore nodded.

He did not ask her to remain available.

He did not suggest another advisory position.

He did not leave a new folder on the table.

That was perhaps the most considerate thing he could have done.

Before leaving, he asked about the estate.

"The northern villages?"

"Prepared for winter."

"The eastern drainage work?"

"Delayed by two weeks."

"The market?"

"Opening on schedule."

"And the bridge?"

"Still being repaired."

Theodore smiled.

"Then things are progressing."

"They are progressing at the speed of construction."

"Which is?"

"Slower than the speed of royal announcements."

He laughed again.

This time, he left without another document.

Cedric's own responsibilities changed in the following weeks.

He had spent much of the previous year moving between estate accounts, regional correspondence, and the capital's increasingly complicated records.

Now the estate required his attention for ordinary reasons.

A tenant dispute needed mediation.

A mill required a new waterwheel.

The northern grain stores needed another inspection.

The estate's eastern drainage estimates had to be revised after the first winter rain.

None of these matters was mysterious.

Cedric seemed pleased by that.

One afternoon, Seraphina found him in the records room with three account books open.

"Are you working on the capital index?" she asked.

"No."

"Then why do you look so unhappy?"

"The mill's repair estimate is unreasonable."

"How unreasonable?"

"It includes a decorative wooden railing."

"Does the mill need one?"

"No."

"Then remove it."

"I have already done so."

"Problem solved."

Cedric looked at the estimate.

"The contractor has submitted a revised price."

"Is it reasonable?"

"Almost."

"Then negotiate."

"I am negotiating."

Seraphina nodded.

"Excellent. You have returned to your natural environment."

"What is that supposed to mean?"

"Being irritated by local contractors."

Cedric closed the book.

"I was never away from that environment."

"True."

They returned to their work.

The estate's administration did not become effortless.

It became ordinary again.

Alaric and Evelyne also settled into a quieter routine.

The Duke continued reviewing regional accounts, although he now allowed Cedric to handle more of the ordinary correspondence.

The Duchess resumed her regular visits to the estate's charitable institutions.

Neither treated Seraphina's return as a victory over the capital.

They simply made room for her in the household's daily affairs.

One evening, Evelyne found Seraphina reviewing the market's stall assignments.

"You could leave that to the steward," she said.

"I could."

"But you are not."

"I want to check the eastern vendors' allocation."

Evelyne sat opposite her.

"Are you enjoying it?"

Seraphina looked down at the list.

"Not particularly."

"Then why do it?"

"Because I want the market to work."

Evelyne smiled.

"That sounds like you."

Seraphina placed the list aside.

"Mother?"

"Yes?"

"Do you think the estate will remain quiet for a while?"

Evelyne considered the question.

"Probably not."

"That is disappointing."

"But quiet does not have to mean uneventful."

Seraphina thought about that.

Outside, the gardeners were moving the last of the autumn equipment into storage. A delivery cart crossed the courtyard. Somewhere in the kitchens, someone had dropped a metal tray.

The estate was full of small inconveniences.

It was also full of people who knew what they were doing.

"I suppose that is acceptable," Seraphina said.

Evelyne reached across the table and straightened the edge of the market list.

"It is more than acceptable."

The Academy's final technical notice arrived near the end of the month.

It confirmed that the capital's protective network remained operational under the authorized seven-zone configuration.

The central assembly had completed its scheduled inspection.

The reserve continued to balance the system within its permitted limits.

The eastern installation corrections remained stable.

The western and southern sections had passed their latest maintenance checks.

The notice did not declare the network perfect.

It did not claim that future maintenance would be unnecessary.

It stated that the system was operating within its approved parameters and that the regular inspection schedule would continue.

Seraphina read the notice.

Then she placed it in the estate's correspondence file.

Mira watched her.

"Nothing else?"

"Nothing else."

"No reply?"

"No reply is required."

"Will the capital send another report?"

"Probably."

"Will you read it?"

"If it is addressed to the estate."

"And if it is addressed to you personally?"

Seraphina closed the file.

"Then I shall consider whether it concerns anything I am responsible for."

Mira nodded.

That appeared to be the end of the matter.

On the last Sunday of the month, the winter market opened.

The stalls occupied the lower courtyard and the adjoining road. Grain merchants arrived before sunrise. A baker complained about the assigned space. Two vegetable sellers disagreed over the width of their tables.

The steward resolved the dispute.

The baker complained again.

The steward resolved that one too.

Seraphina walked through the market with Mira.

She checked the stall arrangements, inspected the temporary shelter, and spoke with the village representatives.

A child dropped a basket of apples.

Several apples rolled beneath a merchant's table.

Seraphina helped retrieve them.

The child thanked her.

Then the child ran away before she could ask whether the basket had been properly secured.

Mira watched the small figure disappear into the crowd.

"You could have asked the market steward to handle that."

"I could have."

"But you did not."

"It took less time to pick up the apples."

They continued walking.

Near the western stalls, Cedric was negotiating with a timber merchant.

Alaric was speaking to two village representatives.

Evelyne had stopped beside the charitable distribution table.

The estate's work was proceeding without requiring Seraphina to stand at the center of it.

She found that reassuring.

There had been a time when every document seemed to lead to another document, every unanswered question to another office, and every office to another person who insisted that someone else was responsible.

Now the estate's problems were smaller.

Not always easier.

But smaller.

A damaged waterwheel could be repaired.

A market dispute could be settled.

A delayed delivery could be rescheduled.

A household account could be checked against its receipts.

These were not insignificant things.

They were simply things with boundaries.

Seraphina stopped beside the market's temporary noticeboard.

The winter schedule had been pinned correctly.

The grain distribution dates were visible.

The medical inspection dates were listed beneath them.

The northern delivery route had been marked in clear handwriting.

Nothing was missing.

She examined the board for another moment.

Then she turned away.

"Shall we return to the house?" Mira asked.

"After I buy something."

Mira looked surprised.

"What would you like?"

Seraphina considered the stalls.

"Apples."

"We have apples at the estate."

"Those are estate apples."

"Are market apples different?"

"They are purchased with market money."

Mira stared at her.

Seraphina approached the nearest fruit seller.

"How much for a basket?"

The seller gave her the price.

Seraphina inspected the apples, selected a basket, and paid.

The transaction took less than a minute.

She carried the basket herself.

It was not a symbolic act.

It did not represent independence, political freedom, or a victory over an elaborate conspiracy.

She had simply wanted apples.

And for once, there was nothing else waiting for her at the end of the purchase.

The capital's records had been corrected.

The network had its proper operating authority.

The eighteen containers had been accounted for.

The emergency loan had a repayment schedule.

The temporary committee had ended.

The estate had its own work.

And Seraphina had no reason to return to the capital unless she chose to.

She walked back toward the house with Mira beside her.

Behind them, the market continued.

Ahead of them, the estate's afternoon accounts were waiting.

Seraphina adjusted the basket against her arm.

"Tomorrow," she said, "we should review the drainage estimates."

Mira sighed.

"I thought today was supposed to be quiet."

"It was."

"Then why are you planning tomorrow's work?"

"Because tomorrow is not today."

Mira considered that.

"That is technically correct."

"Thank you."

They entered the courtyard.

The doors closed behind them.

And the rest of the day passed without an emergency.
