# **Chapter 13 — Eighty Measures That Nobody Remembered**

The first thing Seraphina learned that morning was that her mother had reorganized the reception preparations.

The second thing she learned was that the Royal Academy delegation had requested a private meeting.

The third thing she learned was that Roland Ashford had not slept properly.

She considered all three developments unfortunate.

Only one of them, however, was likely to involve a grain warehouse.

She hoped.

“My lady,” Mira said, “the duchess is waiting in the eastern drawing room.”

Seraphina looked at the clock.

“Am I late?”

“No, my lady.”

“Then why is she waiting?”

“She arrived early.”

Seraphina stared at her.

“Does everyone in this household wake up with the intention of making my schedule more complicated?”

Mira considered the question.

“I believe the duchess has a different intention.”

“Which is?”

“To make the reception successful.”

“That is worse.”

Mira smiled.

Seraphina reluctantly stood.

She had been looking forward to a quiet morning.

It appeared that the kingdom had other plans.

The eastern drawing room had been transformed into a temporary planning office.

Several tables held guest lists, seating arrangements, household schedules, and correspondence from the royal capital.

Duchess Evelyne Veyr stood near the windows, speaking with Margaret Ellison.

A young clerk was arranging invitations into separate piles.

Cedric was examining a seating chart.

Seraphina stopped at the doorway.

“Why are there so many papers?”

Evelyne turned.

“Good morning, darling.”

“Good morning.”

“Come in.”

Seraphina entered cautiously.

She had learned that approaching a table covered in documents was often the first step towards losing an entire afternoon.

Evelyne handed her a guest list.

“I would like you to review the regional invitations.”

“Why?”

“Because several of the invited families have business connections with the northern trade routes. Their attendance may be useful when we discuss the upcoming procurement arrangements.”

Seraphina looked at the list.

There were twelve names.

She recognized three.

One was Baroness Amelia Rosenthal.

Another was Mistress Elinor Brant.

The third was a merchant representative from the eastern territories.

“Are these people being invited because of their business connections?”

“Some are. Others are attending because of longstanding family relationships.”

“Will there be negotiations during the reception?”

“Certainly not.”

Seraphina looked relieved.

Evelyne smiled.

“The reception is a social event. We are not turning the dining hall into a trading office.”

“Thank you.”

“However, people may discuss their interests. It is difficult to prevent conversation.”

Seraphina examined the guest list.

“Then why am I reviewing it?”

“Because you know which merchants have recently worked with the northern offices. You may notice someone who should be included.”

Seraphina looked at the list again.

“Should Mistress Brant attend?”

“She has been invited.”

“Good.”

“And the Academy delegation?”

“They have confirmed their attendance.”

Seraphina sighed.

Evelyne noticed.

“You are concerned about their presence.”

“I am not particularly interested in magical infrastructure.”

“That does not mean you cannot listen to a discussion about it.”

“I would prefer not to become involved.”

“You need not become involved in every subject you hear about.”

Seraphina looked at her mother.

“That is a very sensible distinction.”

“I occasionally make them.”

Cedric coughed.

Seraphina ignored him.

Evelyne continued.

“The Academy delegation is coming to discuss regional facilities. They have asked to meet the duke, the agricultural administrators, and the household representatives responsible for estate maintenance.”

“Why household representatives?”

“Because some of the facilities are connected to private land and regional transport.”

Seraphina considered this.

“Can I simply attend the reception and not the meeting?”

“Of course.”

Evelyne studied her for a moment.

“But if you do attend, I would like you to listen carefully before deciding that you have no interest.”

Seraphina had no answer to that.

Her mother returned to the seating arrangements.

The conversation was over.

Seraphina placed the guest list on the table.

She had survived.

For now.

The messenger arrived shortly after breakfast.

He delivered a sealed packet to the ducal financial office.

The seal belonged to the northern property administration.

The accompanying register was old.

Its pages had been folded and unfolded so many times that the edges had become soft.

Roland Ashford was examining the previous day's procurement comparison when the clerk brought it in.

Roland checked the reference number.

Then he read the first page.

Then the second.

He stopped at the third.

He called for Cedric.

Seraphina was in the library when Cedric found her.

She was reading a book about the history of the northern agricultural system.

She had selected it because it was unlikely to require any immediate decisions.

Cedric entered without knocking.

She looked up.

“That is concerning.”

“What is?”

“You.”

He placed a document on the table.

“Roland found something.”

Seraphina closed the book.

“About the merchants?”

“The old reserve programme.”

She sat straighter.

“What happened?”

Cedric opened the document.

“An additional delivery was recorded for N-14.”

Seraphina looked at the page.

The writing was faded but readable.

**Destination: N-14.**

**Quantity: 80 standard measures.**

**Receiving officer: Martin Hale.**

**Payment status: Settled.**

She read it twice.

“Is this the same Martin Hale Mistress Brant mentioned?”

“Yes.”

“When was the delivery?”

Cedric pointed to the date.

“Three months after the final recorded replenishment.”

Seraphina looked up.

“Then why does the register say the programme had ended?”

“That is what Roland is trying to establish.”

She studied the entry again.

“Does it say who supplied the grain?”

“The supplier reference is on the next page.”

Cedric turned it.

The reference was a name she had seen before.

**Brant & Sons Agricultural Trading.**

Seraphina looked at him.

“Mistress Brant's family company.”

“Yes.”

“Does the register show payment?”

“It says settled.”

“Does it show the amount?”

“No.”

“Does it show a delivery certificate?”

“Not in this copy.”

“Then we know that someone recorded a delivery, but we do not yet know whether the grain arrived.”

Cedric nodded.

“That is Roland's position.”

Seraphina leaned back.

The discovery was important.

It was also incomplete.

She was beginning to suspect that the most dangerous thing in an archive was not a false document.

It was a document that looked complete.

“Where is Roland?”

“In the financial office.”

“Let's go.”

Roland had arranged the relevant records across his desk.

There were four documents.

The first was the old procurement register.

The second was a payment summary.

The third was a warehouse inventory from N-14.

The fourth was a letter from the regional office.

Roland looked up as Seraphina entered.

“My lady.”

“Show me what you found.”

He pointed to the first document.

“This is the entry from the procurement register. It records eighty standard measures delivered to N-14 by Brant & Sons Agricultural Trading.”

“And the payment summary?”

“It records the contract as settled.”

“Does it identify the payment date?”

“Yes. The payment was authorized six weeks after the delivery entry.”

“Does the payment register show that money actually left the treasury?”

“It records a completed payment. We still need the corresponding treasury entry.”

Seraphina nodded.

“And the warehouse inventory?”

Roland placed the third document beside the first.

“The inventory was prepared approximately two months after the recorded delivery.”

She examined the figures.

The register showed an opening balance.

Then several withdrawals.

Then a remaining quantity.

The numbers did not immediately make sense.

“Does this inventory include the eighty measures?”

“It is not clear.”

“Why?”

“Because the inventory uses a different reference system. It records total stock by grain type, not by individual delivery.”

Seraphina frowned.

“Could the eighty measures have been included in the total?”

“Yes.”

“Could they have been distributed?”

“Yes.”

“Could they have been spoiled?”

“Yes.”

“Could they have never arrived?”

Roland nodded.

“That is also possible.”

She looked at the fourth document.

“What is this?”

“A letter from the regional office requesting confirmation of the delivery.”

Seraphina read it.

The letter had been written several months after the payment was authorized.

It asked whether the grain had been received and properly entered into the reserve inventory.

The response was missing.

She placed the letter down.

“So the regional office itself was uncertain.”

“Yes.”

“Did anyone follow up?”

“There is no further correspondence in the packet.”

Cedric looked at Roland.

“Then how was the payment marked settled?”

Roland folded his hands.

“That is the question.”

The next hour was spent establishing what the documents could actually prove.

They agreed on five points.

First, a delivery of eighty standard measures had been recorded against N-14.

Second, the supplier was Brant & Sons Agricultural Trading.

Third, payment had been marked as settled in the procurement register.

Fourth, a later inventory did not identify the delivery separately.

Fifth, the regional office had requested confirmation but no response had been found.

Seraphina looked at the list.

“Do we have proof that the grain was delivered?”

“No,” Roland said.

“Proof that it was not delivered?”

“No.”

“Proof that the payment was received by the merchant?”

“Not yet.”

“Then we have an unresolved transaction.”

“Yes.”

Cedric looked at the old register.

“Eighty measures is not a small amount.”

“No,” Roland said.

“Could it have been an emergency distribution?”

“Possibly.”

“Would that explain the missing inventory entry?”

“If the grain was immediately transferred to another location, yes. But there should be a corresponding transfer record.”

“Could the transfer record have been lost?”

“Yes.”

Seraphina looked at Roland.

“You are very good at explaining how something could have happened.”

Roland gave her a tired look.

“I am an accountant, my lady. I am not a prophet.”

She nodded.

“Fair.”

Cedric examined the dates.

“There is another issue. The payment was authorized six weeks after the delivery. If the grain had been distributed immediately, why was the confirmation requested several months later?”

Roland considered this.

“It may have been a routine reconciliation.”

“Or the original confirmation was missing.”

“Also possible.”

Seraphina leaned forward.

“Do we know whether the warehouse was operational at the time?”

Roland turned to another page.

“The historical property register lists N-14 as active.”

“Does that mean it was usable?”

“No.”

“Then we need to know its condition at that time.”

Cedric nodded.

“I can ask the regional office for historical inspection reports.”

“Please do.”

Seraphina looked at the old register again.

There was a handwritten note in the margin.

She had not noticed it earlier.

The writing was faint.

**Transfer pending confirmation from western receiving office.**

She pointed to it.

“What does that mean?”

Roland leaned closer.

“I cannot say.”

“Does the western receiving office still exist?”

“Not under that name. Its responsibilities were transferred to the central regional administration.”

“Can we find its old records?”

“We can try.”

Seraphina sat back.

Another office.

Another transfer.

Another missing document.

She was beginning to feel that the old administration had been built by people who believed filing a document somewhere was the same as ensuring someone could find it.

At noon, Alaric summoned them.

The duke listened while Roland explained the findings.

He did not interrupt.

When Roland finished, Alaric looked at the documents.

“Has the merchant been contacted?”

“Not yet,” Roland said. “We wanted to confirm the entry first.”

“Good. Contact Mistress Brant. Request her family's records, but make it clear that this is a historical verification, not an accusation.”

Seraphina nodded.

“That is important.”

Alaric looked at her.

“Do you believe the delivery was made?”

“I don't know.”

“Do you suspect the payment was improper?”

“I don't know.”

“Then what do you believe?”

Seraphina thought carefully.

“I believe the record is incomplete. I believe the transaction should have had a receiving confirmation. I believe the payment was recorded as settled despite the missing confirmation. I do not know why.”

Alaric nodded.

“That is a fair assessment.”

He turned to Roland.

“Preserve the original register. Obtain copies for examination. No one is to alter the original entries.”

“Yes, Your Grace.”

“Cedric, find the historical warehouse records.”

“Understood.”

Alaric looked at Seraphina.

“And you?”

She hesitated.

“I would like to ask Mistress Brant about the old company records.”

“You may do so, provided you do not lead her towards a conclusion.”

“I understand.”

“Good.”

Seraphina stood.

She had been hoping to avoid becoming personally involved.

But the merchant had mentioned her father.

And now her family's administration had produced a record connected to the same company.

She could hardly ignore it.

Mistress Brant arrived that afternoon.

She looked surprised when Seraphina requested a meeting.

But she agreed.

They met in the smaller drawing room.

Roland was present to record the discussion.

Cedric remained nearby with the documents.

Seraphina placed a copy of the register on the table.

“Do you recognize this?”

Mistress Brant examined it.

Her expression changed.

“Where did you find this?”

“In the northern property records.”

She read the entry.

Then she looked at the date.

“My father was still managing the company at this time.”

“Do you remember anything about the transaction?”

“No.”

“Could you check your family records?”

“Yes.”

Mistress Brant continued examining the page.

“Eighty measures?”

“Yes.”

She turned to Tobias, who had accompanied her.

“Do we still have the old archive?”

“Some of it. The main records were moved when the company changed ownership.”

“Can you check the procurement ledgers?”

“I can.”

Mistress Brant looked back at Seraphina.

“Was this delivery marked as disputed?”

“The register says settled. A later letter requests confirmation.”

“That is unusual.”

“Why?”

“Because my father was very particular about payment records. He would not normally leave a contract without a signed receiving certificate.”

Roland looked up.

“Do you have copies of the certificates?”

“Possibly.”

“Would you be able to search for them?”

Mistress Brant nodded.

“I will.”

Seraphina considered her next question.

“Did your father ever mention problems with the old reserve programme?”

Mistress Brant was quiet for a moment.

“He mentioned that the programme had become difficult to manage. There were frequent changes in delivery destinations. Some grain was sent to villages directly instead of being stored. Other quantities were transferred between warehouses.”

“Were the transfers recorded?”

“They were supposed to be.”

Roland made a note.

“Did he ever mention N-14?”

“I do not remember.”

“What about N-09?”

“No.”

Seraphina waited.

Mistress Brant looked down at the document again.

“There is one thing I do remember.”

“What is it?”

“My father once complained that the administration had asked him to supply grain to a warehouse that did not appear on the official delivery map.”

Seraphina looked at Roland.

“Do you know which warehouse?”

Mistress Brant shook her head.

“He did not say. I was much younger.”

“Do you remember when?”

“During the same winter, I believe.”

Seraphina nodded.

“Thank you. Please do not assume that the missing delivery is connected to that incident. We are still trying to establish the facts.”

Mistress Brant gave her a thoughtful look.

“You are very careful.”

“I am trying to be.”

“Your father would have appreciated that.”

Seraphina did not know what to say.

She settled for a small nod.

The next morning, Roland received a reply from the regional archives.

The old western receiving office had not maintained a complete record of every transfer.

However, one index book survived.

It contained references to deliveries, warehouse movements, and temporary storage arrangements.

The relevant entry was brief.

**N-14: Eighty measures received from Brant & Sons.**

**Temporary transfer authorized.**

**Destination: Northern Consolidated Depot.**

**Receiving confirmation pending.**

Roland read it three times.

The entry appeared to answer one question.

The grain had not necessarily been delivered to N-14.

It might have been transferred elsewhere.

But the destination was unfamiliar.

There was no Northern Consolidated Depot in the current property register.

There was no modern map reference.

There was no maintenance record.

And there was no confirmation that the grain had ever arrived.

Roland sent for Cedric.

Cedric sent for Harold Fenwick.

Harold sent for the regional surveyor.

And the regional surveyor, after examining the old index, made a suggestion.

The Northern Consolidated Depot might have been a temporary storage site established during the emergency winter.

If so, it could have been dismantled or abandoned decades ago.

The old road network might provide clues.

But no one could identify its location from the surviving records.

Seraphina received the news after lunch.

She was sitting with Evelyne, reviewing the final seating arrangement for the spring reception.

Her mother had just moved two guests to another table when Cedric entered.

“Excuse me, Your Grace.”

Evelyne looked up.

“What is it?”

“We have an update on the old reserve records.”

Seraphina put down the seating chart.

“Did they find the depot?”

“No. But the western receiving index contains a transfer entry.”

He explained.

Evelyne listened quietly.

When he finished, she looked at Seraphina.

“Does this mean the grain was delivered?”

“Not necessarily,” Seraphina said.

Cedric nodded.

“The index records a temporary transfer to a depot that cannot currently be identified.”

Evelyne folded her hands.

“And the payment?”

“Still marked as settled.”

Seraphina considered the problem.

“If the grain was moved to a temporary depot, then the original N-14 inventory would not necessarily show it.”

“Correct,” Cedric said.

“But the receiving confirmation should exist somewhere.”

“Or it was never completed.”

“Can we establish whether the depot was a physical building?”

“We are trying to.”

Evelyne looked at the documents.

“Then this may not be a missing delivery. It may be a missing location.”

Seraphina looked at her mother.

“That is possible.”

Evelyne smiled faintly.

“You are learning to distinguish between the two.”

Seraphina glanced at the seating chart.

“I wish I were learning how to distinguish between a reception and a procurement inquiry.”

Evelyne laughed.

“You may have to attend both.”

Seraphina looked deeply unhappy.

That evening, she returned to her room.

Mira was arranging the final outfit for the reception.

A dark green dress had been selected, with modest silver embroidery along the sleeves.

Seraphina examined it.

“Is this necessary?”

“It is appropriate for the occasion.”

“Could I wear something less elaborate?”

“You could.”

“Would my mother object?”

“Probably.”

Seraphina sighed.

Mira placed the dress carefully over the chair.

“Your mother also asked me to remind you that the Academy delegation will be seated near the eastern gallery.”

“Why?”

“So they may speak with the duke and the regional administrators.”

“Will the saintess attend?”

“I do not know, my lady.”

Seraphina looked at her.

“Have you heard anything?”

“Only that several representatives from the Church have been invited.”

She sat down.

The Church.

The Academy.

The royal capital.

The spring reception.

The original novel's plot was becoming uncomfortably close to her current life.

She opened her notebook.

For a moment, she considered adding another rule.

Then she stopped.

There were already seventeen.

She did not need a rule for every inconvenience.

Instead, she wrote a single sentence beneath the others.

**Do not confuse remembering the story with understanding the world.**

She stared at the words.

The original novel had described the old reserve programme in a few paragraphs.

A winter shortage.

A political disagreement.

An accusation against a noble family.

The villainess had used the crisis to gain influence over the regional administration.

That was all she remembered.

But the actual situation was not a few paragraphs.

It was a merchant who might have delivered grain to a temporary depot.

An accountant trying to locate a payment record.

A regional administrator searching old maps.

A village waiting for repairs.

And a warehouse whose history had been divided between offices that no longer existed.

She closed the notebook.

Tomorrow, there would be another meeting.

There would probably be another document.

Perhaps another location.

Perhaps another person who remembered only part of what happened.

Seraphina placed the notebook in her drawer.

Then she looked at the dress.

At least the dress would not require an audit.

Far from the Veyr estate, in a small village near the eastern trade road, an elderly man received a letter.

His name was Martin Hale.

He had once served as a purchasing clerk during the northern emergency reserve programme.

He had retired more than twenty years ago.

The letter came from Mistress Elinor Brant.

It asked whether he remembered an old grain delivery involving N-14 and a temporary storage depot.

Martin read the letter.

Then he read it again.

For several minutes, he sat without moving.

Finally, he opened a wooden chest beside his bed.

Inside were old correspondence books, faded receipts, and several folded maps.

He searched through them until he found a narrow sheet of paper.

It was yellowed with age.

The ink had faded.

But the handwriting remained legible.

At the top was a reference:

**Northern Consolidated Depot — Temporary Register.**

Below it were three entries.

**N-14 — 80 measures.**

**N-09 — 120 measures.**

**Southmere N-03 — 60 measures.**

And beneath them, in a different hand:

**All quantities transferred under emergency instruction. Final reconciliation to be completed by central office.**

Martin stared at the note.

He remembered the winter.

He remembered the carts.

He remembered the instructions arriving late.

And he remembered that the final reconciliation had never been completed.

He folded the paper carefully.

Then he wrote a reply.

He did not yet know whether he should send it.

Because the name written at the bottom of the emergency instruction was one he had not spoken aloud in twenty-six years.

**End of Chapter 13**
