# Chapter 9 — I Only Wanted Better Storage

Seraphina Veyr had made a terrible mistake.

She had asked a question.

Not an especially complicated question, either.

She had merely asked where Northern Storehouse N-14 was located.

Unfortunately, the answer had required three letters, two meetings, a map, and the temporary emotional collapse of a regional clerk.

By the time Roland Ashford arrived at her sitting room, Seraphina had already begun regretting her curiosity.

“Good morning, my lady.”

“Is it?”

Roland looked at the papers spread across her desk.

“I take it you have received the updated agricultural summary.”

“I have.”

“And you noticed the footnote.”

“I did.”

Roland placed a separate folder beside the report.

“I have been examining the records since yesterday afternoon.”

Seraphina gestured toward the chair.

“Please sit. Tell me what N-14 is.”

Roland opened the folder.

“An old storage building registered under the northern agricultural district.”

“Where?”

“That is the difficulty.”

Seraphina waited.

“The building appears in several historical records, but its location is not consistently identified.”

She looked at him.

“How can a building have a registration number without anyone knowing where it is?”

Roland adjusted his spectacles.

“Because the registration predates the current district mapping system.”

“That sounds inconvenient.”

“It is.”

He unfolded a map.

The northern district was divided into several administrative sections. Each section contained villages, agricultural properties, roads, storage facilities, and smaller holdings.

Roland pointed toward the western edge.

“N-14 was originally listed as a reserve grain store serving the upper agricultural route.”

“Originally?”

“The earliest record I found is from thirty-one years ago. It was built to hold emergency grain during periods of poor harvest.”

“Is it still used?”

“I cannot confirm that.”

“Is it included in the current inventory?”

“No.”

Seraphina stared at him.

“Then why is it included in the agricultural summary?”

Roland turned another page.

“Because the building still appears in the consolidated storage register. It was carried forward from the old records.”

“Does anyone inspect it?”

“Not according to the recent inspection schedule.”

“Does anyone maintain it?”

“Not according to the maintenance register.”

“Does anyone know whether there is grain inside?”

Roland paused.

“That is what I am trying to establish.”

Seraphina looked at the map again.

A small mark appeared near the upper agricultural route.

It was not accompanied by a village name.

Only the code N-14.

“Could it be abandoned?”

“Possibly.”

“Could it have been demolished?”

“Possibly.”

“Could it still be in use?”

“Also possible.”

She leaned back.

“So we have a building that may or may not exist, that may or may not contain grain, and that may or may not be included in our current records.”

Roland nodded.

“That is a fair summary.”

Seraphina looked at the ceiling.

“I wanted to improve the storage records.”

“I understand, my lady.”

“I did not intend to discover an archaeological mystery.”

Roland carefully closed the folder.

“Perhaps it would help to consider this a records reconciliation exercise.”

Seraphina looked at him.

“That is a very accountant-like way of making something sound less alarming.”

“I am an accountant, my lady.”

“Yes. I had noticed.”

The first step was to locate the original records.

Roland had already requested documents from the northern property office.

They arrived shortly before noon.

The bundle contained old inventory lists, maintenance schedules, maps, and several handwritten transfer records.

Seraphina examined them with Roland and Cedric.

The earliest register was surprisingly clear.

N-14 had been built during the reign of King Edmund II, when a series of poor harvests had forced the Crown and regional estates to establish emergency grain reserves.

The building was designed to store approximately six hundred standard measures of grain.

It had thick stone walls, raised wooden flooring, ventilation openings, and a separate drainage channel.

Its purpose was straightforward.

If a village suffered a crop failure, the reserve could be used to provide grain until the next harvest.

Seraphina found the idea sensible.

She also found the subsequent records less reassuring.

Twenty years after its construction, the storehouse had been transferred from direct estate management to the northern agricultural district.

A decade later, its maintenance responsibility had been reassigned to the regional property office.

Several years after that, the agricultural register had been updated to include the building under the current inventory system.

Then the entries became increasingly vague.

*Storage reserve retained.*

*Maintenance deferred.*

*Inspection pending.*

*Status unchanged.*

Seraphina tapped the final entry.

“What does status unchanged mean?”

Roland examined the page.

“It may mean the building remained registered under the same classification.”

“Or that nobody checked it.”

“Yes.”

Cedric turned another page.

“There is a transfer record here.”

Seraphina looked at him.

“What kind?”

“The responsibility for the building was transferred to the regional agricultural office eleven years ago.”

“Was the building inspected at the time?”

Cedric read the note.

“It says the property was accepted in its existing condition.”

“Was that condition described?”

“No.”

Seraphina looked at Roland.

“Is there a signed inventory?”

Roland searched the folder.

“Not among these documents.”

She sat back.

The building's history was beginning to resemble a conversation in which everyone had agreed that someone else would take care of something.

Unfortunately, nobody had written down who.

“Can we establish the last confirmed inspection?”

Roland checked the dates.

“Seven years ago.”

“Seven?”

“Yes.”

“What did it say?”

He found the report.

The document was short.

*Structure standing. Roof condition acceptable. Interior inaccessible due to damaged entrance lock. No further action required at present.*

Seraphina stared at the sentence.

“Interior inaccessible?”

“Yes.”

“And no further action was required?”

“Apparently.”

She closed her eyes.

“Why?”

Roland did not answer immediately.

Cedric read the report again.

“Perhaps the inspector believed the lock could be repaired later.”

“Was it?”

“There is no record.”

Seraphina opened her eyes.

“Then the building has not been properly inspected for seven years.”

“That appears to be the case.”

“And we have been counting it as an available reserve?”

Roland hesitated.

“It has remained on the register.”

“That was not my question.”

“No, my lady. We cannot currently treat its contents as available stock.”

Seraphina nodded.

“Good. Then please make sure the next summary reflects that.”

Roland took a note.

She continued.

“And do not remove the building from the register until we know whether it exists and who is responsible for it.”

Cedric glanced at her.

“That may take some time.”

“I know.”

She looked at the old map.

“Where is the nearest village?”

Cedric pointed toward the lower section.

“Westmere.”

Seraphina frowned.

“Westmere?”

“Yes. The old route passes through the village before continuing north.”

She looked at the map again.

The storehouse was farther from the main road than she had expected.

“Could the villagers know where it is?”

“Possibly.”

Seraphina slowly lowered the map.

She had visited Westmere only yesterday.

She remembered the elder, Alden Mercer.

She remembered the damaged irrigation channel, the storage building, and the farmers.

She also remembered the old northern route.

“Could we ask the village elder?”

Roland nodded.

“We could send a letter.”

Seraphina looked at the window.

She was not going to travel again.

Not immediately.

She was absolutely not going to travel again.

“Send a letter,” she said.

Cedric smiled.

“A wise decision.”

Seraphina narrowed her eyes.

“Do not sound so relieved.”

The letter was sent the following morning.

It asked Alden Mercer whether anyone in Westmere knew the location or history of the old northern storehouse.

It also requested any surviving documents relating to the building.

Seraphina expected a response within several days.

Instead, a reply arrived the next afternoon.

It was short.

*The building is known to some older residents as the Old Hill Store. It stands beyond the northern fields, near the abandoned stone road. My grandfather spoke of it as a place where grain was kept during difficult winters.*

The letter continued.

*I have not entered the building myself. The path is overgrown, and the entrance has been damaged for many years.*

Seraphina read it twice.

Then she placed it on the desk.

“Of course.”

Mira looked up.

“Something wrong, my lady?”

“The building exists.”

“That is good news, is it not?”

“It means we now have a building to inspect.”

Mira smiled.

“Would you like me to arrange another visit?”

“No.”

The answer came immediately.

Mira waited.

Seraphina sighed.

“Not yet.”

The regional agricultural administrator arrived three days later.

His name was Harold Fenwick.

He was a middle-aged man with thinning hair, a carefully trimmed beard, and the slightly strained expression of someone who had spent too much time dealing with questions from senior officials.

He bowed to Seraphina.

“My lady.”

“Thank you for coming, Master Fenwick.”

He sat opposite her.

Roland and Cedric were also present.

Seraphina placed the old inventory register on the table.

“I would like to discuss Northern Storehouse N-14.”

Fenwick's expression changed.

Only slightly.

But Seraphina noticed.

“Certainly, my lady.”

“Are you familiar with the building?”

“I am familiar with the registration.”

“That was not quite what I asked.”

He cleared his throat.

“The storehouse is listed under the northern agricultural reserve.”

“Do you know where it is?”

“Yes.”

“Have you visited it?”

“No.”

“Has anyone from your office visited it recently?”

“Not recently.”

“How recently?”

Fenwick looked toward Roland.

Roland answered.

“The last confirmed inspection was seven years ago.”

Fenwick nodded.

“That sounds correct.”

Seraphina folded her hands.

“Why is it still included in the active storage register?”

“The building has not been formally decommissioned.”

“Why not?”

“There has been no instruction to do so.”

“Has anyone confirmed that it remains suitable for storing grain?”

Fenwick hesitated.

“Not in recent years.”

“Then why is it listed as an available reserve?”

The administrator shifted in his chair.

“It is a historical reserve property.”

Seraphina looked at him.

“Is that the same as an available reserve?”

“No, my lady.”

“Then the distinction should be clear in the records.”

Fenwick nodded.

“Certainly.”

She continued.

“I also understand that the building's maintenance responsibility was transferred to your office eleven years ago.”

“That is correct.”

“Was an inventory of its condition prepared at the time?”

“I would need to check the transfer documents.”

“Please do.”

Fenwick glanced at the papers.

“May I ask why this matter has become a priority?”

Seraphina considered the question.

“Because our agricultural reports include a storage location whose condition and contents have not been verified. We are using those reports to understand the availability of grain reserves.”

“That is a reasonable concern.”

“I am glad we agree.”

Fenwick looked uncomfortable.

Seraphina continued.

“I do not believe there is necessarily misconduct involved. The records may simply have been carried forward without proper review. But we cannot treat an uninspected building as a functioning grain reserve.”

Fenwick nodded.

“I understand.”

“Good. Then I would like your office to arrange an inspection.”

“Certainly.”

“Before that, I would like to know whether the building can be reached safely.”

Fenwick hesitated again.

“The northern path is not maintained.”

“Is it passable?”

“I believe so.”

“Have you checked?”

“No, my lady.”

Seraphina looked at Cedric.

Cedric made a note.

She returned her attention to Fenwick.

“Then please do not tell me that it is passable until someone has checked.”

The administrator lowered his eyes.

“Of course.”

The inspection was scheduled for the following week.

Seraphina was not invited to attend.

This was a decision she supported enthusiastically.

Instead, Roland arranged for two maintenance officers and a representative from the agricultural department to travel to the site.

They were instructed to examine the building's exterior, inspect the roof and drainage, assess the entrance, and determine whether it was safe to enter.

They were not to remove or transfer any grain until the contents had been properly recorded.

Seraphina reviewed the instructions.

“Why are there three people?”

“One from maintenance, one from agriculture, and one to record the inventory,” Roland explained.

“Could one person do all three?”

“Not reliably.”

She nodded.

“Fine.”

Cedric looked at the instructions.

“You have also asked them to photograph the structure.”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“So we have a record of its condition.”

Cedric looked mildly amused.

“You have become rather fond of documentation.”

Seraphina gave him a tired look.

“I have become fond of not having to repeat the same conversation five times.”

Roland made a quiet sound that might have been a laugh.

The inspection team departed two days later.

For the first time since the discovery of N-14, Seraphina was able to spend an entire morning without hearing about it.

She considered this a success.

Then the kitchen steward sent a message asking whether the household supply trial should be extended to the stables.

She stared at the note.

“Why?”

Mira looked at it.

“Perhaps the stable department has heard about the new records.”

Seraphina closed her eyes.

The household was becoming organized.

She was beginning to regret encouraging it.

The inspection report arrived four days later.

Roland brought it personally.

He looked more serious than usual.

Seraphina placed her book aside.

“Is the building standing?”

“Yes, my lady.”

“Is it safe?”

“Not entirely.”

He opened the report.

The storehouse had been located beyond the northern fields, near an old stone road that was no longer used by ordinary carts.

Its exterior walls remained intact.

The roof had suffered damage along the western side.

Several tiles were missing, and one wooden support beam showed signs of decay.

The entrance door had been damaged.

The drainage channel was partly blocked by soil and vegetation.

The building was not suitable for storing grain without repairs.

Seraphina nodded.

“That is disappointing, but not surprising.”

Roland turned to the next page.

“The interior contains grain.”

She looked up.

“How much?”

“The inspectors have not completed the full count. The sacks are stacked along the eastern wall, and several have deteriorated.”

“Do they know who owns it?”

“The labels indicate that the grain was placed there under the northern reserve programme.”

“From when?”

Roland paused.

“The oldest visible labels are dated twenty-six years ago.”

Seraphina stared at him.

“Twenty-six?”

“Yes.”

“Is that possible?”

“The inspectors believe some of the sacks may have been replaced more recently, but the inventory records are incomplete.”

She leaned forward.

“Can the grain still be used?”

“Not until it has been examined.”

“Has anyone checked for contamination?”

“Preliminary inspection only. The agricultural officer has requested a proper assessment.”

Seraphina nodded.

“Good. Nothing should be distributed until it has been examined.”

Roland turned another page.

“There is also a record of a transfer.”

“What kind?”

“A document showing that the storehouse was supplied with grain during the reserve programme.”

“Is it signed?”

“Yes.”

“By whom?”

Roland looked at the paper.

“By the regional reserve officer at the time.”

Seraphina waited.

“Is there a problem?”

“The officer retired twenty-two years ago. His name appears in several other historical records.”

“That does not sound unusual.”

“No. But the quantity listed in the transfer document is different from the quantity in the old reserve register.”

Seraphina slowly leaned back.

“How different?”

“Approximately eighty standard measures.”

She looked at the report.

“Could that be a measurement error?”

“Possibly.”

“Could it be a difference in the way the grain was recorded?”

“Also possible.”

“Could the grain have been used?”

“Yes.”

“Could it have been transferred elsewhere?”

“Yes.”

Seraphina looked at Roland.

“You are very good at listing possibilities.”

“I have found it useful in my profession.”

She sighed.

“Then we need the original transfer records.”

Roland nodded.

“I have already requested them.”

Seraphina studied the inspection report again.

The building had existed.

It had been used.

It had not been properly maintained.

And grain had remained inside it for years, without anyone being entirely certain how much was there or whether it was safe.

She felt an uncomfortable heaviness settle in her stomach.

“How much does it cost to repair?”

Roland consulted the estimate.

“The maintenance officers have provided a preliminary figure. The roof and drainage repairs are manageable. The entrance will need replacement, and the interior flooring requires inspection.”

“Manageable means?”

“Not inexpensive, but within the regional maintenance allocation if prioritized.”

Seraphina nodded.

“Would repairing it make sense?”

“That depends on whether the structure is worth preserving and whether it can serve a useful purpose.”

“Can it?”

Roland glanced at Cedric.

Cedric answered.

“The location is useful. It lies near the old northern route, which still connects several agricultural villages. If repaired, it could serve as a reserve storage point.”

“Would the road need repairs?”

“Some sections would.”

Seraphina looked at the map.

The storehouse had been built for a reason.

The road had once been important.

The villages had grown around the agricultural route.

Over time, the road had fallen out of regular use, the storehouse had been neglected, and its purpose had faded from the administrative records.

But the surrounding villages still existed.

They still grew grain.

They still needed storage.

Perhaps the building was not useless.

Perhaps it had simply been forgotten.

“Could we get a proper estimate for restoring it?”

Cedric nodded.

“Yes.”

“And could the agricultural office determine whether a reserve storehouse would be useful for the surrounding villages?”

“Certainly.”

Seraphina closed the report.

“Then let's do that before deciding whether to spend the money.”

Roland looked relieved.

He had expected her to make a decision immediately.

She had learned enough to know that she should not.

That evening, Seraphina discussed the matter with her father.

Alaric listened while she explained the storehouse's condition and the preliminary findings.

When she finished, he asked, “What do you recommend?”

Seraphina looked at him.

“I do not know yet.”

He raised an eyebrow.

“That is an unusual answer.”

“I do not know whether the building is worth restoring. I do not know whether the grain is safe. I do not know whether the surrounding villages need another storage facility.”

Alaric nodded.

“Then you are asking for further assessment.”

“Yes.”

“Good.”

Seraphina looked at the papers.

“The storehouse was built as a reserve during poor harvests. It may still be useful, but I do not want to spend money simply because it appears in an old register.”

“That is sensible.”

She hesitated.

“Father?”

“Yes?”

“Why did nobody notice that it had been neglected?”

Alaric looked toward the window.

“Because the estate is large, Seraphina. Responsibility changes hands. Administrators retire. Records are copied. Priorities shift. Sometimes the next person assumes the previous person has already dealt with something.”

“That does not make it acceptable.”

“No.”

He looked at her.

“But it does mean that the person who discovers the problem must understand how it happened before deciding what to do.”

Seraphina nodded.

She thought about the old transfer record.

The eighty measures.

The grain that might have been replaced, moved, consumed, or misrecorded.

“Do you think there is something suspicious?”

“I think there is an unexplained difference.”

“That is not the same thing.”

“Exactly.”

Alaric smiled faintly.

“You are learning.”

Seraphina looked down.

“I am learning that everything takes longer than expected.”

“That is also true.”

He returned to his papers.

After a moment, he spoke again.

“You should not feel responsible for every neglected building in the duchy.”

Seraphina was quiet.

“I know.”

“Do you?”

She considered the question.

“I am trying to.”

Alaric nodded.

“Then that is enough for now.”

The next morning, Seraphina received a letter from Alden Mercer.

It contained an additional detail about the old storehouse.

Several older villagers remembered that, during the severe winter twenty-six years earlier, grain had been moved from N-14 to other villages.

The transfers had been made under an emergency distribution order.

Alden had heard the story from his grandfather.

He could not confirm the quantities or destinations.

But he remembered one thing clearly.

The grain had not been distributed freely.

The villages had been told that the reserve would be replenished after the next harvest.

Seraphina read the letter slowly.

Then she looked at Roland's report.

The discrepancy of eighty measures had appeared in a transfer document dated around the same period.

Perhaps the grain had been distributed.

Perhaps the records had not been updated.

Perhaps the reserve had been replenished later.

She wrote a note asking Roland to compare the old emergency distribution orders with the storehouse records.

Then she stopped.

She was beginning to understand that the building was not merely a neglected structure.

It was a record of how the duchy had handled a difficult winter decades earlier.

Whatever had happened then had left a trace.

A trace that nobody had bothered to follow.

Seraphina looked at the old map.

She had wanted to improve the storage reports.

Instead, she had discovered a forgotten storehouse, an incomplete inventory, and a historical discrepancy.

She put down her pen.

“I only wanted better storage.”

Mira, who was arranging the curtains, glanced at her.

“My lady?”

“Nothing.”

Seraphina closed the folder.

“Please bring me the report on the household supply trial.”

Mira nodded.

“And the stable department's request?”

Seraphina froze.

“What request?”

“The stable department has submitted a proposal to use the new delivery records.”

Seraphina stared at her.

“How many departments are involved now?”

“Three, my lady.”

She looked toward the ceiling.

“Please tell them to wait until the first trial has been reviewed.”

“Certainly.”

“And Mira?”

“Yes?”

“If anyone asks whether I am planning to reorganize the entire estate, the answer is no.”

Mira smiled.

“I will inform them.”

Seraphina picked up her tea.

For a few precious minutes, there was silence.

Then a knock sounded at the door.

Mira opened it.

A footman stood outside with a sealed message.

“For Lady Seraphina. From the northern agricultural office.”

Seraphina looked at it.

She did not open it immediately.

She took one sip of tea.

Then another.

Finally, she broke the seal.

The message was brief.

The regional office had located an additional document relating to the old emergency distribution programme.

It appeared to be a copy of the original order.

However, the copy listed a second storage location that had not appeared in the current reserve register.

**Northern Storehouse N-09.**

Seraphina stared at the page.

Then she slowly placed it on the table.

There was a long silence.

Mira looked at the document.

“Another storehouse, my lady?”

Seraphina closed her eyes.

“Yes.”

“Would you like me to ask Roland to come?”

Seraphina opened her eyes.

“No.”

Mira waited.

Seraphina took another sip of tea.

“Let him finish the first one.”

For the first time in several days, she was determined to do absolutely nothing about an administrative mystery.

At least until lunch.

**End of Chapter 9**
