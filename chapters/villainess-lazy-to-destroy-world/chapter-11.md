# **Chapter 11 — The Merchant Who Refused to Lower His Price**

Seraphina Veyr had discovered that there were several ways to ruin a perfectly peaceful morning.

One was to receive a letter from the Royal Academy.

Another was to be informed that a building containing several decades of grain had been left unattended.

A third, she was beginning to suspect, was to attend a meeting about agriculture.

The fourth was to discover that the meeting had been moved forward by an hour.

She was currently experiencing the fourth.

“Why?” she asked.

Mira Bell, who was arranging the papers on her dressing table, glanced at the schedule.

“Lord Cedric requested that the meeting begin earlier, my lady.”

“Why?”

“The merchants from the northern trade road are expected to arrive before noon.”

“Why?”

Mira paused.

“Would you like me to ask Lord Cedric?”

Seraphina considered this.

“No. He will probably explain.”

“Yes, my lady.”

“And then I will know.”

“Yes, my lady.”

“That is the problem.”

Mira lowered her eyes, but Seraphina caught the small movement at the corner of her mouth.

The maid had become increasingly comfortable with allowing herself to find her mistress amusing.

Seraphina considered that progress.

She was also beginning to understand that a peaceful life required a surprising amount of cooperation from other people.

Unfortunately, other people had schedules.

And merchants.

And letters.

And opinions about prices.

Especially opinions about prices.

The northern agricultural review was being held in the smaller conference room of the Veyr estate.

It was not the grand council chamber used for military briefings or formal political discussions. It was a practical room with a long oak table, several maps, two cabinets filled with old registers, and windows overlooking the eastern courtyard.

Seraphina arrived with Mira and found Cedric already seated.

Her cousin looked as though he had been awake for several hours.

There were three documents in front of him, two open ledgers to his left, and a cup of tea that had probably gone cold.

“Good morning,” she said.

“Good morning.”

“Has the meeting started?”

“No.”

“Then why do you look exhausted?”

“Because I prepared for it.”

“That sounds preventable.”

Cedric looked at her.

Seraphina took her seat.

At the head of the table sat Duke Alaric Veyr. Roland Ashford occupied the chair beside him, with a stack of records arranged neatly before him.

Harold Fenwick, the regional agricultural administrator, had arrived from the northern office. He was a broad-shouldered man in his late forties with a weathered face and the cautious expression of someone who had learned that every answer could create three additional questions.

Two other officials were present: a surveyor responsible for agricultural properties and a clerk who had brought the latest grain distribution reports.

Seraphina sat beside Cedric.

She had been invited as an observer.

She intended to behave like one.

“Before we begin,” Alaric said, “I want to clarify the purpose of this review.”

Everyone turned towards him.

“We are examining the condition of the northern reserve facilities, the reliability of our grain records, and the outstanding requests from the villages. We are not conducting a criminal inquiry. We are not assigning blame for records we have not yet verified.”

Roland nodded.

Harold Fenwick looked relieved.

Seraphina approved.

It was always encouraging when a meeting began with an explicit reminder not to accuse people of things.

“First,” Alaric continued, “Westmere.”

Harold opened a folder.

“The inspection team visited the village three days ago. The irrigation channel has suffered damage along the eastern section. Water flow is reduced, but the problem is repairable. The storage building has a roof leak affecting the northern interior wall.”

“Estimated cost?” Cedric asked.

“Thirty-eight silver crowns for the irrigation repairs. Twenty-one for the roof, assuming the timber beneath the damaged section remains sound.”

“Assuming?” Seraphina asked.

Harold turned towards her.

“The inspection team could not examine the entire roof without removing part of the damaged covering. The estimate includes a contingency if the supporting timber needs replacement.”

“Is that included in the thirty-eight?”

“No, my lady. The figures are separate.”

“Good.”

Cedric glanced at her.

She looked back.

“What?”

“Nothing.”

It was the sort of nothing that suggested he had a great deal to say.

Alaric examined the report.

“Approve the irrigation work. The storage roof should be opened for inspection before the final amount is authorized. I do not want us paying for repairs twice because someone guessed at the condition of the timber.”

Harold made a note.

“The bridge repair has been completed,” he added. “The village elder confirmed that carts can cross safely.”

“Has the repair been inspected independently?” Roland asked.

“Yes. The surveyor signed the completion report yesterday.”

“Then record it as completed,” Alaric said.

The clerk wrote something down.

The meeting proceeded through three additional village requests.

A boundary dispute required a land survey.

A request for well cleaning had been approved by the local office but not scheduled.

A road repair had been delayed because the contractor's equipment was being used elsewhere.

Seraphina listened.

The problems were not particularly dramatic.

That was precisely what made them important.

A broken road did not usually become a royal scandal. A damaged roof did not attract the attention of the court. A delayed irrigation repair did not inspire a ballad.

But farmers still had to move their crops.

Villagers still needed clean water.

And a storage building still leaked when it rained.

“Now,” Alaric said, “the reserve facilities.”

Harold turned to the next document.

“N-14 remains under assessment. The building is not suitable for immediate use. The old grain inside is being examined by the agricultural inspection team. We have not authorized its distribution.”

“Good,” Roland said.

“The historical transfer records remain incomplete. We found a reference to N-09 in an older register, but its location has not yet been confirmed.”

Seraphina leaned back.

“Has anyone established whether N-09 is a building or merely an administrative designation?”

Harold looked down at the document.

“Not yet.”

“Then perhaps we should establish that before searching the countryside for a building that may not exist.”

Cedric covered his mouth with one hand.

Alaric looked at the surveyor.

“Add that to the investigation. Confirm the designation through the property registers first.”

The surveyor nodded.

Roland turned a page.

“There is another matter. The grain procurement records.”

Seraphina straightened slightly.

She had heard the term before, but only in passing.

“Procurement?” Alaric asked.

“Purchases made by the regional offices to supplement local harvest reserves. Some grain is purchased directly from farmers. Other quantities are acquired through merchants and delivered to designated storage facilities.”

“And the records?” Cedric asked.

“They are not consistent.”

Roland placed two sheets on the table.

“The purchase records use standard measures. The delivery records use a mixture of standard measures and cart estimates. The warehouse registers record received quantities, but not always the original purchase reference.”

Seraphina studied the pages.

“So we know what was purchased, approximately what was delivered, and what was recorded as received.”

Roland looked at her.

“That is an accurate summary.”

“Then we do not know whether the difference occurred during transport, unloading, weighing, or recording.”

“Correct.”

“Excellent.”

Harold frowned.

“Excellent?”

“No,” Seraphina said. “The situation is terrible. But at least we have identified the number of possible places where it became terrible.”

Cedric sighed.

Alaric continued reading.

“Who handles procurement?”

“The regional office negotiates with merchants,” Harold explained. “For larger quantities, it consults the ducal supply department. The final agreement is approved by the appropriate administrative officer.”

“And the merchants?”

“They supply grain, arrange transport, and receive payment after delivery is verified.”

“Usually?” Seraphina asked.

Harold hesitated.

“Usually.”

She looked at him.

He looked back.

The room became slightly quieter.

“Explain,” Alaric said.

Harold selected another document.

“There have been disagreements over delivery quantities. In some cases, the merchant claims the full quantity was delivered. The warehouse records show a lower amount.”

“Were the merchants paid?”

“In most cases, yes.”

“Based on what?”

“Delivery certificates issued by the receiving office.”

“And the receiving office?”

“Confirms the quantities according to its own records.”

Seraphina considered that.

“So the merchant submits a delivery statement, the warehouse records what it receives, and the payment office uses a certificate prepared from those records.”

“Yes.”

“Who verifies the difference?”

Harold did not answer immediately.

Roland did.

“Sometimes the regional office. Sometimes the warehouse supervisor. Sometimes the discrepancy is settled through correspondence.”

“Sometimes?”

“There is no single procedure.”

Seraphina looked at the documents again.

She was beginning to dislike the word sometimes.

It seemed to be responsible for an unreasonable number of administrative problems.

Alaric folded his hands.

“Arrange a separate review of procurement and delivery records. I want the original contracts, delivery certificates, warehouse registers, and payment records compared.”

Roland made a note.

“Understood.”

“Do not delay the regular purchases,” Alaric added. “We still need grain.”

Harold nodded.

“Of course.”

The door opened.

A servant entered and approached Cedric.

“My lord, the merchant delegation has arrived.”

Cedric looked at the clock.

“Already?”

“They were earlier than expected.”

Seraphina closed her eyes briefly.

Of course they were.

The merchants were not a delegation in the grand political sense.

There were three of them.

The first was a woman in her early fifties named Mistress Elinor Brant, who represented a regional grain trading house. She had sharp eyes, a neat travelling coat, and the composed manner of someone accustomed to negotiating with people who believed their titles made them better at arithmetic.

The second was a younger man named Tobias Reed, her chief commercial clerk.

The third was a representative from a smaller transport company.

They entered, bowed to the duke, and took the seats prepared for them.

Mistress Brant's gaze briefly passed over Seraphina.

“Lady Veyr,” she said.

Seraphina inclined her head.

“Mistress Brant.”

“I did not know you would be attending.”

“I was invited to observe.”

“An important distinction.”

“Very.”

Mistress Brant smiled faintly.

Seraphina immediately decided she liked her.

Not because she was particularly pleasant.

Because she had understood the value of the word observe.

Alaric introduced the purpose of the meeting.

“The Veyr administration intends to review its grain procurement arrangements before the next major purchasing period. We have received several reports of differences between purchased and received quantities. We would like to understand the current difficulties from the merchants' perspective.”

Mistress Brant folded her hands.

“Then I am pleased to say that the difficulties are not difficult to understand.”

Harold Fenwick shifted slightly.

Seraphina looked at the merchant.

“Please explain.”

“The ducal office requests grain at a specified price. We agree to supply it. We arrange transport, loading, and delivery. Then, after the grain reaches the warehouse, we are informed that the quantity received is lower than the quantity dispatched.”

“Does that happen frequently?” Roland asked.

“It happens often enough to be expensive.”

“Do you have records?”

“Of course.”

Tobias opened a leather folder.

He placed several documents on the table.

“These are copies of our dispatch records, transport schedules, and delivery statements from the previous two purchasing periods.”

Roland began examining them.

Seraphina noticed that the documents were arranged chronologically.

There were matching reference numbers on the dispatch sheets and delivery statements.

That was already better than several records she had seen from the regional office.

“Your company claims that all contracted quantities were dispatched?” Cedric asked.

Mistress Brant looked at him.

“Yes.”

“And delivered?”

“We delivered them to the designated receiving points.”

Harold cleared his throat.

“That is not what the warehouse records show.”

“No,” Mistress Brant said. “It is not.”

“Then how do you explain the difference?”

“I cannot explain what happens after the carts leave our supervision.”

The answer was immediate.

Harold's expression hardened.

“You are suggesting the warehouse staff are responsible?”

“I am suggesting that the grain we dispatch is weighed, recorded, and sealed. If the receiving office records less than we sent, someone must establish where the difference occurred.”

“Your transport arrangements are not beyond question.”

“Neither are your receiving procedures.”

The room became still.

Seraphina watched Alaric.

Her father did not interrupt.

That was probably wise.

She looked back at the documents.

“May I ask something?”

Mistress Brant turned towards her.

“Certainly.”

“Do your contracts specify where responsibility changes hands?”

The merchant considered the question.

“Explain what you mean.”

“Does your responsibility end when the grain leaves your warehouse, when it reaches the ducal warehouse, or when the receiving clerk signs the delivery certificate?”

Mistress Brant looked at Tobias.

He opened another folder.

“Our contracts state that responsibility transfers upon acceptance at the designated receiving point.”

“And what counts as acceptance?”

“The receiving clerk's signature.”

“Does the clerk sign before or after weighing?”

“After weighing, according to the procedure.”

“According to the procedure,” Seraphina repeated.

Mistress Brant's eyes narrowed slightly.

“You have noticed the distinction.”

“I have noticed that everyone seems to have a procedure. I am trying to discover whether they are the same procedure.”

For the first time, Mistress Brant smiled properly.

“They are not.”

Harold looked irritated.

“Every warehouse follows the same ducal instructions.”

“Then perhaps the instructions are not being followed consistently,” Mistress Brant replied.

Roland examined one of the dispatch records.

“This delivery was recorded as 120 standard measures.”

“Yes,” Tobias said.

“The receiving certificate states 113.”

“Correct.”

“Was the grain weighed at the receiving point?”

“We were told it was.”

“Were your representatives present?”

“Not at every delivery.”

“Why not?”

“Because the ducal office does not pay the additional cost of keeping a representative at every warehouse.”

Harold leaned forward.

“You are not required to do so.”

“Then you cannot expect us to accept every discrepancy without question.”

Seraphina studied the contract.

The price was listed clearly.

The quantity was listed clearly.

The delivery location was listed clearly.

The method of payment was listed clearly.

The procedure for disputed quantities was not.

Instead, it contained a sentence referring to the receiving office's final determination.

She read it twice.

Then a third time.

“Who wrote this contract?”

Harold looked at the document.

“It is the standard regional procurement agreement.”

“That was not my question.”

“The agreement was prepared by the regional administration several years ago.”

“By whom?”

“I would have to check.”

Seraphina nodded.

“Please do.”

Cedric leaned closer.

“What have you found?”

“An arrangement that gives one side the right to determine whether the other side fulfilled its obligation.”

“That is how receiving procedures work,” Harold said.

“Perhaps. But what happens when the receiving record is disputed?”

Harold frowned.

“The merchant can submit a complaint.”

“And who reviews it?”

“The regional office.”

“Using which records?”

“The delivery records.”

“Which delivery records?”

Harold stopped.

Seraphina looked down at the contract again.

“Is there a procedure for an independent reweighing?”

“No,” Roland answered after examining the relevant section.

“Is there a requirement for the merchant to be present?”

“No.”

“Is there a requirement for the receiving clerk to retain a copy of the weighing record?”

Roland turned several pages.

“Not in this agreement.”

Seraphina placed the contract on the table.

“Then we are asking merchants to accept a process in which they cannot independently verify the final quantity.”

Mistress Brant said nothing.

But her expression changed.

She had expected a dispute.

She had not expected the duke's daughter to identify the weakness in the contract.

Alaric looked at Roland.

“Is that correct?”

Roland examined the agreement more carefully.

“Yes, Your Grace. The contract requires the receiving office to record the quantity but does not specify how the measurement is witnessed or preserved.”

“Then why was it written that way?”

Roland looked uncomfortable.

“Because the agreement was designed to simplify receiving procedures.”

Seraphina looked at him.

“Did it?”

Roland sighed.

“Apparently not.”

The discussion continued for nearly an hour.

Mistress Brant produced additional records.

The smaller transport representative explained that delays at the receiving warehouses sometimes forced carts to remain overnight. Drivers charged additional fees, and merchants absorbed some of the costs.

Harold defended the regional office's existing procedures.

Roland asked for original records.

Cedric took notes.

Seraphina listened.

She learned that grain procurement was more complicated than buying a quantity of wheat and paying for it.

Grain had to be purchased at the right time, transported before roads became difficult, protected from moisture, weighed consistently, and stored in facilities that were actually usable.

A merchant's price reflected more than the grain itself.

It included labour, transport, storage, risk, and the possibility that payment might be delayed.

She had known these things in a general sense.

She had not understood how quickly they accumulated.

Finally, Alaric asked the question that had been waiting beneath the entire discussion.

“What price are you proposing for the next purchasing period?”

Mistress Brant opened a prepared sheet.

“Six silver crowns and forty copper pieces per standard measure, excluding extraordinary transport costs.”

Harold immediately objected.

“That is higher than the current regional rate.”

“Yes.”

“The current rate is six silver crowns.”

“Yes.”

“You are asking for an increase.”

“I am stating the price at which we can supply the requested quantity.”

Alaric looked at the sheet.

“What justifies the increase?”

Mistress Brant did not appear offended.

“Transport costs have increased. The eastern road has deteriorated. Two of our usual storage facilities are undergoing repairs. Labour costs have also risen since the previous harvest.”

“Your company has made a profit in recent years,” Cedric said.

“Fortunately,” she replied. “That is why we remain in business.”

Seraphina looked down to hide a smile.

Cedric did not smile.

“Your proposal is still considerably higher.”

“Then I would advise the ducal office to purchase elsewhere.”

Harold stared at her.

“You cannot expect the duke to accept an ultimatum.”

“It is not an ultimatum. It is a price.”

There was a pause.

Seraphina looked at the merchant.

The woman was not trying to provoke them.

She was simply refusing to pretend that a lower price would not cost her money.

Seraphina found the position strangely refreshing.

In her previous life, she had attended meetings where people spent forty minutes describing why a task was impossible before suggesting that it could be completed by Friday.

At least this merchant was honest.

“May I ask another question?” Seraphina said.

Mistress Brant turned.

“Of course.”

“If the ducal office agreed to the current price of six silver crowns, what quantity could you supply?”

Mistress Brant considered.

“Not the full requested amount.”

“How much?”

“Approximately two-thirds, depending on the delivery schedule.”

“And at six crowns and forty copper pieces?”

“The full amount, provided the receiving arrangements are clarified and the payment schedule remains within the agreed period.”

Seraphina looked at the price sheet.

“What if the delivery schedule were divided into smaller quantities?”

“Transport costs would increase.”

“What if the receiving points were consolidated?”

“Some routes would become longer.”

“What if the ducal office arranged transport for the final stage?”

Mistress Brant glanced at Tobias.

“That would depend on the locations.”

Seraphina nodded.

“So the price is not simply the price of grain.”

“No,” Mistress Brant said. “It is the price of supplying grain under the conditions you require.”

Harold looked at the duke.

“We cannot allow merchants to dictate administrative procedures.”

Seraphina turned towards him.

“Why not?”

Harold blinked.

“Because the ducal office must maintain control over its procurement system.”

“Control over what?”

“The purchasing process.”

“Of course. But if the process makes merchants charge more, or refuse to supply us, what are we controlling?”

Cedric quietly closed his notebook.

Alaric remained silent.

Seraphina looked back at the merchant.

“Would your company consider a contract with a revised receiving procedure, provided the price is negotiated separately?”

Mistress Brant's expression became thoughtful.

“Yes.”

“Would you accept joint weighing at the receiving point?”

“If it is done consistently.”

“Would you provide dispatch records in the same standard measurement used by the warehouse?”

“We already do.”

“Would you allow the receiving office to record any difference before the delivery certificate is signed?”

“Yes.”

“And would you accept payment for the verified quantity, with disputed amounts held until a review is completed?”

Mistress Brant looked at Tobias.

He made a small note.

“That would be acceptable in principle.”

Harold shifted in his chair.

“Lady Seraphina, the administration cannot redesign its entire procurement system because one merchant objects to it.”

Seraphina turned towards him.

“I am not asking you to redesign it because one merchant objects.”

“Then what are you asking?”

“To establish whether the existing system works.”

Harold's mouth tightened.

She continued.

“If it does, we should be able to demonstrate that. If it does not, we should correct it before purchasing more grain under the same conditions.”

Alaric finally spoke.

“That is reasonable.”

Harold fell silent.

The duke looked at Roland.

“Prepare a revised receiving procedure for review. It should include joint weighing where practical, recorded measurements, duplicate delivery receipts, and a clear process for disputed quantities.”

“Yes, Your Grace.”

“Do not assume the merchants are correct merely because they have documents. Their records must be examined as well.”

“Understood.”

“Do not assume the regional office is correct merely because it has official authority.”

Roland nodded.

Seraphina thought that was probably the most useful sentence spoken in the room.

The meeting ended without an agreement on price.

Mistress Brant gathered her documents.

The transport representative looked relieved that nobody had demanded an immediate discount.

Harold appeared to be mentally preparing a lengthy report explaining why the regional office had not been wrong.

Tobias remained behind for a moment to discuss the delivery references with Roland.

Seraphina stood near the window.

She had expected the merchant to be difficult.

Instead, she had discovered that the merchant had a perfectly understandable reason for refusing to lower her price.

The difficulty was that the ducal office wanted cheaper grain, while the merchant wanted to avoid losing money.

Neither objective was particularly unreasonable.

“Lady Veyr.”

Seraphina turned.

Mistress Brant had approached her.

“Yes?”

“You asked the right questions.”

“I asked several questions.”

“Most people ask why the price is high.”

“And you would have explained it.”

“I would have.”

“Then we would still have had the same price.”

Mistress Brant gave a short laugh.

“Exactly.”

Seraphina looked towards the table, where Roland was now comparing two documents.

“Does your company supply other regions?”

“Yes. Merrow, primarily. We have smaller contracts in the western territories.”

“Are the receiving procedures better there?”

“In some places.”

“Worse?”

“In others.”

“That is not particularly reassuring.”

“Trade is rarely reassuring, my lady.”

Seraphina considered this.

“Do you have a copy of your previous contracts?”

Mistress Brant raised an eyebrow.

“Why?”

“To compare their terms.”

“You wish to examine contracts from other regions?”

“Only the relevant receiving and payment clauses.”

The merchant studied her.

“You are not what I expected.”

Seraphina had begun to suspect that this was becoming a common problem.

“I am beginning to hear that often.”

“May I ask what you expected of yourself?”

“A quiet morning.”

Mistress Brant laughed again.

This time, Seraphina did too.

By the time the meeting ended, the sun had passed its highest point.

Seraphina returned to the estate dining room for a late lunch.

Cedric joined her.

He looked tired.

She looked hungry.

They had different priorities.

“Do you think Mistress Brant was being truthful?” he asked.

“I think she was telling us what she wanted us to know.”

“That is not the same thing.”

“No. But it is a beginning.”

Cedric placed his napkin beside his plate.

“The regional office believes the merchants are overcharging. The merchants believe the office is unreliable. Neither side trusts the other.”

“Then we should not make the contract depend on trust.”

Cedric looked at her.

“What would you make it depend on?”

“Records both sides can verify.”

“That sounds simple.”

“It should be.”

“Nothing about procurement is simple.”

“I have noticed.”

A servant brought their meal.

Cedric waited until the servant had left before continuing.

“You understand that if we change the receiving procedure, the regional offices will complain.”

“Probably.”

“The merchants may still refuse the price.”

“Probably.”

“The duke may have to pay more.”

“Possibly.”

“And the next purchasing period is approaching.”

Seraphina picked up her fork.

“Then we should decide whether paying less for grain that we cannot reliably account for is actually cheaper.”

Cedric was quiet.

She ate a piece of roasted vegetable.

It was very good.

“Do you think the merchants are taking advantage of us?” he asked.

“I think some might.”

“And others?”

“May simply be charging what their business requires.”

“And how do we distinguish between them?”

Seraphina considered.

“By examining their costs, their records, their delivery performance, and the terms of their contracts.”

Cedric looked down at his plate.

“You make it sound like there is no easy answer.”

“There isn't.”

“That is unfortunate.”

“Very.”

She took another bite.

Cedric watched her for a moment.

“You were different at the beginning of the year.”

Seraphina nearly choked.

She reached for her water.

Cedric waited.

“What does that mean?” she asked.

“You would have demanded a lower price.”

“Would that have worked?”

“Perhaps.”

“Would it have solved the problem?”

“No.”

“Then I have improved.”

Cedric gave her a tired smile.

“You have certainly become more difficult to predict.”

Seraphina decided that was not entirely a compliment.

That afternoon, Roland delivered a short preliminary report.

It contained three findings.

First, the grain procurement contracts did not define a sufficiently clear procedure for resolving differences between dispatch and receiving quantities.

Second, the regional offices had no consistent method for retaining independent weighing records.

Third, the payment schedules varied between merchants, even when the contracts used identical wording.

Seraphina read the report twice.

Then she set it down.

“Is there anything else?” she asked.

Roland hesitated.

“There is one matter I have not included in the preliminary report.”

“Why?”

“Because I wanted to verify it first.”

She looked at him.

He placed another document on the desk.

It was a copy of a payment register.

“Three deliveries from different merchants were recorded as complete on the same date. All three were received at separate warehouses.”

“Were they?”

“The delivery certificates say so.”

“And the payment register?”

“Shows payment was authorized two days before the final receiving certificates were filed.”

Seraphina looked at the dates.

“Could that be a clerical error?”

“Yes.”

“Could it be a standard advance-payment arrangement?”

“Not according to the contracts.”

“Could the certificates have been submitted late?”

“Possibly.”

She looked up.

“Then we have three possibilities.”

“More than three, my lady.”

“Of course we do.”

Roland placed his finger on the second entry.

“The payments were authorized by the same office. The receiving certificates were signed by different warehouse clerks.”

“Were the quantities identical?”

“No.”

“Were the merchants connected?”

“Not that we have established.”

Seraphina leaned back.

“Then do not connect them yet.”

Roland nodded.

“I agree.”

She returned to the register.

There was something else.

A reference number appeared beside each payment.

The first was marked N-14.

The second was marked N-09.

The third was marked with the name of a village she did not recognize.

Seraphina stared at the page.

“Why are these references here?”

Roland examined them.

“They appear to identify the intended storage locations.”

“N-14 and N-09 are both listed as old reserve facilities.”

“Yes.”

“And the third?”

“I do not know.”

“Have you checked?”

“Not yet.”

She placed the paper down.

For a moment, neither spoke.

The room seemed quieter than it had been before.

Seraphina remembered the old storehouse at Westmere: its damaged roof, the deteriorated grain, the decades-old labels, and the missing eighty standard measures.

She remembered the promise that the reserve would be replenished after the next harvest.

And she remembered that no one had yet established what happened to N-09.

“Could these payments be connected to the old reserve programme?” she asked.

Roland did not answer immediately.

“They might be.”

“Or they might simply use the same location references.”

“That is possible.”

“Then we need the original contracts.”

“Yes.”

“And the dispatch records.”

“Yes.”

“And the warehouse registers.”

“Yes.”

Seraphina looked at him.

“Please tell me you already requested them.”

“I did.”

She let out a breath.

“Thank you.”

Roland gathered the documents.

“I should also mention that the records are incomplete.”

“Of course they are.”

“The regional office has requested additional time.”

“Give them time.”

Roland nodded.

Seraphina stood and walked towards the window.

Outside, the courtyard was quiet. Two gardeners were carrying a wooden crate towards the greenhouse. A servant crossed the path with a basket of folded linen.

Ordinary work.

Ordinary people.

A kingdom that was not nearly as organized as its official documents claimed.

She had wanted to avoid becoming involved in anything important.

Unfortunately, she was beginning to understand that importance was often disguised as something very small.

A roof that leaked.

A cart delayed by a broken road.

A measurement recorded differently in two ledgers.

A contract that allowed one person to decide what another person had delivered.

And, apparently, a grain storehouse whose name kept appearing in places where it did not belong.

“Roland?”

“Yes, my lady?”

“Do you think the merchants know about these old reserve references?”

“I cannot say.”

“Would they?”

“Some may. Others may not.”

“Then we should ask without suggesting an answer.”

Roland gave a small nod.

“I will arrange it.”

Seraphina returned to her desk.

There was a letter from the Royal Academy waiting beside her inkstand.

She had not opened it.

She looked at the envelope.

Then at the grain register.

Then at the envelope again.

The Academy could wait until tomorrow.

The grain register, unfortunately, could not be persuaded to disappear.

She opened her notebook and added a new rule.

**14. Never assume a low price is economical.**

She considered it.

Then added another.

**15. A contract should explain what happens when people disagree.**

She put down her pen.

For a few seconds, she admired the neat handwriting.

Then Mira entered.

“My lady, the Royal Academy has sent a second letter.”

Seraphina stared at her.

“Already?”

“Yes, my lady.”

“Did they say why?”

“They are requesting confirmation of your attendance at the preliminary discussion.”

Seraphina looked towards the ceiling.

The kingdom had apparently decided that she was not allowed to have a quiet week.

“Put it on tomorrow's schedule.”

Mira hesitated.

“Tomorrow, my lady?”

“Yes.”

“And the agricultural review documents?”

“Also tomorrow.”

“The revised procurement procedure?”

“Tomorrow.”

“The letter from Lady Amelia?”

“Tomorrow.”

Mira looked at the desk.

“And your notebook?”

Seraphina closed it.

“Today.”

Mira nodded solemnly.

“Very well, my lady.”

She began collecting the papers.

Seraphina watched her.

Then she noticed one document had been left beneath the grain register.

It was an old copy of a storage allocation list.

The third location reference was written in a faded hand.

**Southmere Reserve Depot — Formerly Registered as N-03.**

Seraphina frowned.

“Where is Southmere?”

Mira looked over.

“In the southern district, my lady.”

“Is it still operating?”

“I do not know.”

Seraphina looked at the document for a long moment.

Then she pushed it into the folder.

“Tomorrow,” she said.

For once, Mira did not smile.

“Yes, my lady.”

Seraphina leaned back in her chair.

She had begun the day intending to observe a meeting.

She had ended it with a merchant negotiation, a procurement review, and three old reserve facilities appearing in payment records.

She closed her eyes.

Perhaps the original villainess had been right about one thing.

The kingdom was full of enemies.

They were simply not always people.

Sometimes they were contracts.

Sometimes they were poorly maintained buildings.

And sometimes they were the word *tomorrow*.

**End of Chapter 11**
