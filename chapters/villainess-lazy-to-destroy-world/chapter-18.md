# Chapter 18 — The Office That Inherited the Problem

The morning after the spring reception, Seraphina discovered that celebrations had consequences.

The first consequence was a headache.

The second was a breakfast table covered in correspondence.

The third was her father's decision to discuss an old grain investigation before she had finished her tea.

She stared at the letter beside his plate.

“Must we?”

Duke Alaric looked up from his correspondence.

“Yes.”

“Could we not enjoy one morning of peace?”

“We have enjoyed several.”

“That is an exaggerated description of events.”

Across the table, Duchess Evelyne smiled.

“Your father received a response from the royal archive last night. I believe you were expecting it.”

Seraphina looked at the letter.

“I was expecting it tomorrow.”

“Apparently the archive was more efficient than expected,” Alaric said.

“An alarming development.”

He pushed a second document toward her.

“The authority register confirms that Sir Geoffrey Marlowe presided over the emergency committee during Lord Chancellor Harrow's absence. It also records a transfer of responsibility for the Northern Consolidated Depot.”

Seraphina read the document again.

The handwritten amendment was short.

It identified a receiving authority and instructed that final reconciliation be completed by the office accepting responsibility.

The name was clear.

**Northern Provincial Supply Office.**

Seraphina looked up.

“Was that office responsible for all three reserve locations?”

“No. The original reserve locations belonged to different administrative divisions. But the emergency transfer placed their grain under the depot's temporary management.”

“And the Northern Provincial Supply Office took responsibility for the depot?”

“That is what the amendment says.”

“Then the final reconciliation should be in its records.”

Alaric nodded.

“Or in the records of the office that succeeded it.”

Seraphina looked at him.

“Did it close?”

“Not exactly. Its duties were reorganized over time. Some were transferred to the regional agricultural administration. Others were absorbed into the provincial treasury.”

She set down the paper.

“So we have to find out which office inherited which records.”

Cedric, seated farther down the table, nodded.

“That is what Roland is trying to establish.”

Seraphina sighed.

“Of course he is.”

Evelyne poured more tea.

“Would you like another piece of bread before you begin?”

Seraphina looked at her mother.

“Yes.”

“Then eat first.”

For once, the instruction was entirely welcome.

The financial office had acquired a new visitor by the time Seraphina arrived.

Roland Ashford stood behind his desk, speaking with a thin, elderly man carrying a leather document case.

The visitor wore a dark brown coat with an old-fashioned collar. His hair was white, and his hands were stained with ink.

Cedric stood beside the filing cabinet.

“Lady Seraphina,” Roland said. “You have arrived at a useful time.”

Seraphina looked at the visitor.

“Who is this?”

“Mr. Edmund Clarke. He served as a receiving clerk in the northern provincial administration before its reorganization.”

The elderly man bowed.

“My lady.”

Seraphina returned the greeting.

“Thank you for coming, Mr. Clarke.”

“I was informed that you were reviewing the old emergency grain records.”

“Yes.”

“I may be able to help with the transfer of responsibility.”

Seraphina gestured toward a chair.

“Please sit.”

Mr. Clarke carefully placed his document case on the table before lowering himself into the chair.

Roland arranged several papers.

“The royal archive has confirmed that the Northern Provincial Supply Office accepted responsibility for the temporary depot three days after the original transfer order.”

Mr. Clarke nodded.

“That would have been the normal procedure.”

“Do you remember the office?”

“Very well, my lord. I worked there for nineteen years.”

“Do you remember the winter emergency?”

Mr. Clarke considered the question.

“I remember the winter. I remember the roads closing. I remember that our office was receiving requests from villages faster than we could process them.”

“Do you remember the Northern Consolidated Depot?”

“Yes.”

Seraphina leaned forward.

“You do?”

“I did not work at the depot itself, my lady. But I remember the transfer of its records.”

“What records?”

Mr. Clarke opened his case.

“Inventory sheets, delivery certificates, temporary storage reports, and a bundle of correspondence from the emergency committee.”

He removed a small notebook.

“The depot's records were sent to our office after the emergency. They were supposed to be reconciled against the original reserve registers.”

Seraphina glanced at Roland.

“Were they?”

Mr. Clarke hesitated.

“I cannot say.”

“Why not?”

“Because the reconciliation was assigned to another clerk.”

“Do you remember the clerk's name?”

“I believe it was Thomas Weller.”

Roland wrote the name down.

“Was the reconciliation completed?”

Mr. Clarke shook his head.

“I do not remember seeing a final report.”

Seraphina looked at him.

“Could it have been completed without you seeing it?”

“Certainly. I was responsible for incoming correspondence, not the entire office.”

That answer was sensible.

It was also frustrating.

Seraphina leaned back.

“What happened to the records?”

Mr. Clarke looked at the notebook in his hands.

“That is the question I was hoping you could answer.”

She blinked.

“Me?”

“I heard that House Veyr was reviewing the old reserve programme. I thought you might have located the missing papers.”

Seraphina looked at Roland.

Roland looked at Cedric.

Cedric looked at the ceiling.

Nobody spoke.

Seraphina turned back to Mr. Clarke.

“We have not.”

“Then I am afraid I have little more to offer.”

“Not necessarily. You said the records were transferred to your office. Do you remember where they were stored?”

“In the old northern archive room.”

“Does that room still exist?”

“The room does. The office does not.”

Seraphina frowned.

“Where is the archive now?”

Mr. Clarke opened his notebook and turned several pages.

“Some documents went to the regional treasury. Others went to the agricultural administration. A smaller collection was sent to the provincial records office.”

“Which collection?”

“The old depot records were supposed to go to the agricultural administration.”

Roland immediately wrote that down.

“Do you know when?”

“Not precisely. Perhaps two years after the reorganization.”

Seraphina looked at Cedric.

“Can we request those records?”

“Yes. I will contact the regional administrator.”

Mr. Clarke cleared his throat.

“There is one more thing.”

Seraphina turned back.

“What is it?”

“The Northern Provincial Supply Office did not manage the depot for the entire period. We only accepted responsibility for the records and the final reconciliation. The physical depot was managed by a temporary emergency staff.”

“Who appointed them?”

“The committee, I believe.”

“Do you remember any names?”

Mr. Clarke thought for a moment.

“One was a warehouse supervisor named Peter Hales.”

Seraphina went still.

Peter Hales.

The same name associated with the disputed maintenance authorization.

She looked at Roland.

He had also recognized it.

“Are you certain?” Roland asked.

Mr. Clarke shook his head.

“No. I remember the name from the records, but I cannot guarantee that it was the same man. There may have been more than one Peter Hales.”

Seraphina nodded slowly.

“Then we should not connect the two matters yet.”

“Agreed,” Roland said.

Mr. Clarke looked relieved.

“I would not wish to accuse someone on the basis of a name.”

“Neither would we.”

Seraphina glanced at the notebook.

“Could you write down what you remember about the transfer of records? Even if the dates are approximate?”

“I can.”

“Please distinguish what you personally remember from what you learned from documents.”

Mr. Clarke gave her a thoughtful look.

“That is a very sensible request, my lady.”

Seraphina shrugged.

“I would rather have three uncertain facts clearly identified than one confident statement that turns out to be wrong.”

Roland's expression suggested approval.

Seraphina ignored it.

She was beginning to suspect that the accountant considered every sensible sentence a personal victory.

The request to the regional agricultural administration was sent that afternoon.

Cedric drafted it.

Roland reviewed it.

Seraphina read the final version.

It asked for:

- The surviving inventory records of the Northern Consolidated Depot.
- The transfer register from the former Northern Provincial Supply Office.
- Any final reconciliation report.
- The appointment records of temporary depot staff.
- Any correspondence concerning the return of the leased land.

Seraphina read the final paragraph.

“Why does it say we are conducting a preliminary historical review?”

“Because we are,” Cedric replied.

“Could it not say we are trying to find out what happened to the grain?”

“That would be less suitable for a formal request.”

She sighed.

“Everyone insists on making simple things complicated.”

Cedric took the letter.

“Would you like to send it?”

“Yes.”

He handed it to the clerk.

Seraphina watched the letter leave the room.

For a few seconds, she felt that she had accomplished something.

Then Roland spoke.

“There is another matter concerning the current grain deliveries.”

She closed her eyes.

“Of course.”

“The trial delivery with Mistress Brant was successful. However, the regional office has identified a difference between the quantities recorded at the dispatch warehouse and the quantities listed on the supplier's invoice.”

“Was it the same tare-weight problem?”

“No. This difference concerns the timing of the dispatch record.”

Seraphina opened her eyes.

“Explain.”

Roland laid out the documents.

“The supplier's invoice records forty measures dispatched on the eighteenth day of Springrise. The receiving office recorded the delivery on the nineteenth. The warehouse register lists the grain as dispatched on the nineteenth.”

“So the dates differ.”

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“The warehouse clerk entered the dispatch after the wagons had already departed.”

Seraphina stared at him.

“Why?”

“He says the loading was completed late in the evening. The clerk responsible for the register had already left, so the entry was made the following morning.”

“Was the grain actually delivered?”

“Yes. The receiving certificates confirm the quantity.”

“Then what is the problem?”

“The regional summary compares dispatch and receiving dates. It flags the entry as a possible delay or mismatch.”

Seraphina looked at the forms.

“So the summary makes it appear that the grain was dispatched and received on the same day, but the original records show otherwise.”

“Correct.”

“Could the summary include both the actual dispatch date and the date the register was updated?”

Roland considered it.

“That would be clearer.”

“Then do that.”

He nodded.

Seraphina looked at the two dates again.

It was a small problem.

But if the regional office had used the summary to calculate delivery times, it might have created a false impression of how quickly the supplier had transported the grain.

She tapped the paper.

“Do not correct the original register. Add a note explaining when the entry was made.”

“Agreed.”

“And make sure the receiving certificate remains attached.”

“Of course.”

She stood.

“Good. That is enough for today.”

Roland looked at the remaining documents.

“There are several other entries.”

Seraphina paused.

“How many?”

“Seven.”

She slowly turned toward him.

“Why would you tell me that?”

“Because you asked whether there were any further issues.”

“I was being polite.”

Roland looked genuinely confused.

Cedric laughed.

The following morning, Seraphina received a letter from Westmere.

It was from Alden Mercer, the village elder.

The letter concerned the irrigation repairs.

The inspection team had arrived, examined the damaged channel, and confirmed that the shifted stone lining and faulty gate were responsible for reduced water flow.

The repair estimate had been approved.

Work would begin within five days.

The letter also mentioned the old storage building.

The roof had been inspected, and the damage was more extensive than initially expected. Several support beams needed replacement.

Alden asked whether the village could temporarily store agricultural tools in the building while repairs were underway.

Seraphina read the letter twice.

She brought it to Cedric.

“Would that be safe?”

Cedric examined the inspection report.

“Not until the supports are replaced.”

“Then the answer is no.”

“We should explain why and provide an alternative if possible.”

Seraphina nodded.

“Is there another storage building?”

“There is a smaller municipal shed near the mill. It is used for road maintenance equipment.”

“Could it accommodate the tools?”

“Possibly, but the village would need to agree.”

“Then ask Alden to discuss it with the village council.”

Cedric made a note.

Seraphina looked at the report again.

“The repairs are beginning in five days. Does the contractor have the necessary materials?”

“Yes. The stone and timber have been ordered.”

“Has the delivery date been confirmed?”

Cedric checked.

“The timber delivery is scheduled for the fourth day.”

“Two days before work begins?”

“That is correct.”

Seraphina frowned.

“What happens if it is delayed?”

Cedric looked at the schedule.

“We would need to postpone the work.”

“Then perhaps the contractor should confirm the delivery before the start date.”

Cedric nodded.

“I will ask.”

Seraphina returned the report.

She was learning something important.

Approving a repair was not the same as completing it.

A document could say that work had been authorized, materials ordered, and a date assigned.

But the village still needed a functioning irrigation channel.

The people living there did not benefit from a beautiful schedule.

They benefited when the water flowed.

Three days later, the Academy submitted its formal request.

Seraphina received it in the eastern office.

The document was accompanied by a technical appendix, a proposed inspection route, and a list of personnel.

She was impressed.

The Academy had provided more detail than she expected.

Master Fenwick had clearly taken their conversation seriously.

She reviewed the plan.

The western boundary conduit ran beneath a section of the old road, crossed a narrow strip of ducal land, and continued toward a small maintenance station.

The inspection would take approximately half a day.

No mana discharge was planned.

No repairs would be attempted during the inspection.

The Academy would provide its own technical equipment.

House Veyr would provide access and a local administrator familiar with the land.

Seraphina turned to Cedric.

“This is acceptable.”

“Would you like to authorize it?”

“Yes, with one condition.”

“What condition?”

“The maintenance station must remain accessible to the local workers. The inspection cannot interfere with their normal duties.”

Cedric added the condition.

The request was approved and returned to the Academy.

Seraphina leaned back.

“That was not difficult.”

Cedric smiled.

“You seem surprised.”

“I am accustomed to people asking for permission before explaining what they intend to do.”

“That is not a particularly unusual problem.”

“It should be.”

He gathered the papers.

“Master Arkwright will be pleased.”

“I hope he is pleased from a safe distance.”

Cedric laughed.

The Academy's response arrived the next day.

It was brief.

Master Fenwick thanked House Veyr for its cooperation and confirmed that the inspection would take place in four days.

There was also a note from Lucien.

> Lady Seraphina,
>
> Thank you for reviewing the proposal.
>
> Your condition concerning access to the maintenance station is reasonable. I had not considered that the inspection might interfere with the workers' usual duties.
>
> I will ensure that the local administrator is consulted before the team arrives.
>
> Respectfully,
>
> Lucien Arkwright.

Seraphina read it.

Then she placed it on the desk.

Mira, who was arranging a stack of correspondence, glanced over.

“Is something wrong, my lady?”

“No.”

“You look thoughtful.”

“I am considering whether it is possible to teach someone to think about other people's schedules before making plans.”

Mira looked at the letter.

“Master Arkwright?”

Seraphina nodded.

Mira smiled.

“He seems intelligent.”

“He is.”

“Then he will learn.”

Seraphina considered that.

“I hope so.”

That evening, Seraphina joined her parents for dinner.

Alaric asked about the Academy inspection.

“The formal request was acceptable,” she said. “We have approved access to the western conduit.”

“Will you attend?”

“No. Cedric will arrange for a local administrator to accompany the team.”

“Good.”

Evelyne looked at her.

“You seem relieved.”

“I have successfully avoided becoming a magical engineer.”

Alaric smiled.

“An important achievement.”

Seraphina took a bite of her food.

Her father continued.

“Roland has also received a response from the regional agricultural administration.”

She stopped chewing.

“What response?”

“They have located part of the old Northern Provincial Supply Office archive.”

Seraphina put down her fork.

“Which records?”

“Several inventory sheets, a temporary depot register, and a transfer schedule.”

“Does it include the final reconciliation?”

“Not yet.”

“Does it include the appointment of the temporary depot staff?”

“Yes.”

Seraphina looked at him.

“And?”

Alaric placed a folded sheet beside her plate.

“The temporary depot supervisor was Peter Hales.”

Silence settled over the table.

Seraphina looked at the name.

It was the same name that had appeared in the disputed maintenance authorization.

She read it again.

“Is it the same Peter Hales?”

“We do not know,” Alaric replied. “The register provides only his name and position. We need to establish his identity.”

“Could the regional office confirm his dates of service?”

“They are checking.”

Seraphina slowly put down her fork.

The two matters might be connected.

Or they might not.

A common name was not evidence.

An old administrative record was not proof of wrongdoing.

Still, the coincidence deserved attention.

“Does the temporary depot register show how much grain arrived?”

Alaric nodded.

“It records the arrival of two hundred and sixty measures.”

Seraphina looked up sharply.

“All of it?”

“According to the register.”

“Then the grain reached the depot.”

“The register says it did.”

“Is there a receiving signature?”

“Yes.”

“Whose?”

Alaric turned the document toward her.

At the bottom of the page was a signature.

**Peter Hales.**

Seraphina stared at it.

For the first time in weeks, the old winter emergency had produced a clear answer.

The grain had been recorded as received.

But the question had changed.

If all two hundred and sixty measures reached the depot, what happened to them afterward?

She looked at her father.

“Do we have the distribution records?”

“Not yet.”

“And the final reconciliation?”

“Not yet.”

Seraphina picked up the document again.

The signature was neat.

The figures were clear.

The date was recorded.

Two hundred and sixty measures received.

No indication of shortage.

No indication of damage.

No indication of distribution.

Only a note in the margin:

**Awaiting final allocation schedule.**

She looked at the note for a long moment.

Then she closed the document.

“We need the allocation schedule.”

Alaric nodded.

“Yes.”

Seraphina returned to her meal.

For several minutes, nobody spoke.

Then Evelyne quietly asked:

“Would you like some more vegetables?”

Seraphina looked at her.

“Yes, please.”

Her mother served her.

The kingdom could wait until after dinner.

Later that night, Seraphina opened her notebook.

She added a new section.

**Northern Consolidated Depot — Updated Findings**

- The Northern Provincial Supply Office accepted responsibility for the depot three days after the original emergency order.
- The office's records were later transferred to several successor institutions.
- Former receiving clerk Edmund Clarke remembers that the depot records were sent for reconciliation.
- The temporary depot was supervised by a man named Peter Hales.
- The surviving depot register records receipt of all 260 measures.
- The receiving signature is attributed to Peter Hales.
- Final allocation schedule not located.
- Final reconciliation not located.
- Identity of Peter Hales not yet independently confirmed.

She paused.

Then she wrote:

**The grain arrived. That does not tell us where it went.**

She read the sentence again.

It was the most important distinction yet.

The original question had been whether the grain had ever reached the temporary depot.

Now they had a record saying it had.

But a receiving register was not a distribution record.

And a distribution record was not proof that the grain had reached the villages.

There were still people on the other side of those numbers.

People who had waited through a terrible winter.

People who might remember what they had received.

People who might remember what they had not.

Seraphina closed the notebook.

She was beginning to understand why her father had insisted on taking this investigation seriously.

The records were old.

The officials were mostly gone.

The offices had been reorganized.

But the consequences had not necessarily disappeared with them.

She extinguished the lamp.

Tomorrow, she would ask Roland to locate the allocation schedule.

Tonight, she intended to sleep.

And if the kingdom wanted to misplace another document, it could do so without her supervision.
