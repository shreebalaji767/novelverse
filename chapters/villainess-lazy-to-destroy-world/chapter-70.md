# Chapter 70 — The Hearing That Was Supposed to Establish the Facts

The hearing notice arrived in a blue envelope.

Seraphina disliked it immediately.

Not because of the color.

The color was perfectly respectable.

She disliked it because the envelope contained eleven pages of instructions, three schedules, two procedural appendices, and a request that all written evidence be submitted in duplicate.

Mira placed it on the breakfast table.

"From the Royal Agricultural Council, my lady."

Seraphina looked at the envelope.

"How many pages?"

"Eleven."

"Did they include the instructions for reading the eleven pages?"

Mira considered this.

"There is an appendix."

Seraphina closed her eyes.

"Of course there is."

She opened the envelope.

The hearing would examine four subjects:

1. The proposed voluntary central index of historical property and infrastructure agreements.
2. The circumstances surrounding the Northern Development Historical Review Committee's memorandum.
3. The role of private archival organizations in the consultation process.
4. The accuracy of public statements concerning regional support and participation.

The council would invite representatives from the Treasury, the Royal Agricultural Council, the Northern Administrative Liaison Office, Northgate Historical Services, and participating estates.

The hearing would not determine private property disputes or reopen completed financial obligations.

That final sentence was important.

Seraphina read it twice.

The council had finally understood that a public hearing about historical records should not become an opportunity for everyone to renegotiate their grandparents' contracts.

She placed the notice beside her plate.

"Who prepared the witness schedule?"

Mira checked the last page.

"The council secretary."

"Good. At least someone has accepted responsibility for the pages."

The preparation took four days.

Cedric arranged the Veyr documents in chronological order.

The first folder contained the original guarantee.

The second contained the Treasury's historical register.

The third held the closure order.

The fourth contained the inspection report prepared by Northgate.

The fifth included the company's correspondence with the estate.

The sixth held the council's memorandum and the forwarding note.

Seraphina examined the arrangement.

"Why are the newspaper articles here?"

Cedric looked at the folder.

"Because the hearing will examine public statements."

"Then separate them from the original records."

He nodded.

She continued.

"The articles should not appear beside the guarantee. Someone might assume they are supporting documents."

Cedric moved them.

"Anything else?"

"Put the Treasury's correction beside the original register entry, not the newspaper report."

"Understood."

Seraphina looked at the six folders.

It was a surprisingly manageable collection.

The problem was not the volume of evidence.

It was the number of conclusions people might attempt to draw from it.

She wanted the hearing to establish five things clearly:

House Veyr had entered a conditional guarantee.

The estate had paid its required contribution.

The remaining conditional amount had been released when the unfinished structure was formally abandoned.

The Treasury's historical register had not been updated.

Northgate had identified the old entry as a potential exposure but had not established an outstanding debt.

None of those facts required a dramatic speech.

They required accurate documents.

Unfortunately, public hearings rarely attracted people because they enjoyed accurate documents.

The day before the hearing, Evelyne entered Seraphina's room.

Her mother carried a small box.

Seraphina looked at it.

"What is that?"

"Your hearing clothes."

"I have clothes."

"You have several dresses."

"Exactly."

"None suitable for a formal council hearing."

Seraphina examined the box.

Inside was a dark blue dress with a high collar, restrained silver embroidery, and no unnecessary decoration.

It was elegant without being extravagant.

She looked at her mother.

"How much?"

"That is not important."

"It usually is."

Evelyne smiled.

"House Veyr can afford a dress."

"That was not my question."

"It was a sensible price."

Seraphina accepted the answer.

Evelyne sat beside her.

"Do you understand what tomorrow may become?"

"A hearing."

"Possibly a political event."

"That is not the same thing."

"No. But people may treat it as one."

Seraphina adjusted the sleeve.

"Then I will answer the questions."

"And if they ask you to speak for the other estates?"

"I will refuse."

"And if they ask whether the council should establish the index?"

"I will say that the council should define its authority, access rules, and correction procedures before deciding."

Evelyne nodded.

"Good."

She looked at her daughter for a moment.

"Do not mistake being careful for being silent."

Seraphina glanced at her.

"I am not planning to be silent."

"I know."

Evelyne stood.

"That is why I brought the dress."

The hearing took place in the Royal Council's eastern chamber.

It was not the largest chamber in the palace.

It was, however, large enough to make every chair appear expensive.

The council had arranged the seats in a semicircle.

At the center sat the presiding officer, Lady Margaret Ashford, a senior member of the Royal Agricultural Council.

Beside her were two legal advisers and a secretary.

The invited representatives sat along the opposite side.

Seraphina arrived with Cedric.

Alaric had decided that the estate's interests would be better served by allowing the younger generation to explain the records they had personally examined.

He had also warned Seraphina not to mistake an invitation for an obligation to answer every question.

She had agreed.

Lord Bellmont was already present.

Lord Marlowe sat two seats away.

Across the chamber, Adrian Holt of the Northern Administrative Liaison Office reviewed his notes.

Northgate's representatives arrived shortly afterward.

Adrian North carried a leather document case.

Lydia Voss held a slim folder.

Neither looked particularly pleased to be there.

Seraphina understood the feeling.

She was not pleased either.

Lady Ashford called the hearing to order.

"The purpose of this proceeding is to establish the facts surrounding the proposed historical records index and the related consultation. It is not a trial, and no finding of misconduct will be made without evidence."

She looked around the chamber.

"All participants will have an opportunity to respond to statements concerning their actions. The original documents will be retained in the council record. Copies submitted by private estates will be returned unless permission for retention is expressly granted."

Seraphina relaxed slightly.

At least the council had learned something from the earlier confusion.

Lady Ashford continued.

"We will begin with the background of the Veyr guarantee."

Seraphina was called first.

She stood.

She explained the guarantee.

The Northern Water and Transport Improvement Scheme had been established several decades earlier to improve roads, a bridge, and a water-control structure.

House Veyr had agreed to contribute 1,600 crowns.

The guarantee allowed for a maximum additional exposure of 2,400 crowns, subject to certified expenditure and a final schedule.

The estate had paid its contribution.

The road and bridge works were completed.

The water-control structure was suspended.

The formal closure order released the remaining conditional guarantees.

The Treasury's historical register had not been updated to reflect the closure.

She placed the relevant documents on the table.

"These are the original guarantee, the payment confirmation, and the closure order."

Lady Ashford examined them.

"Does House Veyr consider any amount outstanding?"

"No."

"On what basis?"

"The closure order explicitly released the conditional guarantee after the unfinished structure was abandoned. The estate's required contribution had already been paid."

"Was the closure order authenticated?"

"Yes. The Treasury confirmed the archival reference and compared the instrument with its retained copy."

"Did the Treasury amend its register?"

"It has agreed to do so. The original entry will remain, with a correction and a reference to the closure order."

Lady Ashford nodded.

"Thank you."

A council member asked whether the estate had suffered financial damage from the inaccurate register.

Seraphina considered the question.

"The register entry did not create a legal debt. However, it created uncertainty. Northgate's correspondence described a potential remaining exposure, and the estate had to spend time establishing whether the old guarantee had been discharged."

"Did the company demand payment?"

"No."

"Did it threaten legal action?"

"No."

"Did it claim to represent the Treasury?"

"Not directly."

The council member looked at her.

"Then why did the estate object to its correspondence?"

"Because the wording could be understood as implying that an official liability remained. The distinction between a possible historical exposure and an outstanding debt should have been made clear."

Lady Ashford nodded.

"That distinction will be included in the record."

Seraphina returned to her seat.

It had been easier than expected.

She suspected the next witness would make it more difficult.

Adrian North was called.

He began by explaining the company's work.

Northgate Historical Services had been established to preserve private and regional records, assist landowners with archival searches, and identify historical documents that might affect present-day property or financial arrangements.

The company had conducted preliminary research into old regional development schemes.

It had identified several incomplete or poorly indexed records.

It had also become interested in the possibility of providing a voluntary archival service if the council established a suitable framework.

Lady Ashford interrupted.

"Was Northgate authorized to collect private estate records?"

"No."

"Was it authorized to represent the estates?"

"No."

"Did it describe itself as a council representative?"

"Not in its formal correspondence."

"Did it imply that the council had appointed it?"

North hesitated.

"Some of our correspondence may have created that impression."

"Why?"

"We were discussing the possibility of future cooperation."

"With whom?"

"The council chairman's office."

"Had the council agreed to cooperate?"

"No formal agreement had been reached."

Lady Ashford looked at him.

"Then why did your letters refer to priority access under a proposed service?"

North opened his document case.

"We believed that early participants would be given an opportunity to access the service first."

"Who told you that?"

"No one explicitly."

The chamber became quiet.

North continued.

"It was an assumption based on the discussions."

Lady Ashford's expression remained neutral.

"An assumption is not an agreement."

"No, my lady."

"Did the estates agree to participate?"

"Not all of them."

"How many did?"

North looked at Lydia Voss.

She consulted her notes.

"Two estates expressed conditional interest. One asked for further information. The remaining correspondence consisted of acknowledgments or unanswered letters."

"Then why did your memorandum describe twelve estates as interested?"

North took a breath.

"The memorandum was prepared from our correspondence records. We interpreted the responses as a broader indication of interest."

"Did you verify that interpretation?"

"No."

"Did you ask the estates whether they agreed to be listed?"

"No."

Lady Ashford wrote something down.

"Why not?"

North looked uncomfortable.

"We believed the list was a preliminary indication for the council's consideration."

Seraphina watched him.

He was not denying the mistake.

But he was also describing it as a misunderstanding rather than a decision.

That distinction might be sincere.

It might also be convenient.

The hearing had not yet established enough to decide.

Lady Ashford continued.

"Did Northgate intend to obtain a commercial contract from the council?"

"Yes. We hoped to be considered if the council approved an archival pilot."

"Was this interest disclosed in your consultation submission?"

"Yes."

"Was it disclosed in the letters to the estates?"

North paused.

"Not consistently."

The answer was entered into the record.

Lydia Voss was called next.

She explained that she had prepared much of the historical correspondence and the preliminary list of estates.

She confirmed that the list had been based on the company's internal correspondence register.

She had classified responses according to three categories:

Positive interest.

Possible interest.

No response.

The problem was that the categories had later been combined.

The final memorandum had described all three as expressions of interest.

Lady Ashford asked who had approved that change.

Lydia consulted her notes.

"The memorandum was reviewed internally."

"By whom?"

"Mr North and myself."

"Was it reviewed by a solicitor?"

"No."

"Was it reviewed by the estates named in it?"

"No."

"Was it reviewed by the council?"

"No."

Lady Ashford paused.

"Then the memorandum was prepared entirely by Northgate."

"Yes."

"Understood."

Lydia also confirmed that the company had used an outside correspondence service to prepare and distribute some of its letters.

This was the first time the service had been mentioned.

Seraphina looked at Cedric.

He wrote a note.

Lady Ashford asked for the company's correspondence records and the name of the service provider.

Lydia agreed to submit them.

The matter was added to the hearing's outstanding evidence list.

Adrian Holt was called after lunch.

He explained that he had attended an informal meeting between the council chairman and Northgate.

The discussion had concerned the possible preservation of historical records.

Northgate had offered to provide an initial catalogue at its own expense.

Holt had considered the offer potentially useful.

He had later forwarded the company's memorandum to the chairman's office.

Lady Ashford asked whether he had checked the twelve-estate list.

"No."

"Why?"

"I understood that the council would examine the submission before using it."

"Did you tell the council that the list was unverified?"

"The forwarding note described the document as a private submission."

"That was not my question."

Holt paused.

"No. I did not explicitly state that the list was unverified."

"Did you believe it had been verified?"

"I did not know."

"Then why did you forward it as evidence of regional interest?"

Holt looked down at his notes.

"I forwarded it as material that might assist the council in assessing interest. I did not intend to certify its contents."

Lady Ashford considered him.

"Did you have any commercial relationship with Northgate?"

"No."

"Any family or financial connection?"

"No."

"Did you promise the company a contract?"

"No."

"Did you recommend that it receive one?"

"I recommended that the council consider its offer."

"That is a different matter."

"Yes."

The secretary recorded the exchange.

Holt's explanation was plausible.

It was also incomplete.

He had forwarded a document without checking its central claim.

The council had relied on the document without independently verifying it.

Northgate had presented its commercial hopes as a developing opportunity.

The estates had been listed as interested without their permission.

There was no evidence that these actions had been coordinated by a single person.

There was, however, evidence that several people had failed to ask basic questions.

The Treasury representative was called next.

He explained that the old Veyr guarantee had been recorded in the historical register.

The original closure order had been found.

The Treasury had confirmed that the remaining conditional amount had been released.

The register correction was in progress.

Lady Ashford asked whether other guarantees might contain similar errors.

The representative replied that a preliminary review had identified three additional records requiring verification.

Two appeared to have been formally discharged.

The third involved a project whose final closure papers had not yet been located.

Lord Bellmont leaned forward.

"Does that mean the estate owes money?"

"No."

"Does it mean the Treasury believes it owes money?"

"No. It means the historical record is incomplete."

"Then I suggest that the distinction be made clear in the public statement."

The representative agreed.

Seraphina listened carefully.

Three records.

Not twelve.

Not an entire region.

Three.

The council would need to investigate them individually.

She was relieved that the Treasury had not attempted to make the Veyr case into proof of a much larger problem.

The most difficult questions came during the afternoon session.

A council member asked whether the proposed central index was necessary.

Lord Corven replied that he supported better recordkeeping but did not want private agreements placed in a central system without clear legal safeguards.

Lord Marlowe agreed.

He also pointed out that a record of an agreement could be useful without making the agreement itself available to everyone.

The council member asked whether that distinction was practical.

Seraphina spoke.

"It depends on what the index contains."

The chamber turned toward her.

She continued.

"A public index might identify the existence of a document, its date, the parties, and whether it has been amended. It does not necessarily need to reproduce the full agreement."

The council member nodded.

"And what if a person needs to know the terms?"

"Then access should depend on the person's legal interest and the consent or authority required under the relevant agreement."

"Would that not make the index difficult to use?"

"Possibly."

Seraphina paused.

"But ease of use is not the only consideration."

A few people smiled.

She continued.

"If a system is easy to use because it exposes private information without proper authority, it is not a good system."

The legal adviser made a note.

Another council member asked whether House Veyr would support a pilot.

Seraphina answered carefully.

"House Veyr would consider participating in a limited pilot if the council establishes clear access rules, correction procedures, document ownership, and withdrawal rights. We would not authorize the transfer of original private records."

"Would you support a public index?"

"That depends on its contents and legal safeguards."

"Would you oppose a private contractor maintaining it?"

"Not automatically. But the contractor's authority, responsibilities, commercial interests, and access to records should be disclosed."

The answer was entered into the record.

It was neither approval nor rejection.

Seraphina considered that a reasonable position.

The hearing ended shortly before sunset.

Lady Ashford thanked the participants.

She announced that the council would publish a factual summary after reviewing the submitted evidence.

The summary would identify:

- Documents received.
- Claims verified.
- Claims not verified.
- Corrections made.
- Questions requiring further investigation.

The council would not issue a finding of misconduct at this stage.

Northgate would be required to submit its correspondence-service records.

The liaison office would provide the original meeting notes.

The Treasury would complete its review of the three additional historical guarantees.

The council would separately consider the proposed index and its legal safeguards.

The secretary collected the documents.

Seraphina returned to her seat.

Cedric leaned toward her.

"That went well."

"Did it?"

"Nobody accused us of trying to seize control of the regional estates."

"That is a low standard."

"It is a standard."

She considered this.

"Unfortunately."

Outside the chamber, journalists had gathered.

The *Crown and Country Gazette* correspondent approached first.

"Lady Seraphina, do you believe the hearing has established that Northgate misrepresented the estates?"

Seraphina answered.

"The hearing established that its memorandum described twelve estates as interested when the evidence supported only two conditional expressions of interest, one request for further information, and several acknowledgments or unanswered letters."

"Do you believe the company acted deliberately?"

"That was not established."

"Do you believe the council was misled?"

"The council relied on an unverified submission. Whether it was deliberately misled is a different question."

Another correspondent stepped forward.

"Will House Veyr support the central index?"

"We will consider the proposal after the access and legal rules are published."

"Do you oppose private archival companies?"

"No."

"Then what is your objection?"

"That a private company should not describe an estate as a participant without its consent."

The correspondent nodded.

The *Aurelion Morning Chronicle* reporter asked another question.

"Do you think the Northern Administrative Liaison Office should be investigated?"

Seraphina looked at him.

"The hearing established that it forwarded a private submission. It did not establish that it verified the contents."

"Is that sufficient?"

"It is sufficient to identify what happened. The council can decide whether its procedures need to change."

The reporter looked disappointed.

He had probably wanted something more dramatic.

Seraphina had no intention of supplying it.

The first newspaper report appeared the following morning.

The *Crown and Country Gazette* published:

**COUNCIL HEARING FINDS REGIONAL SUPPORT WAS OVERSTATED**

The article described the testimony accurately.

It distinguished between Northgate's commercial interest, the liaison office's forwarding role, and the council's decision to rely on an unverified submission.

The *Aurelion Daily Register* published:

**NORTHGATE ADMITS ESTATE INTEREST LIST WAS INACCURATE**

It was also broadly correct.

The *Aurelion Morning Chronicle* published:

**PRIVATE COMPANY, ROYAL OFFICE AND COUNCIL UNDER SCRUTINY AFTER RECORDS HEARING**

Seraphina read the headline.

It was not entirely wrong.

The hearing had examined all three.

But the article placed the three institutions beside one another in a way that suggested a common operation.

The body was more careful.

It stated that no coordinated misconduct had been established.

It also quoted the council's promise to examine its procedures.

Seraphina folded the paper.

At least the newspaper had included the qualification.

That was progress.

A small amount.

But progress.

Two days later, the council's preliminary summary arrived.

It was twelve pages long.

Seraphina stared at it.

Mira placed it on the table.

"Another report, my lady."

"Of course."

She opened it.

The first section confirmed the Veyr guarantee had been discharged.

The second section described Northgate's correspondence and its inaccurate classification of estate responses.

The third described Adrian Holt's forwarding note.

The fourth described the council's failure to verify the list before treating it as evidence of regional interest.

The fifth set out the outstanding questions.

One sentence caught Seraphina's attention.

*The council has not yet determined whether the preliminary list was prepared solely from Northgate's internal correspondence register or whether additional information was supplied by an external correspondence provider.*

She read it again.

An external correspondence provider.

Lydia Voss had mentioned one.

The company had used the service to prepare and distribute letters.

But the council had not yet received the provider's records.

Seraphina turned to the final page.

The summary listed the evidence still required.

Northgate's correspondence register.

The external provider's service agreement.

The original mailing instructions.

The names of individuals who had approved the twelve-estate list.

The council had also requested the original notes from Northgate's informal meeting with the chairman.

The inquiry was not finished.

It had merely established where the remaining questions were.

Seraphina closed the report.

Cedric entered.

"Have you read it?"

"Yes."

"Anything concerning?"

"One unresolved point."

He sat down.

"The correspondence provider?"

"Yes."

"Do you think it matters?"

"It might."

She looked at the report.

"Northgate says it prepared the list from its own records. The company also used an external service to prepare and distribute letters. We need to know whether the provider merely printed and mailed them or helped prepare the contents."

Cedric nodded.

"That is reasonable."

"Until then, we should not assume anything."

He smiled faintly.

"You sound like the council."

Seraphina looked at him.

"That is unfortunate."

He laughed.

The next letter arrived from the council the following afternoon.

It was not addressed to House Veyr.

It was addressed to all participating estates.

The council had decided to extend the inquiry.

It would examine the external correspondence provider's role and the preparation of the twelve-estate list.

It would also review whether any other private submissions had been treated as evidence of regional support without verification.

The council invited the estates to submit any relevant correspondence.

House Veyr had nothing further to add.

Seraphina told Cedric to reply accordingly.

Then she returned to her ordinary estate work.

There were three mill accounts awaiting review.

A request for roof repairs at the western granary.

A dispute over the delivery of winter seed.

And a letter from the regional agricultural office asking whether House Veyr would contribute to a new transport survey.

Seraphina read the last one.

She placed it aside.

"Not yet."

Cedric glanced at it.

"Because of the hearing?"

"Because we have not examined the route."

"Fair."

She returned to the mill accounts.

The hearing had established that no single person had controlled the entire chain of events.

Northgate had overstated interest.

The liaison office had forwarded an unverified submission.

The council had treated it as evidence.

The newspapers had amplified the resulting claims.

Each institution had contributed something different.

The remaining question was whether the correspondence provider had simply carried out instructions or had helped shape the material.

Seraphina had no answer.

For now, she had no reason to invent one.

She turned to the mill accounts.

A figure on the third page was incorrect.

She circled it.

"Tell Master Pike to check the processing fees again."

Cedric took the paper.

"Another discrepancy?"

"Yes."

"Serious?"

"No. Two customers were charged for the same storage period."

Cedric examined the entries.

"How did that happen?"

"One account was copied from the previous month, and the second was entered manually."

He sighed.

"Should I send it back?"

"Yes."

Cedric stood.

Seraphina leaned back.

For a brief moment, she considered the possibility that the kingdom might eventually learn to record things correctly.

Then she remembered the eleven-page hearing notice.

She decided not to become optimistic.

Outside, the autumn wind moved through the trees.

The granaries were being prepared for the coming winter.

The northern roads still needed inspection.

The transport survey remained unanswered.

And somewhere in Aurelion, a private correspondence company was being asked to explain exactly what it had done with twelve names.

Seraphina picked up her pen.

The hearing had not solved everything.

But it had established something important.

A document being forwarded did not mean its contents had been verified.

A name appearing on a list did not mean its owner had agreed.

And a proposal being discussed did not mean it had been approved.

She wrote the correction on the mill account.

Then she added one final instruction.

**Do not copy this month's figures into the next month's ledger without checking them.**

It was not a grand reform.

It was, however, a beginning.

And for once, she hoped the next problem would involve nothing more complicated than a broken wheelbarrow.
