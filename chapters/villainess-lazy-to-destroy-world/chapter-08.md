# Chapter 8 — The Village That Nobody Visits

Seraphina Veyr had a complicated relationship with the countryside.

She liked the scenery.

She liked the fresh air.

She liked the absence of people who wanted to discuss royal politics, marriage arrangements, or the financial implications of maintaining a ducal residence.

What she did not particularly like was travelling.

Unfortunately, the northern villages had begun appearing in her thoughts with increasing frequency.

It started with a report.

Then another report.

Then a collection of figures that did not agree with one another.

And finally, a letter from a village elder asking when someone from the ducal estate intended to inspect the damaged irrigation channel.

Seraphina read the letter twice.

Then she placed it on the table.

“Why has nobody visited?”

Mira, who was arranging her mistress's correspondence, glanced at the page.

“Which village, my lady?”

“Westmere.”

“Oh.”

Seraphina looked up.

“That sounded like a very meaningful oh.”

Mira hesitated.

“Westmere is rather far from the main estate.”

“How far?”

“Approximately half a day's journey by carriage, depending on the condition of the road.”

“That is not particularly far.”

“It is not close either.”

Seraphina looked at the letter again.

The village elder had written politely. The irrigation channel had been damaged during the previous month's heavy rain. Several fields were receiving less water than usual, and the villagers had already submitted two requests for repairs.

The first request had been recorded.

The second had been acknowledged.

Neither had received a response.

“Who is responsible for agricultural maintenance?”

“The regional property office.”

“Have they received the requests?”

“Yes, my lady.”

“Have they responded?”

“Not according to the letter.”

Seraphina folded the paper.

“Then I would like to know why.”

Mira nodded.

“Shall I ask Cedric?”

“Yes. And please request the original correspondence.”

Mira gathered the letter.

Seraphina added, “I am not asking for a formal investigation.”

“Of course.”

“I only want to know whether anyone has answered the village.”

“Of course, my lady.”

Seraphina watched Mira leave.

She looked at the letter again.

The handwriting was uneven.

The writer had clearly taken some time to compose it, choosing his words carefully.

There was no accusation.

No dramatic complaint.

Just a question.

*When may we expect someone to inspect the channel?*

Seraphina disliked that question.

Not because it was difficult.

Because the answer should have been obvious.

Cedric arrived shortly before lunch.

He carried a folder containing the relevant correspondence and a regional maintenance summary.

“Westmere,” he said, placing the folder on the table. “I expected this would come up eventually.”

Seraphina looked at him.

“Why?”

“Because the village has submitted several requests over the last year.”

“Several?”

“Yes.”

“About the irrigation channel?”

“Not all of them. There was also a request concerning the eastern footbridge and a complaint about the condition of the grain storage building.”

Seraphina opened the folder.

“Were those addressed?”

“The footbridge was repaired last spring.”

“And the storage building?”

“An inspection was scheduled.”

“Was it completed?”

Cedric checked the summary.

“It appears so.”

“Appears?”

“The regional office recorded the inspection as completed.”

Seraphina looked at the entry.

There was a date, the name of an inspector, and a brief note.

*Storage structure satisfactory. Minor maintenance recommended.*

No detailed observations were attached.

“Was the building repaired?”

“That is not clear.”

“Was the maintenance recommendation acted upon?”

“I would need to check.”

Seraphina turned another page.

The irrigation channel had been listed as a low-priority maintenance matter.

The original request had been received on the twelfth day of Harvestfall.

The acknowledgement had been sent four days later.

A note beside the entry read:

*Awaiting consolidated regional inspection.*

Seraphina looked at the date.

“That was nearly three months ago.”

“Yes.”

“Why is it still awaiting inspection?”

Cedric leaned forward.

“The regional office combines requests from several villages before sending an inspector. It reduces travel costs.”

“That makes sense.”

“It does, particularly during the harvest season.”

“But Westmere's request was not included in the last inspection?”

“Apparently not.”

“Why?”

Cedric checked the papers.

“It may have been considered less urgent than the bridge repairs.”

“Was that decision recorded?”

“I cannot see a reason.”

Seraphina closed the folder.

“Then I would like to visit.”

Cedric stared at her.

“My lady?”

“Westmere.”

“You wish to visit the village?”

“Yes.”

“Personally?”

Seraphina looked at him.

“I am not aware of another Seraphina Veyr who could go in my place.”

Cedric sighed.

It was a very familiar sound.

“Your father will need to approve.”

“Then ask him.”

“You understand that this will require preparation?”

“I assumed so.”

“And security arrangements.”

“Yes.”

“And a proper escort.”

“Of course.”

“And the journey may take several hours.”

Seraphina regarded him.

“Cedric.”

“Yes?”

“I am not planning to cross the northern mountains.”

He looked unconvinced.

“I will speak with the duke.”

Duke Alaric Veyr received the request that afternoon.

He read it once.

Then he looked at his daughter.

“Why?”

Seraphina had expected this question.

“The village has submitted several requests for repairs. I would like to see the condition of the irrigation channel and understand why the matter has been delayed.”

“You could ask the regional administrator to send a report.”

“I could.”

“Then why travel?”

“Because I would like to see the place.”

Alaric folded the letter.

“Seraphina, you have never visited Westmere.”

“No.”

“You have not travelled to the surrounding villages since you were a child.”

“That is probably true.”

“You do not know the road conditions.”

“That is why I would like to find out.”

Her father looked at Cedric.

Cedric gave a small nod.

“She has asked for the original correspondence and has not made any accusations.”

Alaric returned his attention to his daughter.

“You are aware that your presence will attract attention.”

Seraphina paused.

She had not considered that.

She imagined arriving in a village with a ducal carriage, several guards, and an entourage of servants.

The villagers would likely be nervous.

Some might assume she had come to inspect their conduct.

Others might believe she was bringing a decision.

She would become the center of attention simply by appearing.

“I can keep the party small,” she said.

“That is not what I meant.”

“I know.”

Alaric leaned back.

“If you visit, you must understand that people will expect you to listen. They may ask for things you cannot promise. They may complain about matters outside the authority of the estate.”

“I understand.”

“Do you?”

Seraphina considered the question.

“I will try to.”

Her father studied her for a moment.

Then he nodded.

“Very well. You may go with Cedric. A small escort, no formal announcement. I will inform the regional administrator that you are visiting.”

Seraphina relaxed.

“Thank you.”

“But you will not make commitments concerning expenditure without reviewing the matter with the appropriate department.”

“I know.”

“And you will not attempt to inspect the irrigation channel yourself.”

“I had not planned to climb into it.”

Alaric gave her a long look.

“Good.”

He returned to his papers.

Seraphina stood.

At the door, she heard him speak again.

“Your mother will want to know.”

Seraphina stopped.

“Why?”

“Because she is your mother.”

That was a difficult argument to challenge.

The journey was scheduled for the following morning.

Seraphina spent the evening reviewing the available information.

Westmere was a modest agricultural village in the northern part of the Veyr duchy. It had approximately one hundred and eighty households, most of them dependent on grain cultivation, livestock, and small-scale trade.

The village lay near a narrow river that supplied water to several irrigation channels.

Its main road connected to the regional market route.

The village had no permanent ducal administrator.

Instead, the regional property office sent representatives when necessary.

Seraphina studied the map.

The river curved around the western fields before dividing into two smaller channels.

One supplied the northern farms.

The other ran toward the village's eastern fields.

The damaged section appeared to be on the eastern branch.

She compared the map with the maintenance report.

The report described the damage as minor.

The village elder's letter described reduced water flow.

Those were not necessarily contradictory statements.

A small obstruction could affect the distribution of water without causing visible structural damage.

Still, she wanted to see it.

Mira entered carrying a small travel case.

“My lady, I have prepared your clothes.”

Seraphina looked at the case.

“How many?”

“Three dresses, two riding outfits, a cloak, gloves, and suitable footwear.”

“For one day?”

“You may require a change.”

“I am going to a village, not attending a royal banquet.”

Mira placed the case beside the wardrobe.

“Your mother requested that I prepare a spare formal dress.”

Seraphina stared at the case.

“Why?”

“Your mother believes it is better to be prepared.”

Seraphina sighed.

There were some battles that could not be won.

“Fine.”

Mira began arranging the remaining items.

After a moment, she asked, “Are you looking forward to the journey?”

Seraphina looked at the map.

“I am looking forward to understanding the problem.”

“That was not quite what I asked.”

Seraphina considered it.

“I suppose I am curious.”

Mira smiled.

“Then I hope the road is pleasant.”

Seraphina looked at the window.

Outside, the evening sky was clear.

She had a feeling the road would not be pleasant at all.

The carriage departed shortly after sunrise.

Cedric travelled in the same carriage, accompanied by two guards and a small supply wagon.

Seraphina had insisted on keeping the party limited.

Her father had agreed, although he had added another guard at the last moment.

She had not complained.

The road initially passed through the cultivated lands surrounding the estate.

Fields stretched across gentle slopes, divided by low stone walls and narrow drainage channels.

Workers were already outside.

Some were repairing fences.

Others were preparing equipment for the coming season.

Seraphina watched through the carriage window.

She recognized the land from maps and reports, but the physical distance between places became more apparent when travelling through them.

A village could be a single line on a page.

Reaching it required roads, bridges, carts, animals, and time.

The carriage slowed as the road narrowed.

Cedric glanced outside.

“We will be passing through the lower agricultural district.”

Seraphina looked at the fields.

“Are these all estate lands?”

“Most are tenant holdings. Some belong to smaller landowners who owe taxes and service obligations to the duchy.”

“Do we have a record of the land boundaries?”

“Yes.”

“Is it accurate?”

Cedric smiled faintly.

“You have been reading the administrative reports.”

“Unfortunately.”

“The boundaries are generally accurate. There are occasional disputes, particularly where old markers have been damaged.”

Seraphina looked toward a field where two workers were repairing a stone boundary post.

“Does the estate inspect them?”

“Not regularly.”

“Why?”

“Because the disputes are usually resolved locally.”

“Usually?”

Cedric gave her a sideways glance.

“You are going to find problems everywhere if you keep asking questions.”

“That is an unfortunate possibility.”

He laughed quietly.

The carriage continued.

By midmorning, the road became rougher.

The wheels struck several shallow depressions, and Seraphina had to hold her cup with both hands.

“This road needs repair.”

Cedric nodded.

“It is scheduled for maintenance.”

“When?”

“After the spring rains.”

Seraphina looked at him.

“Is that because the road is expected to deteriorate further during the rains?”

“Partly.”

“Then would it not be better to repair the worst sections before they become worse?”

“Possibly. But the maintenance budget is limited. The regional office prioritizes roads according to traffic, military importance, and expected damage.”

Seraphina nodded.

That made sense.

She disliked how often sensible explanations still produced inconvenient results.

A few minutes later, the carriage passed a roadside cart.

One wheel had broken.

A man stood beside it, examining the damage while a young boy held the reins of the horse.

Cedric instructed the driver to slow down.

“Should we stop?” Seraphina asked.

“If the road is blocked, we should.”

The driver moved the carriage to one side.

Cedric stepped outside and spoke with the man.

Seraphina watched.

The cart was loaded with sacks of grain.

The broken wheel had collapsed inward, leaving the cart tilted.

The man looked tired.

He was perhaps in his forties, with a weathered face and a coat patched at the elbows.

After a brief conversation, Cedric returned.

“The cart belongs to a farmer from Westmere.”

“Is he travelling to the market?”

“Yes. He says he has been delayed for nearly an hour.”

“Can we help?”

“The driver can lend him a tool, but we cannot transport the grain.”

Seraphina looked at the cart.

“Does this happen often?”

Cedric shrugged.

“Not frequently.”

“Are broken carts recorded anywhere?”

“Not usually.”

“Then how does the estate know whether the road is damaging them?”

Cedric paused.

“That is a fair question.”

He returned to the driver and instructed him to lend the farmer a spare tool from the supply wagon.

Seraphina watched the man thank him.

The carriage moved on.

She wrote a short note in her notebook.

*Road conditions: practical impact on transport costs may not be reflected in maintenance reports.*

Then she put the notebook away.

She was beginning to understand that a report could describe a road as usable while a farmer stood beside it with a broken cart.

Both things could be true.

Westmere appeared shortly before noon.

It was smaller than Seraphina expected.

The village consisted of several rows of stone and timber houses, a modest market square, a public well, a chapel, and a handful of workshops.

Beyond the houses lay fields of varying sizes.

Some were already being prepared for planting.

Others remained bare.

A small group of people had gathered near the village entrance.

The village elder stood at the front.

He was an elderly man with white hair, a walking stick, and a dark green coat.

When the carriage stopped, he stepped forward and bowed.

“Lady Seraphina Veyr. Welcome to Westmere.”

Seraphina stepped down.

“Thank you for receiving us.”

“My name is Alden Mercer. I am the village elder.”

She inclined her head.

“Master Mercer.”

He looked slightly surprised that she had addressed him by his title rather than simply acknowledging him.

“This is a great honor, my lady.”

Seraphina smiled politely.

“I hope I am not causing too much inconvenience.”

“Not at all.”

Behind him stood several villagers.

Some bowed.

Others looked at her with open curiosity.

A little girl peered from behind her mother's skirt.

Seraphina resisted the urge to wave.

She was not entirely certain what the correct ducal greeting was for a child hiding behind a skirt.

Cedric stepped forward.

“Master Mercer, Her Ladyship has come to inspect the irrigation channel and discuss the requests submitted to the regional office.”

The elder nodded.

“Of course. We have prepared the relevant records.”

Seraphina looked at him.

“I would also like to speak with some of the farmers, if they are available.”

“Certainly, my lady.”

He hesitated.

“Would you prefer to rest first?”

Seraphina considered the journey.

Her back hurt.

Her shoulders were stiff.

Her stomach was empty.

“Yes,” she said honestly. “That would be appreciated.”

The elder looked relieved.

The village hall was a modest building beside the market square.

Its main room contained several wooden tables, benches, and a large hearth.

A meal had been prepared.

Bread, vegetable stew, cheese, and roasted poultry were placed on the table.

Seraphina sat with Cedric and the village elder.

She had expected a formal welcome.

Instead, she was served a bowl of stew that smelled considerably better than the food she had eaten during the journey.

She took a spoonful.

It was excellent.

“Who prepared this?”

“The village cooks,” Alden replied.

“Please thank them. It is very good.”

The elder smiled.

“I will.”

For a while, they discussed ordinary matters.

The weather had been unpredictable.

The river had risen after the last heavy rainfall.

Several farmers had lost sections of fencing.

The market road was becoming difficult to use.

Seraphina listened.

She did not interrupt.

When the meal ended, Alden brought out a bundle of documents.

“These are copies of the requests submitted to the regional office.”

Seraphina examined them.

The first request concerned the eastern irrigation channel.

The second requested repairs to the storage building.

The third concerned the footbridge.

She recognized the dates.

“Why did you submit three separate requests?”

“Because they concern different matters.”

“Did the regional office ask you to separate them?”

“No, my lady. We believed it would make them easier to process.”

Seraphina nodded.

That was reasonable.

“Did you receive acknowledgements for all three?”

“Yes.”

“Did anyone visit?”

“For the bridge, yes. The repairs were completed last spring.”

“And the storage building?”

Alden looked down.

“An inspector came. He said the structure was satisfactory.”

“Was anything repaired?”

“No.”

Seraphina looked at the report.

“Did he explain why?”

“He said the building was safe to use.”

The elder paused.

“But we had not asked whether it was safe. We asked whether the roof could be repaired.”

Seraphina looked up.

“What is wrong with the roof?”

“Several tiles have cracked. During heavy rain, water enters through the northern side.”

“Does it affect the grain?”

“Not yet. We moved the sacks away from the damaged area.”

Seraphina closed the report.

“Has anyone inspected it since?”

“No.”

She looked at Cedric.

He made a note.

Seraphina returned her attention to the elder.

“And the irrigation channel?”

Alden's expression became more serious.

“The water flow has decreased. We believe the damage is near the eastern bend.”

“Have you tried repairing it?”

“We have cleared some of the smaller obstructions ourselves. But the stone lining has shifted. If we dig too deeply, the bank may collapse.”

“Has anyone been injured?”

“No, my lady.”

“Has the reduced flow affected the fields?”

“Yes.”

He pointed toward the eastern side of the village.

“Several families depend on that channel. They have been receiving less water.”

Seraphina looked at the documents.

“Do you know how much less?”

“We have not measured it.”

“Could you?”

“We could estimate.”

“I would prefer an actual measurement, if possible.”

Alden nodded.

“We can arrange that.”

Seraphina paused.

“Master Mercer, I would like to speak with the farmers who use the channel. I do not want to rely only on the village's general report.”

The elder looked relieved.

“Of course.”

The eastern fields were a short walk from the village.

Seraphina travelled with Cedric, Alden, and two farmers.

The first was a broad-shouldered man named Tomas Reed.

The second was a woman in her thirties named Eliza Ward.

Both seemed uncomfortable speaking directly to the duke's daughter.

Seraphina tried to make the conversation easier.

“I am not here to decide whether anyone has done something wrong. I want to understand what is happening.”

Tomas nodded.

“The water used to reach the lower fields by midday.”

“And now?”

“Sometimes it arrives late in the afternoon. Sometimes there is barely enough.”

Eliza pointed toward the channel.

“The flow becomes weaker after the bend.”

Seraphina looked at the water.

The channel was narrow, lined with stone along its sides.

In some places, weeds had grown between the stones.

The water moved slowly.

She crouched near the bank.

Cedric immediately stepped closer.

“My lady.”

“I am not entering the channel.”

“Good.”

She leaned forward to examine the stone lining.

A section near the bend had shifted inward.

The channel had not collapsed, but the water was being forced through a narrower opening.

Beyond it, the bank appeared damp.

Seraphina stood.

“Has anyone measured the water level before and after the bend?”

Tomas shook his head.

“No, my lady.”

“Could you show me where the water enters and where it divides?”

The farmers led her farther along the channel.

There was a wooden gate controlling the flow toward the eastern fields.

Its hinges were rusted.

One side sat lower than the other.

“Does the gate close properly?” she asked.

“Not anymore,” Tomas replied. “We have to push it down.”

“Who maintains it?”

“The regional office.”

“When was it last repaired?”

Tomas exchanged a glance with Eliza.

“Two years ago, I think.”

Seraphina looked at Cedric.

He made another note.

She did not know enough about irrigation engineering to suggest a repair.

She also did not intend to pretend otherwise.

“Could we ask someone experienced with water channels to inspect both the damaged stonework and the gate?”

Cedric nodded.

“I will contact the regional maintenance office.”

“Please make sure they examine the entire eastern branch, not just the visible damage.”

“Yes, my lady.”

Eliza looked at Seraphina.

“Will it be repaired?”

Seraphina turned toward her.

“I cannot promise that today.”

The woman's expression fell slightly.

Seraphina continued.

“But I can make sure the request is properly examined. If the damage is as you describe, the estate should determine what repairs are needed and how urgently they should be done.”

Eliza nodded.

It was not the answer she wanted.

Seraphina knew that.

But it was the answer she could honestly give.

The storage building stood near the northern edge of the village.

It was a long, low structure with a tiled roof and thick stone walls.

Alden unlocked the door.

Inside, the air smelled of grain, dust, and old wood.

Several sacks were stacked along the walls.

The center of the building was empty.

Seraphina looked up.

A dark stain marked the northern ceiling.

“Is that where the water enters?”

“Yes,” Alden replied.

“Has it become larger?”

“I believe so.”

She looked at the sacks.

“Are these the village reserves?”

“Partly. Some belong to individual farmers.”

“Are they recorded separately?”

“Yes. The village keeps a register.”

“May I see it?”

The elder produced a ledger from a nearby cupboard.

Seraphina opened it.

The entries were simple but consistent.

Names.

Quantities.

Dates of storage.

Withdrawals.

Remaining amounts.

It was much easier to understand than the consolidated reports she had seen at the estate.

She compared the entries with the figures in the regional summary.

There was a difference.

Not a large one.

But enough to catch her attention.

“Master Mercer, the regional report lists a larger remaining quantity than this register.”

The elder leaned closer.

“Does it?”

“Yes.”

He examined the figures.

Then his expression changed.

“That includes the grain held in the western storehouse.”

Seraphina looked at him.

“Is that a separate building?”

“Yes, my lady. It is smaller. It belongs to the miller.”

“Is it included in the village register?”

“No. The miller keeps his own records.”

Seraphina nodded.

The discrepancy had an explanation.

She wrote it down.

“Does the regional office know that these figures are separate?”

“I believe so.”

“Is there a document confirming it?”

Alden thought for a moment.

“There should be.”

Cedric made a note.

Seraphina closed the ledger.

“Please do not change your records to match the regional summary. Keep the village register as it is.”

Alden looked surprised.

“Would that not cause confusion?”

“Only if the records are not clearly identified. We should record the western storehouse separately rather than combine the figures without explanation.”

The elder nodded.

“That would be easier.”

Seraphina looked around the building again.

The roof still needed repair.

The storage records were not perfect, but they were understandable.

The irrigation channel needed a proper inspection.

And the village had spent months waiting for someone to visit.

She was beginning to see why the people here had written another letter.

It was not because they expected a miracle.

They wanted someone to look.

The afternoon passed quickly.

Seraphina spoke with several villagers.

A blacksmith complained that the market road made it difficult to transport finished tools.

A farmer asked whether the estate could reconsider the timing of certain agricultural payments.

An elderly woman mentioned that the public well needed cleaning.

A young man asked whether the village might receive permission to repair a section of the footpath without waiting for regional approval.

Each request was different.

Some were reasonable.

Some required further examination.

Others were outside Seraphina's authority.

She listened, asked questions, and wrote notes.

By the time she returned to the village hall, her notebook was nearly full.

Cedric looked at the pages.

“You have collected quite a number of matters.”

“I noticed.”

“Some will require separate departments.”

“I noticed that too.”

“And several cannot be resolved immediately.”

“I am painfully aware.”

Cedric smiled.

“Then the visit was useful.”

Seraphina looked through the window.

A group of children were playing near the well.

One of them was carrying a wooden hoop.

Another was attempting to balance a stick on his head.

The little girl who had hidden behind her mother's skirt earlier was now chasing a dog.

“Cedric?”

“Yes?”

“Why does nobody from the estate visit this village regularly?”

He considered his answer.

“The regional office sends representatives when requests require attention. There is no permanent administrator here.”

“Why not?”

“Because the village is relatively small.”

“Does that mean its problems are less important?”

“No.”

“Then why are they handled less frequently?”

Cedric was quiet for a moment.

“Because there are many villages, and limited staff.”

Seraphina nodded.

She had expected that answer.

It did not make her like it.

“Could the regional office establish a regular schedule for visits?”

“Possibly. It would require additional travel.”

“Would that be expensive?”

“Not particularly, if visits were combined by district.”

Seraphina looked at him.

“Then perhaps that is worth considering.”

Cedric nodded.

“I will discuss it with the regional administrator.”

She hesitated.

“And perhaps the villages could receive a written record of their requests, including whether they were approved, delayed, or rejected.”

“That would require a more organized correspondence system.”

“Would it be difficult?”

“Not impossible.”

Seraphina sighed.

“Everything seems to require a new system.”

Cedric laughed.

“You are the one who keeps finding gaps.”

“I would prefer to find a comfortable chair.”

“Perhaps you should have remained at the estate.”

Seraphina looked at the village outside.

“No.”

The answer came more quickly than she expected.

She looked back at him.

“I think I needed to see this.”

Cedric did not respond immediately.

Then he nodded.

The return journey began shortly before sunset.

Seraphina was tired.

Her feet hurt.

Her back hurt.

She had acquired a headache.

And she had learned that a village could be forgotten without anyone deliberately deciding to forget it.

No one had ordered the irrigation channel to remain damaged.

No one had instructed the regional office to ignore Westmere.

The requests had simply moved through a system that prioritized other matters.

The village had been placed behind more urgent tasks.

Then behind other tasks.

Then behind the passage of time.

Seraphina watched the fields pass outside the carriage window.

“Cedric?”

“Yes?”

“Do you think the estate is poorly managed?”

He looked at her.

“That is a broad question.”

“I know.”

“The estate is large. Some departments are more efficient than others. There are delays, outdated procedures, and occasional disagreements.”

“Does that mean it is poorly managed?”

“Not necessarily.”

Seraphina considered this.

“Then what does?”

Cedric looked out of the window.

“Perhaps a place is poorly managed when it stops noticing the people affected by its decisions.”

She looked at him.

He continued.

“The estate has always been concerned with its responsibilities. But sometimes we become so occupied with maintaining the system that we forget why the system exists.”

Seraphina was quiet.

She thought about the irrigation channel.

The storage building.

The broken cart.

The villagers waiting for a response.

She had spent the last several days examining records and trying to understand the estate's administrative structure.

But the purpose of those records was not to make the ledgers look orderly.

They were supposed to represent something real.

Fields.

Buildings.

Workers.

Families.

A village that needed water.

She looked down at her notebook.

“I don't think I can fix everything.”

Cedric smiled faintly.

“I would be concerned if you thought you could.”

“Thank you for your confidence.”

“You are welcome.”

Seraphina rested her head against the carriage cushion.

After a moment, she closed her eyes.

The carriage continued toward the estate.

The following morning, Seraphina received a letter from her father.

It was brief.

He had been informed of the visit by Cedric and had reviewed the notes.

The regional maintenance office would send an inspector to Westmere within three days.

The irrigation channel and storage building would be examined.

The regional administrator had also been instructed to review the village's outstanding requests and provide a written explanation for the delays.

Seraphina read the letter twice.

Then she placed it beside her breakfast.

Mira poured tea.

“Good news, my lady?”

“Somewhat.”

“Will the repairs be made?”

“Only after the inspection.”

Mira nodded.

Seraphina picked up a piece of bread.

“Did the village elder receive confirmation?”

“I believe Cedric arranged for a copy of your father's instructions to be sent.”

“Good.”

She took a sip of tea.

Then another.

For a few minutes, the room was quiet.

It was a pleasant morning.

A very ordinary morning.

Then Mira placed another letter beside her plate.

Seraphina looked at it.

“What is that?”

“A report from the northern agricultural office.”

“About Westmere?”

“No, my lady. About the grain storage figures from the three villages you previously reviewed.”

Seraphina stopped chewing.

“Have they completed the verification?”

“Not yet. But they have sent an updated summary.”

She opened the envelope.

The report contained a revised table with standardized measurements.

For the first time, the figures were presented in a consistent format.

She examined them.

The totals were clearer.

The individual village records were separated.

The consolidated figures could be compared.

Then she noticed a footnote.

*One storage location not physically inspected due to access restrictions.*

Seraphina read it again.

“Which storage location?”

Mira glanced at the report.

“I am not certain, my lady.”

Seraphina turned the page.

The location was identified by a code rather than a village name.

**Northern Storehouse: N-14.**

She searched the accompanying list.

It did not appear among the three villages.

It was not included in the previous reports.

She set down her tea.

“Please ask Roland to come.”

Mira nodded.

Seraphina looked at the footnote again.

She had visited a village.

She had wanted to understand why its requests had been ignored.

Now another storage location had appeared in the agricultural records.

She did not know what it meant.

It might be an old building.

It might belong to a separate department.

It might be a harmless clerical omission.

She was not going to assume otherwise.

Still, she found herself writing one final note in her notebook.

*Do not trust a summary until you know what it leaves out.*

She paused.

Then added:

*And perhaps do not visit villages without bringing a more comfortable pair of shoes.*

**End of Chapter 8**
