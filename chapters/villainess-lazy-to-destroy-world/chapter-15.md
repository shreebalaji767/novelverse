# Chapter 15 — The Signature on the Winter Order

The letter arrived at breakfast.

Seraphina considered this a personal attack.

Not because the letter was alarming. Not because it bore an unfamiliar seal. Not even because it had been delivered by a messenger who looked as though he had travelled through three provinces without stopping to eat.

It was because breakfast had only just begun.

She had successfully eaten half a slice of buttered bread, finished a cup of tea, and persuaded herself that the morning might pass without anyone mentioning grain.

Then Mira placed the envelope beside her plate.

“From the northern estate office, my lady.”

Seraphina stared at it.

“Does it concern grain?”

Mira paused.

“Yes.”

“Put it somewhere I cannot see.”

“I believe that would defeat the purpose of delivering it.”

“An unfortunate flaw in the system.”

Across the table, Duchess Evelyne concealed a smile behind her teacup.

Duke Alaric did not.

“Open it, Seraphina.”

“I was hoping Father would say something like, ‘You have done enough. Go back to bed.’”

“You have already slept for eight hours.”

“An excellent foundation for further rest.”

“Open the letter.”

Seraphina sighed and broke the seal.

The letter was from Cedric. Its contents were brief.

Martin Hale had sent a second message.

This one concerned the signature on the emergency winter order.

Seraphina stopped chewing.

She read the letter again.

Then she put it down.

“Father.”

Alaric looked up.

“Yes?”

“Did the old emergency committee have a permanent chairman?”

“It had a presiding official appointed for each emergency session. The position was not necessarily held by the same person throughout the year.”

“Then why would the order bear one person's signature if someone else delivered it?”

“Because the person who signed an order does not always have to deliver it.”

“That is what I thought.”

Alaric folded his napkin.

“However, Martin's recollection suggests the named official may not have been present when the instruction reached the regional office. That is worth checking.”

“Could he have signed it earlier?”

“Certainly.”

“Could someone have delivered it under his authority?”

“Possibly.”

“Could the seal have been used by someone else?”

“Also possible.”

Seraphina looked at her father.

“You are making it difficult to accuse anyone.”

“That is generally the purpose of evidence.”

Evelyne set down her teacup.

“And it is a very good thing that you have not yet accused anyone.”

Seraphina leaned back.

“I wasn't planning to.”

Alaric raised an eyebrow.

“You were planning to?”

“I was planning to avoid planning anything.”

“That is not the same thing.”

“It is the closest thing I have.”

Mira quietly removed the empty bread plate.

Seraphina watched it go.

She had wanted a peaceful breakfast.

Instead, she had received another historical mystery involving several hundred measures of grain, a temporary warehouse, a missing confirmation, and a signature that might have been perfectly legitimate.

The kingdom was becoming increasingly inconvenient.

The financial office was unusually quiet when Seraphina arrived later that morning.

Roland Ashford was seated at his desk with three documents arranged in front of him. Cedric stood beside the window, holding a folder.

Neither looked pleased.

Seraphina immediately became suspicious.

“Has something happened?”

Roland looked up.

“Nothing conclusive, my lady.”

“That is not reassuring.”

“It is not intended to be.”

Cedric gestured toward the chair opposite Roland.

“Please sit. We received Martin Hale's second letter this morning.”

Seraphina sat.

Roland passed her a copy.

The letter was written in careful handwriting, with several corrections where the writer had apparently reconsidered his wording.

> To the Duke of Veyr,
>
> Regarding the emergency winter order preserved in my records, I have examined the name and date again.
>
> I remember the instruction arriving at the regional purchasing office during the winter emergency. The messenger stated that it had come through the central emergency committee.
>
> The official named on the order was not in the capital at that time, according to my recollection. I cannot say whether he had signed the document earlier, whether an authorized deputy carried it, or whether the messenger was mistaken.
>
> I do not wish my memory to be treated as proof of wrongdoing.
>
> I kept the document because it was unusual for an order involving so many stores to be delivered without the accompanying receiving schedule.
>
> Respectfully,
>
> Martin Hale.

Seraphina read it twice.

“He is being very careful.”

“He should be,” Roland replied. “His recollection is important, but it is not a substitute for official records.”

“Do we know the name?”

Cedric opened the folder.

“Yes.”

He placed a separate sheet on the desk.

**Lord Chancellor Edmund Harrow.**

Seraphina looked at it.

She knew the name.

Not from the original novel. At least, she did not remember seeing it there.

She knew it because it was written in the old administrative history she had read several days earlier.

Lord Chancellor Harrow had served under the previous king. He had been responsible for several major reforms in provincial taxation and emergency procurement.

He had died eleven years ago.

Seraphina tapped the paper.

“Was he chairman of the emergency committee?”

“For a period,” Cedric said. “The surviving appointments register lists him as presiding official during the winter emergency.”

“Then Martin may be mistaken about the date.”

“Possibly,” Roland said.

“Or the order may have been signed earlier.”

“Possibly.”

“Or someone else may have delivered it.”

“Also possible.”

Seraphina lowered her hand.

“Do you ever get tired of saying possibly?”

Roland regarded her calmly.

“I would become very tired of saying certainly when I had no evidence.”

Cedric coughed.

It sounded suspiciously like a laugh.

Seraphina ignored him.

“What records do we have?”

Roland arranged the documents.

“First, the emergency order itself. Second, the committee appointment register. Third, the old regional purchasing register. Fourth, the list of temporary depot locations. We have requested the capital's official travel and attendance records for Lord Chancellor Harrow.”

“And his signature specimens?”

“Those are being requested from the royal archives.”

“Could we compare the handwriting?”

“We can compare it. We cannot authenticate it ourselves.”

Seraphina nodded.

“Good.”

Roland looked mildly surprised.

“Good, my lady?”

“Yes. I would prefer not to become an expert in handwriting authentication before lunch.”

Cedric finally smiled.

Roland did not.

He had the expression of a man who had spent twenty-one years learning not to smile in front of the duke's daughter.

Seraphina looked at the documents again.

“Why was the order unusual?”

Roland turned to the final sheet.

“The original register lists the transfer of eighty measures from N-14, one hundred and twenty from N-09, and sixty from Southmere N-03. The total is two hundred and sixty measures.”

“Yes.”

“But the order does not name a final receiving officer. It directs the grain to a temporary Northern Consolidated Depot.”

“Which was on Bellweather land.”

“According to the old survey map, yes.”

“And the depot was dismantled afterward?”

“The lease records indicate that the temporary structures were removed. However, we have not found a complete handover document.”

Seraphina looked at Cedric.

“Was the land returned?”

“The lease states that it was to be returned in its original condition. A later administrative note says the temporary facilities were removed and the land restored.”

“But there is no acknowledgment from the landowner.”

“Correct.”

“Do we know whether the grain was distributed?”

Roland hesitated.

“We have references to emergency distributions from several villages. We do not yet have a complete schedule connecting those distributions to the depot.”

Seraphina leaned back.

“So we have grain transferred from three locations, a temporary depot, a missing final receiving schedule, incomplete distribution records, and a signature that may or may not have been signed in the capital.”

“That is a fair summary.”

“And nobody knows where the final inventory went.”

“Not yet.”

She stared at the ceiling.

“Why couldn't the original emergency have involved something simple? Like a snowstorm?”

Cedric answered.

“It did involve a snowstorm.”

Seraphina looked at him.

“Then why did it need so many documents?”

“Because the snowstorm affected several provinces, closed two major roads, damaged three bridges, and forced the crown to organize emergency food distribution.”

“Oh.”

She considered this.

“Fine. That is a reasonable excuse.”

Roland cleared his throat.

“There is another matter.”

Seraphina closed her eyes.

“Of course there is.”

“The capital has responded to our request for the old committee records. They have agreed to provide copies, but the archive requires a formal statement explaining the purpose of the inquiry.”

“Write that we are trying to determine what happened to two hundred and sixty measures of grain.”

Roland's expression remained unchanged.

“The archive requires a more specific administrative description.”

“Write that we are trying to determine what happened to two hundred and sixty measures of grain, but with more words.”

Cedric looked toward the window.

This time, his shoulders moved.

Roland carefully gathered the documents.

“I will prepare the statement.”

Seraphina stood.

“Thank you. I will return to doing something that does not involve grain.”

Neither man asked what that might be.

They were probably wise not to.

The first delivery under the revised procurement procedure took place that afternoon.

Seraphina had not intended to attend.

She had intended to spend the afternoon reviewing correspondence, perhaps walk in the garden, and possibly read something that did not mention storage facilities.

But Cedric informed her that Mistress Elinor Brant had requested her presence.

“She wants you to observe the first delivery,” he explained.

“Why?”

“Because you suggested the revised receiving procedure.”

“I suggested a procedure. I did not volunteer to supervise every bag of grain in the duchy.”

“She says the first delivery would be easier if the person who proposed the changes were present.”

Seraphina stared at him.

“That sounds suspiciously reasonable.”

Cedric smiled.

“Then you should go.”

“I dislike when people make sensible arguments.”

The receiving yard was busy when they arrived.

Two wagons stood near the covered storage building. Workers were unloading sacks while a clerk checked the delivery list.

Mistress Brant stood beside a wooden table with her ledger open.

She wore a dark travelling coat and had tied her hair back with a plain ribbon. Her expression suggested that she had already argued with three people before noon and expected to argue with at least four more.

When she saw Seraphina, she nodded.

“My lady.”

“Mistress Brant.”

“Thank you for coming.”

“I was told there would be grain.”

Brant blinked.

“There is.”

“Good. I was worried the meeting might be theoretical.”

Cedric turned away.

Mistress Brant studied Seraphina for a moment before gesturing toward the wagons.

“We have brought forty measures for the trial delivery. The first wagon carries twenty-four. The second carries sixteen.”

“Why two wagons?”

“Different loading points. The grain was gathered from two separate storage facilities.”

Seraphina nodded.

“Are the records separate?”

“Yes. Each has its own loading certificate.”

The receiving clerk approached.

“My lady, the first wagon is ready for weighing.”

Seraphina stepped aside.

The new procedure was simple.

The delivery certificate had to identify the source warehouse, the number of measures dispatched, the receiving location, and the person responsible for the count.

The receiving office would verify the quantity.

A representative of the supplier would witness the measurement.

If there was a difference, it would be recorded before the delivery was accepted.

No one would quietly adjust the figures later.

It was not a revolutionary system.

It was simply an attempt to make disagreements visible while everyone still remembered what had happened.

The first wagon was unloaded.

The receiving clerk counted the sacks.

Then he counted them again.

He frowned.

“Mistress Brant.”

“Yes?”

“The certificate lists twenty-four measures.”

“That is correct.”

“The receiving count is twenty-three and three-quarters.”

The yard became quiet.

Seraphina looked at the sacks.

Brant stepped closer.

“Which measurement method did you use?”

“The standard receiving measure.”

“And the weighing scale?”

“Calibrated this morning.”

Brant glanced at her ledger.

“Then I would like to see the record.”

The clerk passed it to her.

She examined the figures.

After a moment, she pointed to one line.

“You have included the weight of the empty sacks in the calculation.”

The clerk blinked.

“What?”

“The tare weight. These sacks are heavier than the ones used in the previous delivery.”

The clerk checked the figures.

His face changed.

Seraphina looked at him.

“Was the measurement adjusted?”

“No, my lady.”

“Then we have found the difference.”

The clerk looked embarrassed.

Brant did not look triumphant. She simply opened her ledger.

“This is why I asked for the measurement method to be written down.”

The clerk checked the figures again.

After correcting the tare weight, the delivery came to twenty-four measures.

The certificate was amended, and both parties signed it.

The second wagon was easier.

At the end of the delivery, Seraphina examined the completed forms.

“Does this create extra work?”

The receiving clerk hesitated.

“A little, my lady.”

“How much?”

“Perhaps fifteen minutes per delivery.”

“Can the form be simplified?”

The clerk looked at the page.

“The source warehouse and receiving location could be printed on the delivery schedule in advance. We would only need to record the count and any difference.”

Brant nodded.

“That would help us as well.”

Seraphina handed the document back.

“Then make that adjustment. The purpose is to prevent confusion, not create a new profession dedicated to filling out forms.”

The clerk looked relieved.

Brant closed her ledger.

“You see, my lady? The price of grain is not always the most expensive part of a delivery.”

Seraphina glanced at the corrected certificate.

“No. Sometimes it is the price of everyone assuming the other person has counted correctly.”

Brant gave a short laugh.

“That is an expensive habit.”

Seraphina watched the workers finish unloading.

For a moment, she thought of the old emergency programme.

Two hundred and sixty measures.

Three reserve locations.

One temporary depot.

No complete receiving schedule.

Perhaps the old clerks had made similar mistakes. Perhaps the records had been separated during the emergency. Perhaps the grain had been distributed, but the final reconciliation had never been completed.

Or perhaps something else had happened.

She reminded herself that a missing document was not proof of a missing delivery.

The difference mattered.

That evening, a second letter arrived from the capital.

This one was addressed to Duke Alaric.

Seraphina was in the eastern sitting room when her father entered with the envelope.

He did not look pleased.

“Have the archives responded?” she asked.

“Yes.”

He placed the letter on the table.

“They have located part of Lord Chancellor Harrow's travel register.”

Seraphina sat straighter.

“What does it say?”

Alaric opened the envelope.

“According to the register, Lord Chancellor Harrow was in the northern provinces during the first week of the winter emergency.”

Seraphina looked at him.

“Not in the capital.”

“No.”

“Then Martin was right?”

“He remembered the general period correctly.”

“Could Harrow have signed the order before travelling?”

“Certainly. The order may have been prepared earlier and delivered afterward.”

“Was there a record of that?”

“Not in the documents we have received.”

Seraphina frowned.

“What about a deputy?”

“The emergency committee had an appointed deputy during the chancellor's absence. We have not yet received the deputy's full authority register.”

“So there are still explanations.”

“Yes.”

Alaric sat opposite her.

“The archive also sent a copy of the committee minutes. The minutes record approval for an emergency transfer of grain to a temporary depot.”

“Two hundred and sixty measures?”

“The minutes do not specify the exact quantity. They authorize the regional offices to transfer available reserves according to need.”

“Who attended the meeting?”

“Lord Chancellor Harrow was not present. The deputy presided.”

Seraphina considered the information.

“Then the order may have been authorized by the deputy.”

“It may have been.”

“Does the signature belong to Harrow?”

“We do not yet know.”

Alaric placed another sheet on the table.

“This is the deputy's name.”

Seraphina read it.

**Sir Geoffrey Marlowe.**

She did not recognize it.

“Was he responsible for the emergency committee?”

“Temporarily. He was a senior official in the central treasury.”

“Is he still alive?”

“No. He died eight years ago.”

Seraphina sighed.

“Of course.”

Her father gave her a patient look.

“What?”

“Everyone involved in this appears to be dead.”

“Not everyone.”

“The documents?”

“Very much alive.”

“That is unfortunate.”

Alaric ignored the remark.

“The next step is to obtain the authority register and compare it with the original order.”

Seraphina nodded.

“Then we wait.”

“Yes.”

She looked at the letter again.

There was something almost comforting about the absence of a conclusion.

They had found another piece of the puzzle, but it did not yet tell them what the whole picture looked like.

She preferred that to a convenient accusation.

“Father?”

“Yes?”

“Do you think the grain reached the depot?”

Alaric was quiet for a moment.

“I think the records show that it was ordered to be transferred. I do not yet know whether every measure arrived.”

“And after that?”

“We need to establish what happened to it.”

Seraphina looked down at the letter.

Two hundred and sixty measures.

It was an old quantity, but it represented food that had been intended for people who had needed it.

Some of those people might still live in Westmere.

Some might remember the winter.

Some might remember receiving grain.

And some might remember receiving nothing.

“Could we ask the villages?” she said.

“We can. But we should be careful. Memories from twenty-six years ago will vary, and some of the people who were adults then may no longer be alive.”

“I know.”

“Cedric has already asked the regional offices to identify the villages included in the emergency distribution schedule.”

Seraphina looked up.

“Good.”

Alaric watched her.

“You seem less reluctant to be involved than you were a week ago.”

“I am still reluctant.”

“I see.”

“I simply dislike leaving questions unfinished.”

Her father smiled faintly.

“That is becoming clear.”

Seraphina frowned.

“That sounds like a criticism.”

“It is an observation.”

“Then I would prefer fewer observations.”

Alaric laughed quietly.

For a moment, the matter seemed smaller.

Not solved.

Not harmless.

But manageable.

Later that night, Seraphina sat at her desk with her notebook open.

She had begun writing down things she wanted to remember, partly because her previous-life memories were incomplete and partly because she had discovered that people could forget important details even when they had lived through them.

She wrote:

**Old Winter Emergency — Current Findings**

- N-14: 80 measures.
- N-09: 120 measures.
- Southmere N-03: 60 measures.
- Total: 260 measures.
- Transfer ordered to Northern Consolidated Depot.
- Depot located on temporarily leased Bellweather land.
- Temporary structures reportedly removed.
- Final receiving schedule not located.
- Complete distribution records not located.
- Lord Chancellor Harrow was in the northern provinces during the first week of the emergency.
- Sir Geoffrey Marlowe presided over the committee in his absence.
- Whether Harrow signed the order earlier, whether Marlowe authorized it, and who delivered it remain unconfirmed.

She paused.

Then added:

**Do not mistake an unexplained record for evidence of theft.**

She read the line twice.

It was an important distinction.

A person could lose a document without stealing anything.

An office could fail to reconcile its accounts without anyone deliberately falsifying them.

A warehouse could be emptied during an emergency and never properly recorded afterward.

But none of those possibilities meant the missing records were unimportant.

The people who had depended on those stores had deserved better.

Seraphina closed the notebook.

On the other side of her desk lay the invitation to the spring reception.

She picked it up.

The Royal Academy delegation would attend.

So would Saintess Liora Everwyn.

Seraphina looked at the invitation for several seconds.

She remembered the novel's broad outline.

The Academy.

The saintess.

The crown prince.

The magical research that would eventually lead to disaster.

But the memories were blurred around the edges. She could not remember which events had happened first, which characters had been involved, or how the catastrophe had begun.

She only remembered that the original Seraphina Veyr had been deeply involved in it.

And she remembered the ending.

Her own execution.

Seraphina placed the invitation back on the desk.

“I am going to be polite,” she told the empty room.

Mira, who was arranging the curtains nearby, glanced over.

“Of course, my lady.”

“I am going to speak to the guests.”

“Certainly.”

“I am not going to compete with the saintess.”

Mira nodded.

“That sounds sensible.”

“I am not going to interfere with the Royal Academy.”

“Very sensible.”

“And I am not going to investigate anything involving magical research.”

Mira looked at the invitation.

Then at Seraphina.

“My lady, you have not yet met the delegation.”

“I know.”

“You do not know what they will ask.”

“I know.”

“And the reception is in two days.”

Seraphina closed her eyes.

“I also know that.”

Mira finished arranging the curtains.

“Then perhaps you should sleep.”

Seraphina considered the suggestion.

It was the first sensible thing anyone had said all day.

She stood, changed into her nightclothes, and climbed into bed.

Just before Mira extinguished the lamp, Seraphina spoke again.

“Mira?”

“Yes, my lady?”

“If someone asks me about magical infrastructure, tell them I am interested in gardening.”

Mira paused.

“Do you know anything about gardening?”

“No.”

“Then perhaps I should tell them you are interested in the weather.”

Seraphina thought about it.

“That is better.”

Mira extinguished the lamp.

In the darkness, Seraphina listened to the quiet sounds of the estate.

The old winter emergency remained unresolved.

The missing grain remained unaccounted for.

The spring reception was approaching.

And somewhere in the royal capital, a delegation of scholars was preparing to visit House Veyr.

Seraphina pulled the blanket over her shoulder.

She had wanted a quiet life.

Apparently, the kingdom had other plans.

She hoped they would at least wait until after breakfast.
