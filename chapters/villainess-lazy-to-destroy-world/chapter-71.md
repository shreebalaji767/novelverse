# **Chapter 71 — The Road That Had Been Closed Before the Snow**

The first snow of the northern winter arrived three weeks earlier than expected.

It did not arrive dramatically.

There was no sudden white curtain over the mountains, no thunderous wind, and no convenient announcement from the heavens.

It began as a thin layer on the roofs of Aurelion's northern warehouses.

By morning, the roads beyond the capital had turned pale.

By noon, the Royal Northern Transport Office had issued its first notice.

**TEMPORARY RESTRICTIONS ON HEAVY CARRIAGE MOVEMENT**

Seraphina read it while eating a piece of toast.

Mira stood beside the breakfast table.

"The notice concerns the northern routes, my lady."

Seraphina looked at the page.

"Which routes?"

"The main road through the Veyr border district, the old western pass, and the lower mountain road."

"Are they closed?"

"Not entirely. Heavy wagons are restricted on two sections. The western pass is closed to all traffic until inspection."

Seraphina took another bite.

"Why?"

"The snow."

"That explains the weather. Not the road."

Mira consulted the notice.

"The transport office reports that the western retaining wall may have shifted."

Seraphina stopped eating.

"May have?"

"Yes."

"Has anyone inspected it?"

"The notice says an inspection is pending."

She looked at the document.

The western pass was one of the main routes used to move grain, timber, salt, and mining supplies between the northern settlements and the capital.

If it remained closed, the alternate route would add nearly two days to the journey.

Two days was not an inconvenience when the cargo consisted of decorative furniture.

It was an inconvenience when the cargo consisted of flour.

Seraphina placed the toast down.

"Send this to Cedric."

Mira nodded.

"Should I ask him to arrange an inspection?"

"No."

Mira paused.

Seraphina continued.

"Ask him whether the estate has any current information about the route. We are not the transport office."

Mira smiled faintly.

"Very good, my lady."

Seraphina picked up the notice again.

She had spent the previous three weeks dealing with a hearing about historical records.

She had no intention of becoming responsible for a road merely because someone had placed a notice on her breakfast table.

Unfortunately, the road passed through Veyr territory.

And the snow was falling.

Cedric arrived before lunch.

He carried the transport notice, a regional map, and a letter from the northern grain merchants.

Seraphina looked at the letter.

"That was quick."

"The merchants received the closure notice yesterday."

"Why did they write to us?"

"Because the alternate route passes through the eastern Veyr district."

She unfolded the map.

The western pass connected the capital road to the northern mining settlements.

The eastern route passed through Westmere, crossed the old stone bridge, and continued toward the northern granaries.

It was longer.

It was also narrower.

Heavy wagons could use it, but only if the bridge and two steep sections were suitable.

Seraphina examined the route.

"Has the eastern road been inspected?"

"Not recently."

"How recently?"

Cedric opened his notes.

"The last full inspection was four years ago."

"Four years?"

"The road has received ordinary maintenance since then."

"That is not the same thing."

"No."

Seraphina looked at the map.

The road had been used for grain deliveries during the previous winter.

It had also carried timber wagons in spring.

But no one had checked whether the bridge could support the heavier traffic now being redirected from the western pass.

"How many wagons are expected?"

Cedric placed another sheet on the table.

"According to the merchants, approximately forty-two over the next six days."

Seraphina stared at the number.

"Forty-two?"

"That is their estimate."

"How many can the road safely carry?"

"We do not know."

She leaned back.

This was the kind of question she disliked.

Not because it was complicated.

Because the answer required someone who knew more than she did.

"Ask Master Armitage to inspect the bridge and the two steep sections."

Cedric nodded.

"Should he inspect the whole road?"

"No. The road is not ours in its entirety. The regional authority maintains the northern section."

"Then we will ask the authority to inspect its section."

"Good."

Cedric looked at her.

"Would you like to review the inspection report?"

"Yes."

"Should we contact the merchants?"

"Ask them to provide the expected wagon weights and cargo."

Cedric smiled.

"That sounds sensible."

Seraphina gave him a flat look.

"It is a bridge, Cedric. I would prefer it to remain one."

The first report arrived two days later.

Master Colin Armitage had inspected the Veyr-owned section of the eastern route with a regional road officer.

The bridge was structurally sound.

Its stone supports showed no new movement.

The timber deck, however, had been repaired repeatedly.

Several boards were worn.

The eastern approach had softened beneath the recent rain and snow.

The bridge could carry ordinary grain wagons.

It could not safely accommodate the heavier mining carts without additional restrictions.

The report recommended:

- Replacing twelve damaged deck boards.
- Reinforcing the eastern approach.
- Restricting heavy mining carts.
- Requiring wagons to cross individually.
- Inspecting the bridge again after the next major snowfall.

Seraphina read the report.

"How long will the repairs take?"

Cedric checked the estimate.

"Two days, if the timber is available."

"Is it?"

"The estate has suitable boards in the maintenance store."

"Then arrange the repairs."

Cedric nodded.

"The regional officer has also requested permission to place a temporary weight-limit notice near the bridge."

"Of course."

Seraphina signed the approval.

The bridge would remain open.

The heavier carts would be diverted.

The grain wagons could proceed.

It was not a perfect solution.

But it was better than closing the entire route.

She returned the report.

"Now we need to know whether the road beyond the bridge can handle the traffic."

Cedric looked at her.

"That is the regional authority's responsibility."

"Then ask them."

He laughed.

Seraphina did not.

She was becoming very tired of roads.

The next letter came from Westmere.

It was written by Alden Mercer, the village elder.

The grain merchants had begun arriving.

The first six wagons had crossed the bridge safely.

The remaining wagons were expected over the next several days.

However, the village's temporary storage yard was already full.

The merchants had been told that the western pass would reopen within forty-eight hours.

It had not.

They were now asking whether the estate could provide additional storage.

Seraphina read the letter.

"How much grain?"

Cedric consulted the attached schedule.

"Approximately three hundred measures."

"How much can the village store?"

"One hundred and twenty measures in the existing yard, assuming the old shed is cleared."

"Is it clear?"

"No."

Seraphina looked at him.

"What is in it?"

"Timber, two broken carts, and a collection of equipment belonging to the road maintenance crew."

"Why is road equipment stored in a grain shed?"

Cedric considered the question.

"I suspect it was convenient."

Seraphina closed her eyes.

Convenience was the most dangerous word in the kingdom.

It had apparently been responsible for several generations of people placing things wherever they happened to find space.

"Have the equipment moved to the maintenance yard."

Cedric nodded.

"And the broken carts?"

"Ask their owners to collect them."

"What if they cannot?"

"Then record them and arrange disposal after the owners have been notified."

Cedric wrote the instructions.

Seraphina continued.

"The shed should be inspected for dampness before grain is stored."

"Yes."

"Do not assume that an empty shed is a suitable grain store."

Cedric smiled.

"I will make that clear."

The estate's storage manager, Robert Hale, arrived the following morning.

He explained that the old shed had been used for temporary grain storage during the previous winter.

It had not been used since.

The roof was intact.

The floor was dry.

The walls, however, had several gaps near the western corner.

The shed could store grain if the gaps were repaired and the floor raised slightly.

The work would take one day.

Seraphina approved it.

She also asked that the grain be stored in separate lots.

The merchants had different owners.

Their cargo should not be combined merely because it occupied the same building.

Robert Hale agreed.

He had already prepared a temporary register.

It recorded the merchant, wagon number, quantity received, condition, and intended destination.

Seraphina examined it.

"Good."

Robert looked relieved.

"Thank you, my lady."

She returned the register.

"Do not thank me. Thank the person who decided to write down the wagon numbers."

Robert smiled.

"I will."

The western pass remained closed.

The transport office issued a second notice.

The retaining wall had shifted more than expected.

The inspection team found a crack in the upper masonry and several stones displaced near the drainage channel.

The road could not reopen until the wall was stabilized.

The repair would require timber supports, stonework, and a temporary diversion around the damaged section.

The notice estimated eight days.

Seraphina read it.

"Eight days."

Cedric nodded.

"At least."

"How many wagons are waiting?"

"Twenty-seven."

"Where?"

"Mostly at the northern junction. Some have been redirected to Westmere."

She looked at the map.

The eastern route could handle the grain wagons.

It could not handle the mining carts.

The western pass would remain closed.

The northern settlements would have to use the alternate road for at least another week.

The question was no longer whether the road could carry the traffic.

It was whether the traffic could be organized so that the road did not fail under it.

Seraphina stood.

"Send a message to the regional transport office."

Cedric waited.

"Ask them to establish a temporary schedule for the eastern route."

"Would you like the estate to prepare it?"

"No. The transport office should prepare it."

"Then what should we do?"

"Provide the bridge's weight limits and the estate's inspection report."

Cedric nodded.

"And ask the merchants to submit their wagon weights."

"Yes."

Seraphina paused.

"Also ask whether the northern settlements have enough storage for the redirected grain."

Cedric looked at her.

"That is a separate issue."

"Yes."

"Should we investigate it?"

"No. We should ask the regional office whether it has already been investigated."

Cedric smiled.

"That is a distinction I am learning to appreciate."

The temporary schedule was issued three days later.

Grain wagons would cross the eastern bridge in groups of six.

Heavy mining carts would remain on the northern road until the western pass reopened.

The regional transport office would place an officer at the junction to direct traffic.

The estate would provide the bridge inspection report and maintain the temporary weight-limit notice.

Westmere's storage shed would hold up to three hundred measures of grain.

The arrangement was not elegant.

It was not particularly efficient.

But it prevented the merchants from crowding the bridge and kept the grain moving.

Seraphina read the schedule.

"Who decided the six-wagon limit?"

Cedric checked the note.

"The regional engineer."

"Good."

"Would you like to review it?"

"No."

Cedric blinked.

Seraphina continued.

"The engineer knows the road. I know the estate's records. We have provided the information he needs."

Cedric looked pleased.

It was a small victory.

Not for the road.

For the boundaries.

The first complication came from a merchant named Dorian Pell.

He had arrived in Westmere with four wagons of salt and a fifth wagon carrying mining equipment.

The salt was permitted on the eastern route.

The mining equipment was not.

Pell objected.

He claimed that the equipment was lighter than the ordinary mining carts and should be allowed across.

The regional officer refused.

Pell then asked the estate to intervene.

Seraphina received his letter.

It contained three pages of argument.

She read the first page.

Then the second.

Then she stopped.

"Does he provide the weight?"

Cedric checked.

"No."

"Does he provide the dimensions?"

"No."

"Does he provide the engineer's approval?"

"No."

Seraphina placed the letter down.

"Then the answer is no."

Cedric nodded.

"Should we explain?"

"Yes. Tell him the restriction applies to the class of equipment unless the regional engineer approves an exception."

Cedric wrote it down.

Seraphina continued.

"Do not say the estate refuses permission. We do not control the route."

"Understood."

Pell's reply arrived the following day.

He had obtained a weight certificate from the manufacturer.

The equipment was lighter than the carts normally used by the mines.

The regional engineer reviewed the certificate and approved a limited exception.

Pell's wagon crossed the bridge.

It was an entirely ordinary resolution.

No one had needed to threaten anyone.

No one had needed to invoke the Crown.

The engineer had simply received the relevant information.

Seraphina considered this a pleasant change.

The grain deliveries continued.

The storage shed filled.

The bridge remained open.

The western pass repairs progressed slowly because the snow had frozen the upper drainage channel.

The transport office revised its reopening estimate.

Twelve days.

Seraphina read the notice.

"That is longer than eight."

Cedric nodded.

"The repair crew found additional damage."

"How much?"

"One section of the retaining wall requires rebuilding."

Seraphina looked at the map.

The road was not merely delayed.

It was damaged.

The northern settlements would remain dependent on the eastern route for nearly two weeks.

She turned to Cedric.

"Have the merchants confirmed their deliveries?"

"Yes."

"Have the villages confirmed their storage?"

"Most have."

"Most?"

Cedric opened the report.

"Two settlements have not responded."

Seraphina frowned.

"Which?"

"Greyford and Alder's Crossing."

The names were familiar.

Both had been involved in the old grain reserve records.

Seraphina did not assume that the current problem had anything to do with the old one.

But the settlements were now facing a practical difficulty.

She looked at the report.

"Send a messenger."

Cedric nodded.

"Ask them to confirm their current stores, expected deliveries, and whether they need assistance."

"Yes."

"And do not use the old reserve figures."

Cedric looked at her.

"Of course."

"Those figures are historical. We need current quantities."

He smiled.

"I will make sure the distinction is clear."

The reply from Greyford arrived first.

The settlement had enough grain for its immediate needs.

However, the mill had stopped operating because the waterwheel's lower bearing had cracked.

The mill could still grind grain slowly using a smaller auxiliary wheel.

It was not enough to meet the settlement's normal demand.

A repair was possible.

The bearing had to be replaced.

The nearest suitable replacement was stored in the regional maintenance depot.

The depot had not yet released it because the request lacked a current inspection certificate.

Seraphina read the letter.

She looked at Cedric.

"Why does every useful object in this kingdom require three signatures before it can move?"

Cedric sighed.

"I do not know."

"Has the mill been inspected?"

"Yes."

"By whom?"

"Master Edmund Pike."

"Then send the inspection certificate to the depot."

Cedric nodded.

"And ask whether the bearing can be released under the existing maintenance authority."

"Yes."

Seraphina paused.

"Do not ask the estate to purchase it until we know whether the depot already owns it."

Cedric wrote the instruction.

The bearing was released the following day.

The mill repair began.

Greyford would be able to grind grain again within four days.

It was not a dramatic rescue.

It was a cracked bearing.

But the settlement needed flour.

Alder's Crossing had a different problem.

Its grain stores were sufficient.

Its road, however, had become difficult to use.

The village bridge crossed a narrow stream.

The recent snow had melted during the afternoon and frozen again overnight.

The bridge's timber surface had become slippery.

The village had restricted heavy carts.

The grain merchants were waiting.

The regional office had not yet sent an inspection team.

Seraphina examined the report.

The bridge was not owned by House Veyr.

It belonged to the regional authority.

The estate had no responsibility for its maintenance.

But the road connected the northern settlements to the eastern route.

If the bridge remained unusable, the grain deliveries would be delayed.

Seraphina looked at Cedric.

"Can Master Armitage inspect it?"

"Yes, but he cannot authorize repairs."

"Then ask him to inspect it and send the report to the regional authority."

Cedric nodded.

"Should we provide temporary materials?"

"No. The authority should decide."

She considered the report.

"However, if the bridge is unsafe, the village should not be told to wait for the paperwork."

Cedric looked at her.

"Do you want us to warn them?"

"Yes. Ask the village elder to restrict heavy traffic until the inspection is complete."

"That is reasonable."

The message was sent.

The village restricted the bridge.

The grain wagons were redirected through a longer track.

The inspection took place the following morning.

The bridge was sound.

Its surface needed scraping and the addition of rough timber strips.

The regional authority authorized the work.

The wagons crossed safely the next day.

Seraphina read the confirmation.

She put it aside.

Then she returned to her accounts.

The road crisis was not over.

But the grain was moving.

By the end of the second week, the western pass had reopened.

The retaining wall had been rebuilt.

The drainage channel had been cleared.

The road inspector approved limited traffic.

Heavy wagons would be allowed through in controlled groups.

The eastern route returned to ordinary use.

The grain merchants completed their deliveries.

Westmere's temporary storage shed was emptied.

The equipment was returned to the maintenance yard.

The broken carts were collected.

The bridge's repaired boards remained in place.

Seraphina received the final report.

It contained a list of expenses.

The estate had paid for timber, labor, and the temporary storage arrangements.

The regional authority had paid for its own road repairs.

The merchants had paid for the additional transport costs.

The villages had contributed labor where they could.

No single institution had carried the entire burden.

Seraphina read the final paragraph.

*The temporary arrangement is considered concluded. A review of the eastern route's winter capacity is recommended before the next season.*

She nodded.

That was sensible.

She did not intend to attend the review.

Cedric could represent the estate.

The regional engineer could inspect the road.

The merchants could provide their traffic figures.

The village elders could report their needs.

And Seraphina could remain at home.

The next morning, Mira brought her a small package.

"From Westmere, my lady."

Seraphina opened it.

Inside was a loaf of bread.

It was rough, dark, and slightly uneven.

A note accompanied it.

*The first flour ground after the bearing was replaced. Greyford sends its thanks.*

Seraphina looked at the bread.

Mira smiled.

"That is very kind."

Seraphina broke off a piece.

It was warm.

She ate it.

Then she looked at the note again.

The old grain reserve records had once contained missing quantities, uncertain receipts, and unexplained transfers.

This winter, the records had been useful.

Not because they had solved the problem.

Because the estate had kept current information separate from old figures.

Because the bridge had been inspected before the heavy wagons crossed.

Because the mill bearing had been released after the proper certificate was provided.

Because the village had been warned before the road became unsafe.

No single action had been extraordinary.

Together, they had kept the grain moving.

Seraphina finished the bread.

Mira waited.

"Would you like another piece?"

Seraphina looked at the remaining loaf.

"Yes."

Mira cut another slice.

Outside, the snow continued to fall.

The northern roads were open again.

The mills were working.

The stores were being replenished.

And for the first time in several weeks, Seraphina had no immediate reason to examine a historical document.

She leaned back in her chair.

Then Cedric entered.

He carried a new report.

Seraphina looked at it.

"What is it?"

"The western military road."

She stared at him.

"Is it broken?"

Cedric glanced at the report.

"Not exactly."

Seraphina closed her eyes.

The kingdom had apparently decided that winter was a suitable time to discover every road it had neglected.

And she had the unpleasant feeling that the next one would not be nearly as simple as a cracked mill bearing.
