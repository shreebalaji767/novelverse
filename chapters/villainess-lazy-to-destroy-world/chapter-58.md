# Chapter 58 — The Fence That Everyone Thought Someone Else Owned

The orchard fence inspection produced a list of eleven problems.

Seraphina had expected six.

She considered the additional five an unnecessary display of enthusiasm.

Thomas Barlow presented the results in the garden office on Monday morning. He had brought a rough sketch of the orchard, a list of damaged sections, and a small wooden box containing samples of rotten fence posts.

The samples were arranged from least damaged to most damaged.

Seraphina examined them.

"Why have you brought me pieces of wood?"

"To show you the condition of the posts, my lady."

"I can see that they are rotten."

"These are not all equally rotten."

She looked at the samples again.

The first was slightly discolored.

The second had a split near its base.

The third crumbled when she pressed it.

The fourth had apparently been included because Barlow considered it personally offensive.

"Which ones need immediate replacement?"

"Four."

"Which ones can wait?"

"Three, if we reinforce them."

"And the remaining four?"

"Those are not rotten. They are leaning because the ground has softened around them."

Seraphina nodded.

"Then the list is useful."

Barlow looked pleased.

"The fence can be repaired over the next two weeks without disrupting the orchard work."

"Good. Who will do it?"

"Our own workers can handle most of it. The western stretch needs a carpenter."

"Arrange one."

Barlow placed another sheet on the desk.

"There is one more matter."

Seraphina looked at him.

There was always one more matter.

"The southern fence."

"What about it?"

"It belongs partly to the orchard and partly to the lower pasture."

Seraphina paused.

"Partly?"

"The fence follows the old boundary for most of its length. But near the stream, it turns toward the pasture."

"Why?"

"Because the stream changed course."

Seraphina looked at the sketch.

The fence ran along the southern edge of the orchard before bending around a small strip of grass.

The strip was used by the pasture workers to move livestock toward the water.

It was not part of the orchard.

It was not entirely part of the pasture either.

"Who maintains that section?"

Barlow hesitated.

"The gardeners repair the orchard side."

"And the pasture workers?"

"They repair the lower side."

"Who pays for the posts?"

"That depends."

Seraphina set down the sketch.

"On what?"

"On who notices the damage first."

She stared at him.

Barlow looked apologetic.

"It has worked so far."

Seraphina slowly closed the wooden box.

She had begun to dislike that sentence.

Cedric was called to the garden office.

He examined the sketch, then the old estate map.

The southern fence appeared on both documents.

The boundary, however, did not follow the fence.

It followed the stream.

The stream had moved.

Not dramatically. Not enough to alter the general shape of the land. But its southern bank had shifted over several decades, leaving the fence on a different line from the one shown on the original map.

Cedric studied the measurements.

"How long has this been happening?"

Barlow shrugged.

"Longer than I have worked here."

"Has anyone surveyed it?"

"Not recently."

Seraphina looked at the map.

"Does the fence define the boundary?"

"No," Cedric replied. "The stream does."

"Then the fence is not a boundary marker."

"Correct."

"Who owns the strip between the old fence and the new streambank?"

Cedric examined the title record.

"House Veyr."

"All of it?"

"According to the land register."

Barlow looked surprised.

"Then the pasture workers have been using Veyr land?"

"With permission," Cedric said.

Seraphina considered the arrangement.

The land belonged to House Veyr.

The gardeners maintained one side of the fence.

The pasture workers maintained the other.

The stream was used by both groups.

The fence served two different purposes.

There was no dispute.

There was simply no clear record of who was responsible for maintaining the entire structure.

"Is the fence needed?" she asked.

Barlow nodded.

"Without it, livestock could enter the orchard."

"Can we move it?"

"Possibly. But the pasture workers need access to the stream."

Seraphina looked at Cedric.

"Then we should not move it until we understand how the pasture is used."

Cedric agreed.

He arranged for a joint inspection with Barlow and the pasture supervisor.

The inspection took place the following afternoon.

The pasture supervisor, Martin Cole, arrived with a map of the livestock routes.

He explained that the southern strip was used to move cattle toward the stream during dry weather.

The fence prevented animals from entering the orchard while allowing them access to the water.

A small gate had been installed near the bend.

The gate was old.

Its hinges were rusted.

Its latch could be opened from either side.

It had also been repaired with a piece of rope.

Seraphina looked at it.

"Is that rope permanent?"

Martin Cole shook his head.

"No, my lady."

"How long has it been there?"

"Three years."

She looked at Cedric.

He looked at the gate.

Neither spoke.

Barlow cleared his throat.

"The original latch broke during the spring floods. We intended to replace it."

"Why was it not replaced?"

"The carpenter was occupied with the western stable."

Seraphina turned toward the gate.

"Can the livestock open it?"

"Not usually."

"Usually?"

"One particularly stubborn cow has managed it twice."

Seraphina recognized the beginning of a pattern.

"Is it the same cow that entered the orchard?"

"No, my lady."

"How many cows are there?"

"One hundred and twelve."

She decided not to ask whether all of them were stubborn.

Master Hobb was called to inspect the gate.

He found that the post had begun to rot at ground level. The hinges could be reused, but the latch needed replacement.

He also recommended raising the lower fence rail in two places where water collected after rain.

The repairs were straightforward.

The more difficult question concerned the streambank.

The current had undercut a small section near the bend.

The bank was not in immediate danger of collapsing, but the soil was soft.

Master Hobb recommended reinforcing it with stone and planting suitable vegetation.

"Would the fence need to be moved?" Seraphina asked.

"No," he replied. "Not if the bank is stabilized."

"Would the repair affect the stream?"

"Only locally. We should avoid narrowing the channel."

Seraphina nodded.

"Then proceed with the bank repair and gate replacement."

Cedric wrote down the instructions.

"And the maintenance responsibility?" he asked.

Seraphina looked at the fence.

"House Veyr owns the land. The orchard and pasture both benefit from the fence. The gardeners and pasture workers can continue handling ordinary repairs, but the estate should be responsible for the structure."

"That would simplify the arrangement."

"Record it."

The repairs began three days later.

The carpenter replaced the gate latch.

The rotten post was removed.

The lower fence rails were raised.

The streambank was reinforced with carefully placed stone.

The workers also cleared the small drainage channel beside the fence, which had become blocked by fallen leaves and accumulated soil.

The work took four days.

The orchard remained protected.

The cattle continued to reach the stream.

The fence no longer depended on a piece of rope and the hope that no animal would discover how gates worked.

Seraphina visited the site once.

She found Martin Cole watching the cattle drink.

"Is everything working?"

"Yes, my lady."

"Any problems?"

"One cow dislikes the new gate."

"Does the cow have a reason?"

Martin looked thoughtful.

"It preferred the old one."

Seraphina considered this.

"Then the cow may submit a complaint."

Martin laughed.

The fence was finished.

The matter was closed.

The following week, Cedric brought Seraphina a report about the orchard's remaining boundary arrangements.

The southern fence had been repaired.

The western wall had been inspected.

The northern boundary was marked by old stone posts.

The eastern boundary followed the road.

The streambank was stable.

The report also included a note about a narrow strip of land beside the eastern road.

The strip was used by the gardeners to store pruning equipment.

It had once been part of a larger service yard.

The service yard had been divided decades earlier when the road was widened.

The land register showed that the strip belonged to House Veyr.

The road register showed that it was maintained by the regional road authority.

The gardeners had been using it without a formal agreement.

Seraphina read the note.

"Why does the road authority maintain our land?"

Cedric opened the supporting documents.

"Because the strip was included in the road maintenance boundary after the widening."

"Was ownership transferred?"

"No."

"Was an access agreement signed?"

"Not that I can find."

"Then why is the road authority maintaining it?"

Cedric looked at the record.

"The maintenance schedule says the strip is used for road drainage."

Seraphina frowned.

"Is it?"

"Apparently."

"Then the estate should not store equipment there."

Cedric nodded.

"I will have the gardeners move it."

"Not until we inspect the drainage."

Cedric looked up.

"Why?"

"Because if the authority has been maintaining it as part of the road drainage system, moving the equipment is only the first step. We need to know what the land is doing."

Cedric closed the folder.

"I will arrange a survey."

Seraphina looked toward the window.

The estate's boundaries were beginning to resemble a collection of old conversations that had never been properly finished.

She wondered whether the royal capital had similar problems.

Then she decided that she did not want to know.

The survey revealed that the strip served an important purpose.

It carried rainwater away from the eastern road and into a drainage ditch that eventually joined the lower stream.

The road authority had been clearing it for years.

House Veyr had retained ownership.

The gardeners had used it because it was convenient.

No one had objected because the equipment did not obstruct the drainage.

The arrangement had worked.

But the road authority's records described the strip as public drainage land, while the estate's records described it as private service land.

The difference mattered because the authority was planning to repair the road's eastern drainage system.

The proposed work included widening the ditch.

The widening would require temporary access to the strip.

The road authority had assumed it could proceed under its maintenance schedule.

House Veyr had never formally granted that access.

Cedric looked at Seraphina.

"We need an agreement."

"Yes."

"Would you like to negotiate it?"

"No. You may."

He smiled.

"I thought so."

Seraphina examined the survey.

The estate could grant temporary and recurring maintenance access without transferring ownership.

The authority would be responsible for the drainage works.

House Veyr would retain the right to inspect the land and receive notice before major alterations.

The gardeners would be prohibited from storing equipment in the drainage area.

A narrow section could remain available for estate use, provided it did not obstruct water flow.

It was a simple arrangement.

Cedric prepared the proposal.

The road authority accepted it.

The agreement was signed two weeks later.

The strip remained House Veyr property.

The drainage system remained under the authority's maintenance.

The gardeners moved their tools to the service yard.

No one lost land.

No one gained unexpected responsibilities.

The rainwater continued to flow where it was supposed to.

Seraphina considered this an excellent outcome.

At dinner, Alaric asked about the latest inspection.

Seraphina explained the fence, the streambank, and the road drainage strip.

He listened carefully.

When she finished, he said, "The old records are becoming more complicated than I expected."

"They were always complicated."

"Perhaps."

Evelyne placed a serving dish on the table.

"The question is whether they were complicated when they were written or became complicated afterward."

Seraphina considered that.

"Both."

Alaric nodded.

"Your great-grandfather widened the eastern road. Your grandfather approved the drainage work. The gardeners used the remaining strip. The road authority maintained it."

"And no one thought to record the access arrangement."

"Apparently not."

Seraphina took some vegetables.

"At least the land register was correct."

"That is something."

She looked at him.

"Why did the estate not review these arrangements earlier?"

Alaric did not answer immediately.

"Because the estate had other priorities. The road needed repairs. The orchard needed attention. The pasture needed fencing. People did the work and moved on."

"That does not make it a good system."

"No," he agreed. "But it explains how it survived."

Evelyne smiled.

"Your father is trying to say that people often choose the solution that works today, even when it creates a question for tomorrow."

Seraphina looked at the table.

"Tomorrow seems to have a great many questions."

Alaric laughed.

"That is why we have administrators."

Cedric, seated opposite her, raised his glass.

Seraphina ignored him.

The estate review continued.

The regional office had received the shared-property format.

Two neighboring estates had begun their own inspections.

Master Armitage had accepted a private survey assignment for one of them.

The Veyr register had been updated.

The orchard fence was repaired.

The streambank was stable.

The eastern drainage strip now had a written access arrangement.

For a brief period, Seraphina believed that the work might settle into a manageable routine.

Then Cedric arrived with a new report.

It concerned the old southern drainage channel.

The same channel that had been inspected during the shared-property review.

The channel had been cleared.

The banks had been repaired.

The water was flowing properly.

However, the downstream property owner had reported that the channel was carrying more water than before.

Cedric placed the report on her desk.

Seraphina read it.

"Is that possible?"

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because the upper section was cleared."

She looked at him.

"Was it blocked before?"

"Partly."

"And now it is not."

"Correct."

Seraphina put the report down.

"Then the water is doing what water does."

Cedric nodded.

"But the downstream owner is concerned about flooding."

Seraphina sighed.

The channel had been cleared to prevent flooding.

It had apparently succeeded in moving the problem farther along.

She stood.

"Arrange a survey of the entire channel."

Cedric looked at the report.

"The entire channel?"

"Yes. We should have done that before clearing the upper section."

Cedric hesitated.

"That was not included in the original work."

"I know."

She picked up her cloak.

"Let's find out where the water is going."

Outside, the afternoon sky had begun to darken.

Seraphina looked toward the western fields.

The estate had spent weeks correcting old agreements and repairing neglected structures.

Now the water had decided to remind them that a channel did not end simply because a document did.

She began to suspect that rivers were even more difficult than families.

And families, in her experience, were already difficult enough.
