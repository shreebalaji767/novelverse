# **Chapter 35 — The Man Who Received the Equipment**

The name was written in blue ink.

A. Mercer.

Seraphina examined the transfer record for the fourth time.

The document was not particularly impressive. It contained a date, a reference number, a brief equipment description and two signatures.

The first belonged to Robert Fenwick, the receiving clerk at Central Stores.

The second belonged to A. Mercer, who had apparently received the equipment at Western Relay Station W-2.

Three regulating assemblies.

Two mounting frames.

Workshop tools.

Unclassified metal components.

The date was three months after the original transfer.

Seraphina placed the record beside the earlier documents.

The chain was becoming clearer.

Veylan Works had supplied equipment.

The regional office had transferred it to Central Stores.

Central Stores had later dispatched it to W-2.

Someone at W-2 had signed for it.

The remaining question was what happened after that.

"Is there anything else, my lady?"

Mira stood beside the desk, holding a tray.

Seraphina looked at her.

"Tea?"

"Yes."

"Thank you."

Mira placed the tray down.

"Does the new document solve the problem?"

"No."

Mira looked disappointed.

"It establishes that the equipment reached the station," Seraphina explained. "But we still don't know whether it was installed, stored, inspected or moved again."

"Then the person who signed for it might know."

"Exactly."

Mira glanced at the signature.

"Mercer. Is that a common name?"

"Common enough to make searching inconvenient."

Seraphina took a sip of tea.

She had begun to suspect that the kingdom's archives were populated by people who had spent their entire working lives making sure future generations could not identify them.

The first task was to establish the identity of A. Mercer.

Seraphina wrote to Adrian Holt, requesting that the Royal Infrastructure Department search its personnel registers for anyone assigned to W-2 under that name.

She also requested the original delivery record.

A copy was useful.

An original was better.

The difference mattered because the surviving document did not identify the signatory's position.

A. Mercer could have been a station supervisor, a maintenance worker, a regional clerk or an authorized representative.

The signature alone did not establish responsibility for the equipment.

Seraphina included that point in her letter.

Then she wrote to Roland.

He was to search the surviving Veyr regional employment records for anyone named Mercer who had worked in engineering, maintenance or stores administration.

She did not ask him to search every Mercer in the duchy.

She had no intention of turning an ordinary name into a family-wide investigation.

The reply from Aurelion arrived first.

Holt had found an old personnel index.

It listed three employees named Mercer.

One had worked in the Royal Logistics Department.

One had been a temporary clerk at the former Regional Engineering Office.

The third had been assigned to the western maintenance programme.

His full name was **Alden Mercer**.

His position was listed as regional maintenance supervisor.

His assignment began two years before the equipment delivery and ended the following year.

Seraphina read the record.

"That is promising."

Cedric, who had come to discuss the estate's winter transport budget, examined the page.

"Is he still employed?"

"No. His assignment ended twenty years ago."

"Retired?"

"Possibly."

"Do we know where he went?"

"Not yet."

Cedric looked at the personnel entry.

"Regional maintenance supervisor. That would explain why he received the equipment."

"It would explain why he was authorized to receive it."

"Is there a difference?"

"Yes. He might have signed for the delivery without inspecting the contents or supervising the installation."

Cedric returned the page.

"You have become very particular about signatures."

"Signatures are useful. They are not magical."

Cedric smiled.

"That sounds like something Master Whitlock would say."

Seraphina looked at the document again.

"Perhaps I am becoming difficult."

"Perhaps."

Roland's search produced a more useful result.

Alden Mercer had been the maintenance supervisor for Westmere and the surrounding districts.

After the regional engineering office was reorganized, he had returned to his home village.

He had later served as a village elder.

The property register listed him as the owner of a small farm near the old junction road.

He was still alive.

Seraphina read the report.

"Can we establish whether this is the same man?"

"The dates and occupation match."

"Does the property register include his age?"

"Yes. He would have been in his thirties when the equipment was delivered."

"That is plausible."

Roland waited.

"Would you like me to contact him?"

"Not yet. First, ask the Department whether the personnel record and delivery signature refer to the same individual."

Roland nodded.

Seraphina added another instruction.

"And please make sure nobody approaches him as though he is suspected of anything."

"Of course."

The committee's next meeting began with an update from Lucien.

The Academy had examined the equipment categories listed in the transfer record.

The three regulating assemblies were consistent with the components required for the western station's circuit.

The mounting frames were also compatible.

However, the description was not sufficiently detailed to establish the exact models.

"Could they have been used at another station?" Seraphina asked.

Lucien considered the question.

"Yes. The equipment was not unique to W-2."

"Then the delivery record is more important than the technical description."

"Agreed."

Professor Vale turned a page.

"The original station inventory may identify the components."

"Have you found it?" Theodore asked.

"Not yet. We have located a reference to an inventory prepared before the final inspection."

"Where?"

"An engineering register."

"Is the register available?"

Professor Vale shook his head.

"Only the index."

Seraphina looked at the reference number.

"Could the inventory have been attached to the original delivery record?"

"Possibly," Lucien said. "But the surviving copy contains no attachment notation."

Theodore turned toward Holt.

"Request the original."

Holt made a note.

Minister Garrick looked at the papers.

"How many original documents are we going to request?"

Seraphina answered before anyone else could.

"As many as are necessary to distinguish what happened from what people remember happened."

Garrick looked at her.

"That is an expensive habit."

"Cheaper than making decisions based on incomplete records."

Cedric lowered his eyes.

He was smiling again.

The committee then reviewed the current status of the six facilities.

The western station remained the most promising source of information.

The northern station had been confirmed as an existing structure, but its equipment ownership remained unresolved.

The eastern station was still represented only by an old catalogue reference.

The southern storehouse had been identified as a regional circuit depot, but its exact relationship to the programme was unconfirmed.

The Ashenford station remained unverified.

The sixth project allocation still had no confirmed location.

Theodore studied the table.

"We are spending considerable time on W-2."

"Because it has the clearest documentary trail," Seraphina replied.

"Should we divide the investigation?"

"Eventually. But not simply to make the table look balanced."

Lucien nodded.

"Some sites may have fewer surviving records than others."

"Then we should record that accurately."

Theodore looked toward Holt.

"Prepare separate research schedules for each facility. W-2 can continue while the others are examined."

Holt agreed.

The meeting moved on.

Alden Mercer's response arrived nine days later.

He had received the Department's letter.

He was willing to meet a representative, but he wanted to know why his name had appeared in the inquiry.

Seraphina read the response twice.

It was reasonable.

A man who had worked in a regional office two decades earlier might be concerned to receive an official letter about an old engineering programme.

She asked Holt to explain that the committee was reconstructing the administrative history of the six facilities and that his signature appeared on a surviving equipment delivery record.

No accusation was being made.

No personal liability was being suggested.

The Department would like to ask whether he remembered the delivery.

Alden Mercer agreed.

The meeting was scheduled for the following week in Westmere.

Seraphina decided to attend.

Not because she wanted to take over the investigation.

Because she had read enough old records to understand that a document could be accurate and still fail to explain the circumstances surrounding it.

A person might provide the missing context.

Or simply confirm that the document was all that remained.

Westmere had changed since Seraphina's earlier visit.

The main road had been repaired.

The grain storehouse had received a new roof.

Several merchants had established permanent stalls near the junction.

The village was busier than she remembered.

Alden Mercer lived in a modest house at the edge of the settlement.

He was a broad-shouldered man in his late fifties, with grey hair and a stiff left hand.

He greeted Seraphina and the Department's representative politely.

"Lady Seraphina. I was told you were coming."

"Thank you for receiving us, Master Mercer."

He led them into a small room.

A kettle was already warming on the stove.

His wife offered tea.

Seraphina accepted.

The Department's representative introduced himself as Mr Holt's assistant, Daniel Price.

He explained the committee's purpose.

Mercer listened without interrupting.

When Price finished, Mercer looked at the copy of the delivery record.

"That is my signature."

Seraphina leaned forward.

"Do you remember receiving the equipment?"

"Yes."

The answer came quickly.

"Could you describe what arrived?"

Mercer examined the document.

"Three regulating assemblies. Two frames. Several boxes of tools."

"Do you remember the condition?"

"Some of it was packed properly. One of the frames had a damaged corner."

"Was the equipment inspected?"

"Not by me. I signed for the delivery."

Seraphina nodded.

"Who inspected it?"

"An engineer from the regional office."

"Do you remember his name?"

Mercer frowned.

"Not immediately."

He looked toward the window.

"There were several people working on the western station at that time."

"Do you remember Darius Veylan?"

Mercer turned back.

"Yes."

Seraphina waited.

"He was the contractor's engineer. A quiet man. Very particular about his equipment."

"Was he present when the delivery arrived?"

"I believe so."

"Are you certain?"

Mercer shook his head.

"No. I remember him at the station, but I cannot say whether he was there on that particular day."

Seraphina did not press him.

"Did you supervise the installation?"

"No. My responsibility was maintenance coordination and site access. The technical work belonged to the engineering team."

"Was the equipment installed?"

Mercer looked at the delivery record again.

"Some of it."

"Which parts?"

"I remember the mounting frames being fitted. The regulating assemblies were stored for a time."

"Why?"

"Because the circuit was not ready."

Seraphina exchanged a brief glance with Price.

"Was that due to a technical problem?"

"Yes."

"What kind?"

Mercer rubbed his left hand.

"The distribution was unstable. The engineers wanted to complete another calibration before connecting the assemblies."

"Was the calibration completed?"

"I don't know."

Mercer looked genuinely uncertain.

"I was transferred not long afterward."

The interview continued for another hour.

Mercer remembered that the station had been active during the early stages of the programme.

Workers had visited regularly.

The site had received tools and materials.

The maintenance team had maintained a small logbook.

But he could not remember who had taken responsibility for the equipment after his transfer.

He also remembered that the station's storage room had been locked before he left.

"Who held the key?" Seraphina asked.

"The site supervisor."

"Was that you?"

"For a short time."

"And after your transfer?"

"Another supervisor would have received it."

"Do you remember who?"

"No."

Mercer looked uncomfortable.

"I should have kept better records."

Seraphina shook her head.

"You were not asked to remember twenty years of administrative changes."

He looked at her.

"I signed the delivery record."

"Yes."

"Then perhaps I should know what happened."

"Signing for equipment does not make you responsible for every later decision involving it."

Mercer was quiet.

Seraphina continued gently.

"We are trying to establish the sequence of events. Your recollection helps us, but we will compare it with the surviving records."

Mercer nodded.

After a moment, he said something else.

"There was a disagreement about the final inspection."

Seraphina looked up.

"What kind of disagreement?"

"The engineering team wanted to delay it. Someone from the regional office wanted the station marked as complete."

"Who?"

"I don't remember."

"Was the station operational?"

Mercer hesitated.

"Not fully."

"Was it safe?"

"I cannot answer that. I wasn't the engineer."

Seraphina wrote down the distinction.

The station might have been physically complete.

Its equipment might have been partially installed.

Its circuit might not have been operational.

Those were different conditions.

And someone had apparently wanted to mark the station as complete despite the unresolved technical work.

That was important.

But it was still only one man's recollection.

Before they left, Mercer asked whether the committee had found the old maintenance logbook.

Seraphina looked at him.

"Do you remember where it was kept?"

"In the station's records cabinet."

"Was it transferred when you left?"

"I don't know."

"Could it still be there?"

"Possibly."

He thought for a moment.

"There was a second cabinet in the regional office. Some of the site records were kept there."

"Do you remember its contents?"

"Maintenance logs, inspection notes, work orders. Ordinary things."

"Do you remember who handled the transfer?"

Mercer shook his head.

"No. But if the cabinet was cleared, the records would have gone to the regional archive."

Seraphina thanked him.

She did not ask him to search his own house.

She did not suggest that he should have preserved the documents.

The committee would make the necessary request through the Department.

On the return journey, Price reviewed his notes.

"That was useful."

"Yes."

"He confirmed that the equipment arrived."

"And that some components were installed."

"He also confirmed that the station was not fully operational."

"According to his recollection."

Price nodded.

"Of course."

Seraphina looked through the carriage window.

"Please separate his recollection from the documentary evidence in the report."

"I will."

"Also, record that he was responsible for receiving the delivery, not supervising the technical work."

Price made another note.

"You are very careful about responsibilities."

"Because people tend to inherit responsibilities from documents that were never intended to assign them."

Price looked at her.

"That sounds like a familiar problem."

"It is."

Back at the estate, Cedric was waiting in the west office.

He had received a copy of the preliminary interview notes.

"Well?"

"Mercer remembers the delivery."

"That is good."

"He says some equipment was installed, but the regulating assemblies were stored because the circuit was not ready."

Cedric looked at the report.

"And there was a disagreement over the final inspection?"

"Yes."

"Someone wanted the station marked complete."

"According to Mercer."

Cedric sat down.

"That could explain why the records are inconsistent."

"It could."

"Do you think the regional office falsified the completion status?"

Seraphina shook her head.

"We don't know whether the station was ever formally marked complete. We don't know who made the recommendation. We don't know whether the disagreement concerned the physical construction, the technical inspection or the financial settlement."

Cedric sighed.

"You have an answer for everything."

"I have a list of things we don't know."

He laughed.

"That is not quite the same thing."

"No."

She placed the interview notes on the desk.

"Send the Department a request for the regional office's final inspection procedures."

"Why?"

"Because if someone wanted to mark the station complete, we need to know what completion meant under the rules at that time."

Cedric nodded.

"Physical construction?"

"Perhaps."

"Equipment installation?"

"Possibly."

"Successful circuit testing?"

"That is what we need to establish."

The next committee meeting was held in Aurelion.

Lucien had reviewed the technical requirements for W-2.

The station's construction and the circuit's commissioning were recorded as separate stages.

A building could be completed before its magical infrastructure was operational.

Equipment could be installed before final calibration.

A station could therefore be physically complete while remaining technically inactive.

Seraphina listened carefully.

"So a completion certificate might refer only to the building?"

"Yes," Lucien said.

"Not the circuit?"

"Correct."

Professor Vale added, "The original programme forms distinguished between structural completion, equipment installation and operational commissioning."

"Do we have the forms?"

"We have a blank copy."

"Do we have the completed form for W-2?"

Professor Vale shook his head.

"Not yet."

Theodore looked at Holt.

"Then the committee should request all three records separately."

Holt made a note.

Minister Garrick looked at the table.

"Could the financial settlement have been approved after structural completion, even if the circuit remained inactive?"

"That would depend on the contract," Seraphina replied.

Mr Bellamy nodded.

"The payment schedule may have been divided into construction, equipment delivery and commissioning milestones."

"Then we need the original contract schedule."

Garrick looked at her.

"Again?"

Seraphina met his gaze.

"Yes."

He exhaled.

"Very well."

At the end of the meeting, Theodore asked Seraphina to remain.

The others departed gradually.

Lucien carried his technical notes away.

Professor Vale followed.

Holt gathered the committee documents.

Cedric stayed by the door.

Theodore waited until the room was quiet.

"Do you think the western station was deliberately abandoned?"

Seraphina considered the question.

"I don't know."

"But you have found evidence that the station was not fully operational."

"Evidence that it was not fully operational at a particular point in time. We still need to establish what happened afterward."

"The contractor stopped trading."

"Yes."

"The equipment was transferred."

"Yes."

"The engineer was reassigned."

"Yes."

"And the final inspection is missing."

"Yes."

Theodore looked at her.

"Those facts are beginning to form a pattern."

"They are forming a sequence. Whether that sequence indicates deliberate abandonment, administrative failure or a technical dispute remains unknown."

He leaned back.

"You refuse to make the easy conclusion."

"I refuse to make a conclusion that might be wrong."

Theodore nodded.

"That may be the most useful thing you contribute to this committee."

Seraphina did not answer immediately.

Then she said, "I hope the committee eventually becomes capable of doing it without me."

Theodore smiled faintly.

"I suspect that is not what the Treasury would prefer."

"That is the Treasury's problem."

Three days later, the Royal Infrastructure Department located the original inspection register.

It had been stored in a separate archive cabinet.

The relevant entry was short.

Western Relay Station W-2.

Structural completion: recorded.

Equipment installation: partial.

Operational commissioning: pending.

The final inspection date was blank.

Beneath the entry was a handwritten instruction.

*Do not release final settlement until commissioning report is received.*

The instruction bore a signature.

Seraphina recognized the name immediately.

**Darius Veylan.**

She read the entry twice.

The contractor had claimed that work was completed.

The equipment had been received at the station.

The regional supervisor remembered partial installation.

And the engineer had explicitly instructed the office not to release the final settlement until the commissioning report was received.

Seraphina placed the document on the desk.

There was no longer much doubt that Darius Veylan had been involved in the technical work at W-2.

But his instruction raised a new question.

If he had requested that the final settlement be withheld, why had the Treasury later approved a reduced payment?

And who had decided that the station's incomplete commissioning did not prevent the settlement?

She turned to the bottom of the page.

There was another note.

*Commissioning report to be submitted by regional office after corrective work.*

No report number.

No date.

No recipient.

Only one additional line, written in a different hand:

*File transferred.*

Seraphina stared at the words.

The file had been transferred.

But to whom?

And why had the committee spent so long searching for a report that might have been attached to a different file entirely?

She closed the register.

For the first time, the mystery of W-2 was beginning to look less like a missing piece of equipment and more like a missing decision.

And somewhere in the old administration, someone had made that decision without leaving a complete record of it.

**End of Chapter 35**
