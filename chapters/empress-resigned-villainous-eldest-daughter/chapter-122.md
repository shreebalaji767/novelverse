# **Chapter 122: The Former Empress Has Discovered That an Afternoon Invitation May Be Enjoyed Without Bringing a Household Register. The Marquis's Eldest Daughter Has Received a Twenty-Bolt Proposal and Has Learned That a Simple Cloth Can Still Have Complicated Terms. The Marquis's Heir Has Found the Dispatch Entry He Wanted and Has Discovered That Finding the Receiving Record Is an Entirely Different Achievement.**

**26th Day of the Seventh Month, 18th Year of Yonghe.**

The former Empress Xu Yuru spent the morning deciding whether to bring her embroidery to the western pavilion.

The question was not important enough to require a consultation.

Nevertheless, she considered it while Fang Ru arranged the tea things, while the sunlight moved across the courtyard, and while a small branch on the cream cloth continued to look rather more like a crooked walking stick than a branch.

"If you take the embroidery," Fang Ru said, "you may work on it while Her Majesty—"

She stopped.

Yuru glanced up.

"While the Dowager is speaking?" she suggested.

Fang Ru lowered her eyes. "While the Dowager is speaking."

"That would be rather rude if I spent the entire visit looking down."

"You need not work on it the entire time."

"Then I would be carrying it for no particular reason."

Fang Ru looked at the cloth. "You have nearly finished the branch."

"Nearly is not finished."

"That is true."

Yuru set the needle down and examined the work.

The branch was uneven. One leaf had been placed too close to the stem, another had become longer than intended, and the third was shaped like a small fish that had lost its way.

She had considered removing it twice.

On both occasions, she had decided that removing it would take longer than accepting its existence.

"Fold it," she said.

Fang Ru picked up the cloth.

"Shall I bring the needle case?"

"No."

"The thread?"

"No."

"The small scissors?"

Yuru looked at her.

Fang Ru quietly put the scissors back.

"Then I shall bring nothing," she said.

"Bring the handkerchief you were carrying yesterday. It is useful to have one."

Fang Ru smiled faintly.

The visit was to take place in the western pavilion, where Empress Dowager Wei had arranged tea for the afternoon. The invitation had been personal, and its wording had contained no reference to household matters, records, ceremonies, or officials.

Yuru had accepted it for precisely that reason.

She changed into a pale grey-blue robe with a narrow embroidered border and selected a pair of comfortable shoes. She did not choose the most elaborate ornaments in her possession. A small jade hairpin was sufficient.

Fang Ru checked the carriage arrangements.

"The pavilion attendants have confirmed the hour," she said. "The carriage will be ready."

"Good."

"Would you like me to take the new tea jar?"

"No. The Dowager already has enough tea jars."

"Then the small porcelain cup?"

"She has cups too."

Fang Ru paused. "It seems difficult to bring something to someone who has everything."

"Then we shall bring nothing."

They departed without a gift.

The western pavilion stood beside a small pond, beyond a covered passage and a row of flowering shrubs. Its roof was modest compared with the great palace halls, but its position allowed a breeze to pass through the open sides.

Empress Dowager Wei was already seated when Yuru arrived.

"You came," the Dowager said.

"You invited me."

"That is not always enough to persuade people."

"It was enough to persuade me."

The Dowager smiled and indicated the seat opposite her.

Tea had been prepared in a small clay kettle. Beside it stood a dish of melon seeds and another containing slices of pear.

Yuru bowed and sat.

The Dowager looked at her hands.

"You did not bring your embroidery."

"I decided that I would either talk to you or sew. I thought it better to choose one."

"A sensible decision."

"I am not certain the embroidery agrees."

"Does it complain?"

"It has not yet learned to speak."

"Then it is more considerate than most people."

Yuru laughed.

The first cup of tea was poured. It was lighter than the previous tea the Dowager had sent to her residence.

"Have you finished the book about gardens?" the Dowager asked.

"Not yet."

"Is it useful?"

"Sometimes. It has taught me that stones should be placed where people need to walk."

The Dowager looked at her.

"That sounds like a lesson that ought not to require a book."

"It ought not. Yet the author has devoted several pages to it."

"Perhaps the author has met more foolish gardeners than we have."

"Perhaps."

They spoke about the pond, the weather, and the difficulty of keeping fruit from spoiling during humid days. The Dowager mentioned that one of her attendants had insisted a particular pear was ripe, only to discover that it was hard enough to be used as a paperweight.

Yuru asked whether the attendant had eaten it.

"She did."

"That was unfortunate."

"She claimed it improved after being cut into smaller pieces."

"That is a very determined approach to fruit."

The Dowager laughed.

At one point, an attendant approached with a small tray. She carried a folded note and waited until the Dowager indicated that she might speak.

"Your Majesty, the kitchen asks whether the evening fruit should be prepared as usual."

"Which fruit?"

"The pears and the remaining melons."

The Dowager considered this.

"Have the pears cut into smaller pieces. Leave the melons whole."

The attendant bowed.

Yuru took another sip of tea.

The exchange was ordinary, brief, and entirely unrelated to her.

She found that she liked it.

After the attendant left, the Dowager asked whether she had decided where to live.

"Not yet."

"Do you feel hurried?"

"No."

"Then do not hurry."

"I have begun to suspect that choosing a residence will take longer than choosing a new pair of shoes."

"That depends on the shoes."

Yuru smiled.

The Dowager looked at her over the rim of her cup.

"Your aunt's visit is approaching, is it not?"

"At the end of the month."

"Will you stay overnight?"

"No. I accepted a day visit."

"Will your parents be there?"

"I believe so."

"Then I hope you enjoy it."

Yuru nodded.

She did not explain that she had already spent more time considering what to bring than the visit itself required. The Dowager did not ask.

They finished their tea without discussing the palace's accounts or the people who had once sent every difficult question to Yuru's residence.

When she rose to leave, the Dowager said, "Come again if you wish."

"I will."

"And if you do not wish to come, that is also acceptable."

Yuru bowed.

"That is a generous invitation."

"It is an invitation, not an order."

The distinction stayed with Yuru as she returned to her residence.

On the way, Fang Ru asked whether the tea had been better.

"It was."

"Would you like me to record the variety?"

"No."

"Then how will we remember it?"

"I shall remember that it was good."

Fang Ru considered this answer.

It was not an efficient system.

It was, however, an acceptable one.

In the eastern market, Shen Mingzhao was examining a piece of brown cloth.

It lay across the workshop table, its edges weighted by two wooden rulers. The weave was simple, the colour ordinary, and the thickness suitable for everyday garments.

There was nothing remarkable about it.

That was precisely why the proposal interested her.

The merchant, a man named Guo Jian, had asked about twenty bolts of the cloth. He had supplied a small sample and proposed delivery within thirty days. Unlike several other merchants who had approached the workshop recently, he had not demanded an immediate answer or suggested that a large quantity should be produced on trust.

He had, however, omitted the price.

Mingzhao turned the sample over.

"How much material will be needed for twenty bolts?" she asked.

The foreman, Sun Qiao, stood beside the table with his notes.

"Less difficult to estimate than the finer cloth. The weave is ordinary. The workers already know the method."

"Does the sample show the full width?"

"Yes. I measured it twice."

"Was it washed?"

"No. I asked the merchant. He says the sample is in the same condition as the cloth he intends to supply."

"His statement is not a measurement."

"No, miss."

Sun Qiao had learned that agreement was often the safest response when Mingzhao pointed out a weakness in a statement.

She looked at him.

"Do not agree merely because I have said something."

He straightened.

"The sample has not been washed. We cannot yet say how much it will shrink."

"Better."

She returned her attention to the cloth.

"Estimate the cost of twenty bolts, including ordinary waste, dyeing, wages, finishing, and delivery. Show the amount separately for each."

Sun Qiao nodded.

"Should I reserve the looms?"

"No."

"The workers have asked whether another order is coming."

"They may ask. We have not accepted one."

"They would prefer to know whether they should remain."

Mingzhao looked at the foreman.

"Have their existing agreements been fulfilled?"

"Yes."

"Then they have been paid for the work they agreed to do. If they seek other employment, they may do so."

Sun Qiao hesitated.

"Some of them have become accustomed to working here."

"That is not a contract."

"No, miss."

"Do not promise them work that I have not obtained."

He bowed.

Mingzhao placed the cloth sample beside the written inquiry.

The merchant's proposal contained three things: a requested quantity, a proposed delivery period, and a sample.

It contained no price, no deposit, no clear inspection arrangement, and no agreement about what would happen if the finished cloth differed from the sample.

It was not yet a contract.

It was not even a particularly complete proposal.

Still, it was better than a request for goods followed by a promise to settle the price later.

"Send a reply," she said. "Ask for his price per bolt, the amount he expects to pay in advance, and whether the supplied sample is to be the standard for inspection. Ask whether he will accept ordinary variation in colour or width, and if so, how much."

Sun Qiao wrote the points down.

"Anything else?"

"Ask who will receive the goods and where."

"Yes."

"And do not tell him that we are prepared to accept twenty bolts."

Sun Qiao paused.

"Even though he requested twenty?"

"He requested twenty. We have not agreed."

"Understood."

Mingzhao looked again at the sample.

The workshop had completed the earlier two hundred-bolt order and the eighty-bolt order. Both accounts were closed. The workers had been paid, the suppliers settled, and the remaining money divided among the workshop's ordinary reserve, possible trial production, and future expansion.

That did not mean she should immediately replace completed work with new obligations.

An empty loom was not a financial disaster.

A loom producing goods for a buyer who had never agreed to the price could become one.

She handed the sample back to Sun Qiao.

"Keep it with the inquiry. Do not mix it with the other samples."

"Yes."

He carried it away.

Mingzhao then opened the separate record concerning the six-bolt trial.

The first supplier had still not provided an acceptable written standard for colour variation. The second supplier's cloth had been closer in weight but differed in sheen. Neither had received an order.

She wrote a short instruction beneath the latest correspondence:

*No purchase until sample, price, and production conditions are agreed.*

She read it once, then closed the file.

A person could write the same instruction ten times and still be tempted to ignore it when a merchant offered an attractive price.

Mingzhao preferred to make the instruction difficult to misunderstand.

At the Marquisate of Qingyuan, Shen Mingyuan had begun to regret asking for the receiving register.

Not because the request was unreasonable.

Because it had produced another list of records that might exist.

He sat in the accounts room with Steward Zhou De and Liang Wen. On the table lay the report from the previous day's examination of the Northern Road Movement Supplement.

The four-cart entry had been found.

The cart numbers matched the old movement pages.

The cargo column remained blank.

The destination was incomplete.

The phrase *To be confirmed upon arrival* appeared beside all four entries.

There was no receiving acknowledgment in the register.

Mingyuan read the report again.

"Could the receiving record have been entered in another book?"

"Possibly," Zhou said. "The northern depot did not always use the same register as the central transport office."

"Then we need the depot register."

"Yes."

"Have we found it?"

"Not yet."

Mingyuan looked at Liang Wen.

"What have the records clerks located?"

Liang Wen opened a second sheet.

"The central collection index lists several receiving registers from the northern depots, but the entries are grouped by year and office. The index does not identify individual dispatches."

"Which registers cover the relevant month?"

"Three are listed. One is a grain receipt register, one concerns general freight, and one is described as a temporary receiving book."

Mingyuan frowned.

"Why are there three?"

"Because the northern depot received different kinds of goods through different offices. The grain register was maintained separately. The general freight register covered ordinary deliveries. The temporary receiving book was used when a regular register was unavailable or when a delivery required separate confirmation."

"Which one contains the four carts?"

"We do not know."

"Have all three been examined?"

"No. The grain register was checked only for the dates around the dispatch. The general freight register has not yet been brought out. The temporary receiving book is listed in the collection index, but its final shelf location has not been confirmed."

Mingyuan leaned back.

The grain discrepancy had already become a separate inquiry. The four carts might have carried anything, or nothing of consequence. He had repeatedly instructed the clerks not to assume that an unexplained dispatch must account for the missing grain.

He would not allow the two matters to become one merely because the dates were inconveniently close.

"Have the clerks search the general freight register first," he said. "Then the temporary receiving book. They should examine the pages before and after the relevant date, not only the four cart numbers."

Liang Wen recorded the instruction.

"Should they compare the cargo descriptions with the grain register?"

"Only if the records independently show that the same goods were involved. Do not begin with that assumption."

"Understood."

Zhou De watched Mingyuan read the report again.

"Young master, there is another difficulty."

Mingyuan looked up.

"What?"

"The collection index gives the register's title and former office, but not the name of the clerk who transferred it. The general collection received several boxes together. The receiving list records the number of bundles, not the individual books inside each bundle."

"So we may find the register but not know who handled it before it arrived."

"That is possible."

Mingyuan looked at the old index.

"Then we record the transfer history as far as it can be established. We do not invent the missing part."

Zhou nodded.

Mingyuan turned to Liang Wen.

"Have you found any acknowledgment from the northern depot?"

"Not yet."

"Any reference to a separate receiving clerk?"

"Only a roster for the depot office. It lists several clerks, but the roster does not tell us who was on duty on that day."

"Then we need the duty records."

Liang Wen hesitated.

"Some of those records may have been kept locally."

"Then ask whether they survive."

"And if they do not?"

"Record that they have not been found. Do not write that they never existed."

Liang Wen bowed.

The room fell quiet.

Mingyuan glanced at the four-cart entry once more.

He had wanted the record because he believed it might answer a question.

Instead, it had shown him how many questions could remain after a record was found.

He was beginning to understand why his sister had insisted on separating what was known from what was merely suspected.

It was not that she had enjoyed making the accounts difficult.

The accounts had already been difficult.

She had simply refused to pretend otherwise.

"Have the collection clerks been given a deadline?" he asked.

"Three days to report what they have located," Zhou replied. "They may request more time if the registers require careful examination."

"Good. I want a report in three days, not a conclusion in three days."

Zhou inclined his head.

Mingyuan picked up the next document.

It concerned the revised property schedule. Several entries had been reclassified since the previous review. The nine confirmed advances remained separate from the four payments for which repayment arrangements were unclear. The ten uncertain entries remained uncertain. The seventy-two taels in study repairs were still listed as a separate personal expenditure, with reimbursement undecided.

The disputed two hundred and twenty-eight taels had not been resolved.

He read the page slowly.

"Has the missing original receipt for the confirmed advance been found?"

"No," Zhou said.

"Then the surviving copy remains marked as a copy."

"Yes."

"And the payment entry that may have been a supplier payment?"

"Still unverified. The voucher has not been found."

"Then leave the recipient unconfirmed."

Liang Wen nodded.

Mingyuan signed the instruction authorizing the continued search.

He did not sign the property schedule as final.

It was not final.

At the workshop, Mingzhao received the foreman's cost estimate shortly before the afternoon meal.

It was more detailed than she had expected.

The estimate separated raw material, dye, weaving wages, finishing, ordinary waste, packing, and delivery. Sun Qiao had also included a small allowance for correcting uneven edges, though he noted that the sample did not suggest such correction would be necessary if the supplied material was consistent.

Mingzhao examined the figures.

"Did you include the cost of washing a sample?"

"Yes. I have shown it separately."

"Good."

"The merchant's sample may shrink."

"Then we should know how much."

Sun Qiao waited.

"The estimate is reasonable," she said. "But it is not a production authorization."

"I understand."

"Do you?"

He nodded.

Mingzhao placed the estimate beside the merchant's inquiry.

"The price must leave room for the workshop's expenses and a profit worth accepting. If he expects twenty bolts at a price that barely covers the material, we will decline."

Sun Qiao looked at the figures.

"Would you consider reducing the profit if the order were easy?"

"Only if the order offered another benefit that we can identify. Ease of production does not mean the workshop should work for nothing."

He bowed.

A servant entered with the merchant's reply.

Mingzhao opened it.

Guo Jian had supplied a price per bolt. It was not unreasonable, but it was lower than the figure Sun Qiao had calculated as the minimum acceptable price.

He had also offered a deposit.

The amount was small.

The remaining payment would be made after delivery, but the letter did not specify how long after delivery. The merchant had agreed that the sample should be used for inspection, though he had written that minor differences in shade should be considered acceptable.

Mingzhao read the final sentence twice.

"Minor differences," she said.

Sun Qiao leaned forward slightly.

"That is vague."

"Yes."

She placed the letter beside the sample.

The merchant had made progress. He had supplied a price, offered a deposit, and acknowledged the sample.

He had also attempted to reserve the right to decide what counted as a minor difference.

Mingzhao took up her brush.

She dictated a reply.

The workshop would consider the twenty-bolt order at a revised price. The sample would be the inspection standard. Any permitted variation in shade would need to be agreed in writing before production. The balance would be payable within three days of delivery and inspection. The receiving place and the person authorized to accept the goods must be named.

She added one further condition: the deposit must be sufficient to cover the material purchase and the first stage of production.

Sun Qiao listened carefully.

"Will he accept?"

"That is his decision."

"Should we hold the looms?"

"No."

He bowed and carried the reply away.

Mingzhao returned to the six-bolt trial file.

The first supplier's latest letter had arrived with the merchant's reply. It repeated that the colour could not be guaranteed exactly, though the width could be controlled after a loom adjustment.

Mingzhao did not answer immediately.

She placed the letter beside the three samples.

The first was too warm.

The second was closer but still visibly different.

The third had a more acceptable colour, but the weave was tighter and the edge uneven.

The merchant wanted her to accept a degree of variation without defining it.

She wrote a single line on the correspondence cover:

*Await measurable standard. No purchase.*

The six-bolt trial remained a possibility.

The twenty-bolt inquiry was a separate matter.

Neither was yet an order.

By late afternoon, Xu Yuru had returned to her residence.

Fang Ru helped her remove the outer robe and placed the jade hairpin on the dressing table.

"Was the visit pleasant?" she asked.

"Yes."

"Did Her Majesty speak about the palace?"

"Only when an attendant asked about the fruit."

Fang Ru looked relieved.

"That is good."

Yuru sat by the window.

"Why?"

"Because I thought you might be asked to help with something."

"She did not ask."

"Then the visit was truly a visit."

Yuru considered the remark.

"Yes."

Fang Ru poured a little warm water into a cup.

"Would you like to continue the embroidery?"

Yuru looked toward the folded cloth.

"Not today."

"Shall I put it away?"

"Yes. I will look at it tomorrow."

Fang Ru folded it carefully.

Yuru took up the garden book from the small table. She had been reading a section about fruit trees and the distance between their roots. The author had included a diagram that was considerably more complicated than the subject appeared to require.

She read the first page.

Then the second.

At the third, she discovered that the author had spent half a page arguing with an unnamed gardener who apparently believed that trees could be planted wherever there was an empty patch of ground.

Yuru closed the book.

"Is something wrong?" Fang Ru asked.

"The author has become angry with a gardener."

"Perhaps the gardener did something foolish."

"Perhaps. But the tree has already been planted. I do not see how arguing with him in a book will help."

Fang Ru smiled.

Yuru placed the book aside and looked at the light in the courtyard.

She had once spent entire afternoons reviewing accounts, resolving disputes, correcting schedules, and answering questions that had been waiting for her since morning.

Now an afternoon could pass without anyone asking her to decide how many servants should be assigned to a ceremony or whether a delivery had been properly recorded.

The silence was still unfamiliar.

But it was no longer entirely uncomfortable.

She picked up a small pear from the fruit dish.

It was ripe.

She ate it without asking anyone to record its condition.

That evening, at the Marquisate, Mingyuan received the first report from the collection clerks.

The general freight register had been located.

It covered the relevant month.

The pages for the dispatch date were intact, but the entries did not list the four cart numbers. There was a separate notation referring to an attached receiving sheet, though the sheet itself was not bound into the register.

Mingyuan read the report twice.

"Where is the attached sheet?"

"The clerk has not found it," Zhou replied.

"Does the register say what the sheet contained?"

"No. Only that it was attached."

"Was it removed?"

"The binding shows no obvious tear. It may have been kept separately."

Mingyuan placed the report down.

"And the temporary receiving book?"

"Still being searched for."

He looked at the blank space where the receiving acknowledgment ought to have been.

A separate sheet might explain the four carts.

Or it might refer to another delivery entirely.

He would not decide before seeing it.

"Ask the clerks to search the temporary receiving book and the loose sheets stored with the general freight register," he said. "They must preserve the order in which the papers are found."

Zhou nodded.

"Should we send someone to the northern depot?"

"Not yet. First establish what the central collection holds. Then we can ask the depot for the corresponding records."

He looked again at the report.

The four carts had been dispatched.

That much was established.

What they carried, where they went, and who received them remained unanswered.

Mingyuan had hoped that finding the register would bring the inquiry closer to its end.

Instead, it had revealed that the next record might be a loose sheet, stored separately, with no certainty that it belonged to the entry at all.

He put the report in the proper file.

"Do not attach it to the grain inquiry," he said.

Zhou glanced at him.

"I have not."

"Good."

Mingyuan closed the file.

He had begun to understand that an investigation could become less confusing only when people stopped trying to make every document answer every question.

Unfortunately, the clerks had now discovered that one missing register might have left behind a missing sheet.

The records office was likely to become very busy.

The missing sheet, meanwhile, had not yet expressed any intention of being found.
