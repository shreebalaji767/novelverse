# **Chapter 97: The Former Empress Has Discovered That a Household Manual Can Be Improved by Writing Down the Questions Its Author Forgot to Answer. The Marquis's Eldest Daughter Has Learned That a Workshop's First Duty Is to Follow the Approved Sample Rather Than Impress Its Employer. The Marquis's Heir Has Found That a Missing Notebook Can Be Officially Issued, Unofficially Unreturned, and Still Entirely Unhelpful.**

**1st Day of the Seventh Month, 18th Year of Yonghe.**

The first day of the seventh month began with rain.

It was not heavy enough to interrupt the ordinary business of the capital, but it was persistent enough to make the western paths damp and discourage anyone from walking without a reason.

Xu Yuru watched the rain from the covered veranda of the guest residence.

Fang Ru brought her a cup of tea.

"Would you still like to walk later, Madam?"

"If it stops."

"And if it does not?"

"Then I shall not walk."

Fang Ru nodded.

Yuru glanced at her.

"You sound surprised."

"I thought you might say that the rain should not interfere with a planned walk."

"That would be a rather unreasonable thing to demand of the rain."

Fang Ru placed the tea down.

Yuru returned to the table, where her notebook lay open beside her father's household manual.

The previous day, she had written down several questions the author had failed to answer. This morning, she had added a few more.

A servant who noticed a problem outside his assigned duty should report it.

A servant who was authorized to correct a small problem should know the limit of that authority.

A servant who found an error in a record should not erase the original entry merely to make the page look orderly.

Yuru paused at the third point.

She had written it more firmly than the others.

She remembered the palace accounts in which a correction had sometimes been entered so neatly that the original mistake could no longer be identified. It had been easier to produce a clean page than to preserve the history of how the page had become wrong.

She had disliked such records even when she was Empress.

She disliked them now.

But the notebook was not an instruction for the palace. It was a record of what she had been thinking while reading her father's book.

She turned the page and wrote a heading:

**Questions for a Household Manager Who Wishes to Sleep at Night.**

Fang Ru, passing behind her, read it.

"That is a long heading."

"It is a serious subject."

"Will you add more questions?"

"Probably."

"Then the notebook may become a book."

Yuru looked at her.

"That would be unfortunate. I have just finished reading one."

Fang Ru smiled and returned to the tea tray.

The rain continued.

Yuru drank her tea and decided that the walk could wait.

At the eastern-market workshop, Shen Mingzhao received the final report on the afternoon inspection trial.

The first six bolts had been examined after the fabric rested.

Five passed the initial comparison with the approved sample.

One required a closer edge examination.

The remaining fourteen bolts from the group had now been checked.

Of the twenty, seventeen passed the first inspection. Two required minor corrections, and one was held for a second comparison because the edge tension appeared inconsistent.

After correction and re-examination, all twenty passed.

The report also included a comparison with the earlier inspection schedule.

The later inspection had made the edges easier to assess, but it had not eliminated the need for correction. The supervisor recommended retaining the afternoon inspection for the remainder of the order, while keeping the same standards and recording the interval between weaving and inspection.

Mingzhao read the report carefully.

"Did the supervisor recommend changing the specifications?"

Qiao Yue, who had brought the report, shook her head.

"No. He recommended changing only the inspection time."

"Good."

Mingzhao examined the correction notes.

The two corrected bolts had required minor adjustments. The third had passed after a second comparison. None had been rejected or written off.

She signed the report's approval for the trial arrangement to continue.

Then she returned the papers.

"Tell the supervisor that the inspection schedule may remain in place for this order. He should not treat it as permission to alter the approved sample."

"Yes, Madam."

"And the next group?"

"Twenty more are being prepared. The supervisor expects them to enter the weaving stage tomorrow."

Mingzhao nodded.

The first ten bolts were complete.

The next twenty had now passed internal inspection.

The third group was being prepared.

The order was moving.

She looked at the production sheet again.

There was something satisfying about seeing a problem identified, corrected, and recorded without requiring her to spend an entire afternoon at the workshop.

It was not the same satisfaction as personally solving it.

It was better in one important respect.

The process could be repeated without her.

She put the report away.

The Marquisate's transport office sent a second response before noon.

It confirmed that the movement notebook had been issued to the northern warehouse transport supervisor during the relevant season.

It did not identify the individual who had signed for it.

The central archive had not yet completed the reorganization of the damaged register volume.

The office had also confirmed that no return entry for the notebook appeared in the surviving copy.

Mingyuan read the report.

"So the notebook was issued."

"Yes," Liang Wen replied.

"And there is no return entry."

"Yes."

"But we do not know whether it was returned without being recorded, retained by the supervisor, lost, or filed elsewhere."

"That is correct."

Mingyuan placed the report beside the four-cart dispatch record.

The two documents now supported a limited conclusion: a separate transport notebook had been issued to the northern warehouse supervisor, and the surviving record did not show its return.

That was all.

The notebook might have contained the missing destination.

It might have contained the cargo description.

It might have contained nothing relevant to the four carts.

The estate had no right to choose among those possibilities simply because one of them would make the investigation easier.

"Has the former supervisor been located?" Mingyuan asked.

"Not yet. The northern warehouse's personnel records identify the position but not the person's current residence."

"Then ask the local office for the departure register and the names of the people who held the position during that period."

Liang Wen wrote it down.

Mingyuan turned to the grain schedule.

Forty-seven sacks had been recorded in the original stock.

Twenty had been covered by the instruction.

Eighteen had been confirmed as received.

Two remained unconfirmed.

Twenty-seven remained unexplained.

The four-cart entry still had no confirmed cargo or destination.

The blank-destination transport payment remained separate.

The old payment voucher attributed to Mingzhao remained missing.

Mingyuan had hoped the first day of the new month would bring at least one clear answer.

Instead, it had brought confirmation that a missing record had once existed.

He rubbed his forehead.

Steward Zhou, standing near the door, said, "At least we know where to continue looking."

Mingyuan looked at him.

"Yes."

He glanced again at the report.

"We know where to continue looking."

It was not the same as knowing what had happened.

But it was better than pretending otherwise.

The Marquis of Qingyuan received Mingyuan's report that afternoon.

Shen Guowei read the account of the transport notebook twice.

"How can a notebook be issued and not returned?"

Mingyuan stood before his father.

"That is what the surviving register shows."

"Then the supervisor must have kept it."

"We do not know that."

"What else could have happened?"

"It may have been returned without an entry. It may have been filed elsewhere. It may have been lost. The surviving record does not tell us."

The Marquis frowned.

"You are making a simple matter complicated."

Mingyuan did not answer immediately.

He knew his father wanted a name.

A name would give the family something to question. A person could be summoned, challenged, and asked to explain. A missing notebook could not be made to answer merely because the family disliked the uncertainty.

"The northern warehouse records are incomplete," Mingyuan said. "If we accuse someone before establishing who held the notebook, we may end up pursuing the wrong person."

The Marquis placed the report on his desk.

"And the grain?"

"Still unresolved."

"Twenty-seven sacks?"

"Twenty-seven unexplained in the current records. The four-cart entry has not been connected to them."

The Marquis looked dissatisfied.

Mingyuan continued.

"The accounts office is also preparing the property-division schedule for Mingzhao. The first draft needs correction before it is sent."

His father's expression changed.

"Why?"

"Some entries describe confirmed payments as possible repayments. The study repairs are described as her personal expenses rather than estate maintenance paid from her personal funds."

"Does that change the amount?"

"It changes what the amount means."

The Marquis leaned back.

"Your sister will argue over every word."

"She has asked us to distinguish what is established from what is uncertain."

"She always has a reason to object."

Mingyuan looked at his father.

"Then we should make fewer mistakes."

For a moment, the room was silent.

The Marquis looked at the report again.

He had expected his son to bring him a conclusion.

Instead, Mingyuan had brought him a list of things that still required investigation and a reminder that the family had not yet prepared its own property records correctly.

It was not the kind of progress Shen Guowei preferred.

But he could not easily dismiss it.

"Have the schedule corrected," he said at last.

"Yes, Father."

"And do not let this matter continue indefinitely."

Mingyuan bowed.

He understood the instruction.

He also understood that a deadline could not create evidence.

In the afternoon, Shen Mingzhao received the updated property-division letter from the Marquisate.

It contained no meeting date.

It did contain a promise that the corrected schedules would be sent before any meeting was proposed.

Mingzhao read it and set it beside her other correspondence.

Qiao Yue was arranging the account papers.

"Will you reply?"

"Not today."

"Are you displeased?"

"No."

Mingzhao looked at the letter again.

"The family has acknowledged the conditions. There is nothing useful to add until the documents arrive."

"That is a change."

"What is?"

"Previously, you would have written another letter to ensure they understood."

Mingzhao gave her a cool look.

"Do you believe they did not understand?"

"I believe you did not trust them to remember."

Mingzhao considered the answer.

Then she returned to the papers.

"That is not entirely wrong."

She did not write another letter.

The property division remained incomplete.

The nine confirmed advances, four payment-confirmed but unresolved entries, ten uncertain entries, seventy-two taels in study repairs, and two hundred and twenty-eight disputed taels still required proper classification.

The northern canal property remained her own possible acquisition, not part of the family estate.

The workshop business remained separate from the Marquisate's accounts.

She had no intention of allowing the family to merge those matters simply because they were all connected to her.

She turned to the next paper.

It was the workshop's production schedule.

The new order was progressing.

That, at least, was something she could review without having to argue with her father.

At the palace guest residence, the rain finally stopped.

Fang Ru opened the veranda doors.

The air was cool, and the western path had begun to dry.

"Would you like to walk now, Madam?"

Yuru looked at the sky.

"How long would it take?"

"To the small pavilion and back?"

"Not long."

"Then perhaps the path will be dry enough."

Yuru put aside her notebook.

She had been considering whether to continue reading the household manual or begin the book on provincial granaries.

The two books had been borrowed from her father on the twenty-first day of the sixth month. Three months remained before their return date.

There was no reason to finish both immediately.

She stood and took her outer robe.

The walk was quiet.

Fang Ru accompanied her at a comfortable distance. The path was damp in a few places, but the paving stones were not slippery.

At the small pavilion, Yuru paused to look at the garden.

The rain had left the leaves bright.

She remembered the garden visit with the Emperor and Empress Dowager, but she did not feel compelled to repeat it. That had been a pleasant afternoon with people she knew. This was simply a walk.

Fang Ru stood beside her.

"Would you like to sit?"

"Not today."

They continued along the path.

Yuru had once measured her days by the number of matters she had settled.

Now she was learning to measure them by less demanding things.

A chapter read.

A letter sent.

A walk completed.

An afternoon in which no one had asked her to take responsibility for a problem that belonged to someone else.

It was not an empty life.

It was simply no longer arranged around the needs of the palace.

When they returned to the guest residence, Yuru asked for tea.

Fang Ru brought it.

The household manual remained on the table, open to the chapter about servants' responsibilities.

Yuru looked at the marked pages.

She had not finished agreeing with the author.

That was acceptable.

She could return to it tomorrow.

At the workshop, the supervisor posted the next day's production schedule.

The first thirty bolts were complete and had passed internal inspection.

The next twenty were entering production.

The remaining thirty had not yet been authorized for work.

The afternoon inspection arrangement would continue for the next group, with the same approved sample and specifications.

No one had been asked to increase the daily target.

No one had been promised a reward for finishing early.

The workers knew that the buyer would inspect the goods against the written sample and that any correction would have to be completed within the contract's terms.

Mingzhao received the schedule before dinner.

She checked the quantities and found them correct.

Then she put it away.

The order was not finished.

The family division was not finished.

The grain investigation was not finished.

And the missing transport notebook had not returned simply because a new month had begun.

But the first thirty bolts were complete, the workshop was following its own procedure, and the Marquisate had finally begun preparing the property documents according to the conditions she had set.

Mingzhao closed the account book.

There would be more work tomorrow.

For tonight, she did not intend to invent any.
