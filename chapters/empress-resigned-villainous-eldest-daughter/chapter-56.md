# **Chapter 56: The Former Empress Has Been Told That Tomorrow's Garden Visit Requires No Preparation. The Marquis's Eldest Daughter Has Been Told That a Simple Workshop Sample Requires Three Separate Opinions. Both Women Have Begun to Suspect That the Word “Simple” Is Being Used Improperly.**

**19th Day of the Fifth Month, 18th Year of Yonghe.**



The former Empress Xu Yuru received the morning's first message while she was eating porridge.



It was a short message.



The Imperial Gardens had been prepared for the informal visit scheduled for the following day. The eastern path had been cleared. The stone steps near the lotus pond had been checked. A shaded pavilion had been selected. Tea and fruit would be provided.



No ceremonial arrangements were necessary.



No documents would be brought.



No officials would attend.



Xu Yuru read the message once, folded it, and placed it beside her bowl.



Fang Ru, who was standing nearby, waited for further instructions.



There were none.



After a moment, Fang Ru asked, "Madam, shall I prepare the formal visiting robe?"



Xu Yuru looked at her.



"For a garden?"



"The Emperor will be present."



"The Emperor has two legs. He can walk through a garden without requiring me to wear a ceremonial robe."



Fang Ru lowered her head.



Her shoulders moved slightly.



Xu Yuru continued eating.



The porridge was warm, the pickled vegetables were properly salted, and the morning was quiet.



For several years, mornings had begun with reports. If the reports were not waiting, someone had been waiting to explain why they were late. If no one was explaining, an urgent matter had usually been discovered by the person who was supposed to be responsible for it.



Now the message had arrived, been read, and been put aside.



Nothing had collapsed.



Xu Yuru found this mildly encouraging.



She took another spoonful of porridge.



"Prepare the light blue robe," she said at last. "The one without embroidered phoenixes."



Fang Ru nodded.



"And the walking shoes."



"Yes, Madam."



"Do not prepare three pairs."



Fang Ru paused.



Xu Yuru looked at her again.



"One pair is sufficient for walking."



Fang Ru bowed.



The former Empress returned to her breakfast.



At the Imperial Palace, Chief Eunuch Gao Shun received a different kind of difficulty.



The Emperor had summoned him to confirm that tomorrow's visit would remain informal.



Gao Shun stood before the imperial desk with the expression of a man who had already discovered that the word *informal* could create more work than the word *ceremonial*.



"Have the pavilion arrangements been completed?" Emperor Zhao Jingheng asked.



"Yes, Your Majesty."



"Tea?"



"Prepared."



"Fruit?"



"Prepared."



"Any documents?"



Gao Shun hesitated.



The Emperor raised his eyes.



"No documents," he said.



"None have been prepared, Your Majesty."



"Good."



Gao Shun bowed.



The Emperor returned to the memorial before him.



A moment later, he looked up again.



"Have the garden maintenance records been placed in the pavilion?"



Gao Shun's face remained perfectly composed.



"No, Your Majesty."



"Good."



The Emperor read another line.



"Have the maintenance records been placed anywhere near the pavilion?"



Gao Shun considered the question carefully.



"They are in the Works Office, Your Majesty."



"Keep them there."



"Yes, Your Majesty."



The Emperor dismissed him.



Gao Shun withdrew with the measured dignity of a senior official who had successfully prevented a stack of documents from attending a garden visit.



He did not consider this a minor achievement.



In the palace, a document that was not invited could still arrive through an open door.

At the Marquisate of Qingyuan, Shen Mingyuan discovered that a temporary agreement could be more complicated than a permanent one.



The western field tenant had signed the temporary cultivation agreement the previous day. The document specified the period of cultivation, the payment arrangement, the tenant's responsibilities, the estate's responsibilities, and the conditions under which either party could give notice.



It did not renew the expired lease.



It did not establish a new long-term tenancy.



It did not settle the field's future ownership or management.



Mingyuan had read the agreement twice before signing it.



This morning, Steward Zhou De placed the signed copy on the table.



"The tenant has received his copy," the steward said. "The estate copy is here."



Mingyuan examined the seal.



"Is the temporary arrangement entered into the property schedule?"



"It will be."



"Not under the old lease?"



"No, Young Master."



Mingyuan nodded.



He had once thought that estate management consisted mainly of deciding what should be done.



He was beginning to understand that it also consisted of preventing one decision from being mistaken for another.



A temporary permission could become a renewed lease if someone copied the wrong heading.



A payment could become rent for an entire year if the receipt used an old description.



A field could become an inherited responsibility merely because the family had cultivated it for generations.



The steward watched him.



"Young Master, the tenant has asked whether he may begin preparing the soil."



"Has the agreement allowed it?"



"Yes."



"Then he may."



Steward Zhou bowed.



Mingyuan leaned back.



For a moment, he felt satisfied.



Then he remembered the grain records.



The satisfaction disappeared.



"Has Sun Qiming sent another report?"



"Not yet."



"Has the old warehouse steward Zhang been contacted?"



"His residence has been confirmed. A letter is being prepared."



"Do not write that he is responsible for the missing grain."



Steward Zhou looked at him.



"We have not established that."



"Then the letter should ask about the records, not accuse him."



The steward nodded.



Mingyuan looked down at the temporary cultivation agreement again.



The document had taken less than an hour to complete.



The grain discrepancy had occupied the family for days.



He was beginning to suspect that a field was easier to manage than a number.

In the eastern market, Shen Mingzhao had received three opinions about a piece of silk folding.



The first opinion came from Lin Shuyun.



The second came from the senior finisher.



The third came from the workshop assistant who had been asked to compare the sample with the buyer's original specification.



Mingzhao read all three.



Then she read the buyer's message.



Zhou Wenqi had examined the revised sample.



He acknowledged that the new folding method reduced handling time. However, he wanted to confirm whether the slightly altered edge appearance would remain consistent after transport and storage.



He had not approved the revised method.



Mingzhao placed the message on the table.



Lin Shuyun sat opposite her, holding a ledger.



"That is reasonable," Lin said.



"It is inconvenient."



"Those are not mutually exclusive."



Mingzhao looked at her.



Lin Shuyun did not lower her eyes.



The workshop had continued using the original method. The new order's production schedule had not been altered, and the four additional workers were still following the established process.



The two trainees had improved, though not at the same pace.



One had become faster without losing much accuracy.



The other had become more accurate but still required additional time.



Their work was being checked separately from that of the experienced finishers.



Mingzhao had insisted on this distinction.



A training record was not a production record.



A production record was not a payment record.



And a payment record was not proof of profit.



She had become tired of people treating those four statements as though they were one sentence.



Lin Shuyun turned a page.



"The original forty-two bolts have been paid for," she said. "The finishing costs are still being calculated."



"I know."



"The new order has its deposit."



"I know."



"The workers' wages—"



"I know."



Lin Shuyun stopped.



Mingzhao placed the buyer's message beside the sample.



"Then we know the same things."



Lin Shuyun smiled faintly.



"That is an improvement."



Mingzhao did not smile.



She had not yet decided whether the improvement was large enough to justify the expense.

At the workshop, the senior finisher stood beside the long table.



He had worked with silk for more than twenty years. His hands were steady, his judgment was usually sound, and his pride was considerable.



The revised folding method had been his idea.



He had not intended to disregard the buyer's requirements. He had intended to improve the process.



When Mingzhao arrived, he bowed.



"Miss Shen."



She examined the two samples.



The original sample had a clean, familiar edge.



The revised sample was faster to prepare, but the fold sat differently at the corner.



It was not a dramatic difference.



That was precisely the problem.



A dramatic difference could be rejected immediately.



A small difference required discussion.



Mingzhao placed the samples side by side.



"How much time does the revised method save?"



"Approximately one tenth of the handling time for each bolt," the finisher said.



"Approximately?"



"We have not completed the full count."



"Then record it as an estimate."



He bowed.



"Yes, Miss Shen."



"Does the revised method affect the cloth itself?"



"No damage has been observed."



"Has the sample been stored for the period specified by the buyer?"



"Not yet."



Mingzhao nodded.



"Then we have not completed the comparison."



The finisher looked at the sample.



"I believed the method would be acceptable."



"You may be correct."



He seemed relieved.



Mingzhao continued.



"But believing it acceptable is not the same as receiving approval."



The relief vanished.



Lin Shuyun, standing behind her, coughed softly.



Mingzhao ignored her.



"Continue with the original method," she said. "Keep the revised sample separate. Record the time saved, the edge appearance, and the storage result. When Zhou Wenqi replies, we will decide."



The finisher bowed.



He did not appear pleased.



He also did not argue.



Mingzhao considered this a successful meeting.



It had lasted less than half an hour.



No one had raised their voice.



No one had altered the production method.



No one had claimed that an estimate was a final figure.



The workshop had survived.



The day was still young.

At the Xu residence, Xu Yuru's mother had discovered that the former Empress intended to visit a garden without bringing a formal procession.



Madam Xu considered this information for several moments.



Then she asked, "Will you be walking for a long time?"



"Not particularly."



"Will there be steps?"



"Some."



"Have you taken your walking shoes?"



"Yes."



Madam Xu nodded.



She did not ask whether the Emperor would attend.



She did not ask whether the Empress Dowager would attend.



She did not ask whether the visit would be recorded in the imperial calendar.



Instead, she ordered a small packet of dried fruit prepared for her daughter.



Xu Yuru looked at it.



"Mother, the palace will provide fruit."



"Palace fruit is for the palace."



"This is a garden visit."



"Then you may eat this in the garden."



Xu Yuru held the packet.



It was wrapped in plain paper and tied with a narrow cord.



She had received more elaborate gifts in her life.



This one was difficult to refuse.



"Thank you," she said.



Madam Xu waved a hand.



"Do not thank me. It is only fruit."



Xu Yuru looked down at the packet.



She had once been responsible for deciding how much fruit should be purchased, how it should be stored, who should receive it, and whether the amount distributed matched the amount recorded.



Now her mother had given her a packet of fruit without a ledger.



The former Empress placed it carefully beside her walking shoes.



She would take it tomorrow.

That afternoon, Shen Mingzhao received a letter from the landlord of her temporary residence.



The rear drain had been cleaned.



The damaged board had been replaced.



The upper latch had been repaired.



The kitchen shelves had been adjusted.



The lower room remained damp after rain, and the landlord proposed observing it through another rainfall before deciding whether further work was necessary.



Mingzhao read the letter.



Qiao Yue stood beside the desk.



"Will the landlord repair the lower room?"



"After observing the next rain."



"That sounds like a reasonable answer."



"It sounds like an answer that requires another answer."



Qiao Yue considered this.



"Would you prefer that he repair it immediately?"



"Without knowing the cause?"



"Perhaps."



Mingzhao folded the letter.



"No. We will observe it."



Qiao Yue looked disappointed.



Mingzhao noticed.



"Why?"



"I thought you would demand that the landlord repair it today."



"I am not paying for an unnecessary repair."



"That is sensible."



Mingzhao glanced at her.



"Do not sound surprised."



Qiao Yue bowed her head.



"I would never."



The words were respectful.



The expression was not.



Mingzhao returned to her papers.



The northern canal property remained unresolved. Master Luo had not completed the boundary assessment, and the certified neighbouring registration had not arrived.



The estimated repairs remained substantial.



The roof alone required approximately one hundred and twenty taels.



Drainage would require another thirty.



The kitchen and well would add further costs.



Mingzhao had not paid a deposit.



She had not purchased the property.



She had not even decided whether the land would be worth acquiring if the boundary were confirmed.



For the first time in years, she could examine a property without being told that the family needed it, that the family had always owned it, or that she ought to manage it because she was capable.



She found the freedom refreshing.



She found the uncertainty expensive.



Both were true.

At the Imperial Palace, the Crown Prince Zhao Cheng'an completed the morning's stock reconciliation.



He had separated the oil entries by unit.



The jars recorded as purchased were not automatically treated as jars distributed.



The quantities issued by weight were not converted into jars merely because the older register had used a different format.



The duplicate entries had been marked for review.



The unresolved total remained unresolved.



He placed the corrected sheet before the clerk.



"Do not replace the earlier record," he said. "Attach this as a reconciliation."



The clerk bowed.



"Yes, Your Highness."



The Crown Prince looked at the two sheets.



The earlier record was untidy.



The newer one was clearer.



Neither was complete.



He had once believed that a clean account was a correct account.



Now he understood that a clean account could be wrong if someone had removed the inconvenient entries.



He ordered the original sheets preserved.



Then he noticed a small note at the bottom of the page.



It concerned a container whose unit had not been specified.



The clerk waited.



The Crown Prince read it twice.



"Leave it pending."



"Yes, Your Highness."



He set the paper aside.



The matter was not solved.



It was, however, no longer pretending to be solved.

By evening, the former Empress had received a final message from Gao Shun.



The pavilion would be ready.



The Emperor would arrive without ceremonial attendants beyond those necessary for ordinary service.



The Empress Dowager would attend.



Tea and fruit would be provided.



No reports would be brought.



Xu Yuru read the message.



Fang Ru stood nearby.



"Madam, shall I prepare the carriage?"



"Yes."



"Shall I ask whether the pavilion has cushions?"



"Only if you intend to sit on them."



Fang Ru lowered her head.



Xu Yuru folded the message.



Tomorrow, she would walk through the garden.



She would drink tea.



She would speak with the Emperor and the Empress Dowager.



She would not inspect the garden accounts.



She would not review the palace's supply arrangements.



She would not correct the attendants' seating order unless someone asked her a personal question about it.



The former Empress placed the message in a drawer.



Then she opened the drawer again.



Inside lay the small packet of dried fruit from her mother.



She took it out and put it beside the carriage arrangements.



Fang Ru watched.



"Madam?"



Xu Yuru looked at the packet.



"Tomorrow," she said, "I intend to eat fruit that was not purchased by the Imperial Household Administration."



Fang Ru bowed.



This was a declaration of independence that even the palace accounts could not dispute.



And somewhere in the eastern market, Shen Mingzhao was still waiting for a buyer to approve a folding method that saved one tenth of a handling period.



Neither woman had yet discovered that the simplest decisions in their new lives were becoming the most carefully documented.



The next day, the garden would be visited.



The workshop would continue working.



And the Great Yong Dynasty would once again be required to function without asking either woman to approve the fruit.
