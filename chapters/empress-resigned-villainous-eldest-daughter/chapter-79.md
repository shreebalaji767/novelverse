# **Chapter 79: The Former Empress Has Been Asked Whether She Would Like Another Book. The Marquis's Eldest Daughter Has Been Asked Whether She Would Like Another Contract. The Marquis Has Discovered That a Written Procedure Cannot Be Completed by Simply Asking His Daughter to Approve It.**

**13th Day of the Sixth Month, 18th Year of Yonghe.**



The morning began with a letter from the Xu residence.



It was addressed to the former Empress, written in Madam Xu's familiar hand, and delivered to the western guest residence shortly after breakfast.



Fang Ru placed it beside Yuru's tea.



“Your mother has written again, Madam.”



Yuru opened it.



The letter was brief. Madam Xu thanked her for the message about the book and mentioned that her husband had been pleased to hear that the section on provincial granaries had interested her.



She also asked whether Yuru would like another book.



Yuru read the final sentence twice.



Then she smiled.



Fang Ru noticed.



“Is there something amusing?”



“My father has apparently decided that returning one book is an invitation to lend me another.”



“Perhaps he has a large collection.”



“He does.”



“Then he may have been waiting for an excuse.”



Yuru placed the letter on the table.



Her father had always enjoyed discussing books. When she was younger, he had sometimes brought her a volume after dinner and asked her opinion the following morning. She had not always appreciated the habit at the time.



Now she found herself remembering it with affection.



“Perhaps I will borrow another when I visit.”



“Would you like me to prepare a reply?”



“No. I can write it myself.”



She took a fresh sheet of paper.



Her answer thanked her mother and said that she would be pleased to borrow another book during her next visit. She added that her father should not select something too difficult merely because he believed she had finished the previous one quickly.



Fang Ru read the sentence after Yuru had folded the letter.



“Your father may consider that a challenge.”



“He may.”



“Then he will certainly select something difficult.”



Yuru looked at her.



“That is why I included the warning.”



Fang Ru lowered her head to conceal a smile.



The letter was sent before midday.



The rest of the morning passed quietly. Yuru read for a while, then walked through the western courtyard with Fang Ru.



She did not need to go anywhere in particular.



The palace grounds were familiar, but the former Empress was learning to experience them without mentally arranging the duties attached to every building.



She passed a small service gate where two attendants were carrying baskets.



One of them greeted her respectfully.



Yuru returned the greeting and continued.



She did not ask what was in the baskets.



She did not ask which department had ordered them.



She did not ask whether the delivery had been entered into the appropriate register.



The attendants continued on their way.



The baskets reached their destination.



The palace did not collapse.

At the eastern market residence, Shen Mingzhao was receiving a visitor.



The visitor was not from the Marquisate.



It was a representative of a small trading house that had previously purchased silk through Lin Shuyun.



He had come to ask whether Mingzhao would consider another order after the current contract was completed.



Mingzhao listened without interrupting.



The proposed quantity was smaller than Zhou Wenqi's order, but the buyer wanted a shorter delivery period and a lower price.



The representative described the arrangement as an opportunity for a long-term partnership.



Mingzhao asked three questions.



First, how many bolts did the buyer require?



Second, what specifications would apply?



Third, when would payment be made?



The representative answered the first question clearly.



He was less precise about the second.



As for payment, he said the trading house generally settled accounts after receiving the goods.



Mingzhao placed her cup on the table.



“Generally?”



The man smiled.



“Our arrangements are usually quite flexible.”



“Then your payment schedule is not fixed.”



“We have never had serious difficulty with our partners.”



“That was not my question.”



The representative's smile weakened.



He tried again.



The trading house would pay a portion upon delivery and settle the remainder after resale.



Mingzhao asked how long resale usually took.



“That depends on the market.”



“And if the market is slow?”



“The payment may be delayed.”



“By how long?”



The man hesitated.



“Sometimes several weeks.”



Mingzhao looked at the proposed quantity.



A smaller order was not automatically a safer order. A short delivery period could disrupt existing production, and delayed payment could leave her paying wages while waiting for the buyer to sell the goods.



She was not interested in becoming an unsecured lender simply because someone had described the arrangement as a partnership.



“I cannot accept those terms.”



The representative looked surprised.



“You have not even discussed the price.”



“I have discussed the payment conditions.”



“We could negotiate the price.”



“That would not change the payment conditions.”



He tried to persuade her that the trading house had a respectable reputation.



Mingzhao did not dispute it.



“A respectable reputation is useful,” she said. “It does not replace a contract.”



The representative eventually understood that she would not accept the proposed arrangement.



He left with a polite promise to reconsider the terms.



Qiao Yue accompanied him to the door.



When she returned, she found Mingzhao examining the current workshop report.



“You refused another order?”



“I refused the payment arrangement.”



“The quantity was not large.”



“The risk was not small.”



Qiao Yue sat opposite her.



“Perhaps they will return with better terms.”



“If they do, I will consider them.”



“And if they do not?”



“Then they will find another supplier.”



Qiao Yue considered this.



“You are becoming difficult to persuade.”



“I was always difficult to persuade.”



“You used to be more willing to listen.”



“I still listened.”



“You used to discuss matters for longer.”



“I have learned that a conversation can become very long without becoming more useful.”



Qiao Yue laughed.



Mingzhao returned to her papers.



The current contract had priority.

The first twenty-eight bolts of the remaining production order had reached a new stage.



The two bolts requiring correction the previous day had been examined again.



One passed after the edge was adjusted.



The other required a second correction because the fold had become uneven during the first attempt.



The material remained usable.



The workshop supervisor recorded the correction and identified the cause: the worker had applied too much pressure at the edge while trying to make the fold appear sharper.



Mingzhao read the report.



“Was the worker one of the trainees?”



“No,” Qiao Yue replied. “An experienced finisher.”



Mingzhao looked up.



“An experienced finisher?”



“Yes.”



“Then why was the pressure incorrect?”



“The supervisor believes he was trying to speed up the work.”



“Did he change the approved method?”



“No. He followed the written instructions but rushed the final stage.”



Mingzhao read the report again.



This was a different problem from a trainee misunderstanding the instructions.



The method had been approved. The sample had been accepted. The worker knew what was required.



He had simply decided that he could complete it faster.



“Have the supervisor speak with him,” Mingzhao said. “The instruction should remain unchanged. He must complete the same inspection as everyone else.”



Qiao Yue nodded.



“Should his name be entered?”



“Yes. The correction record should identify who performed the work and who checked it.”



“Some workers may dislike having their names recorded beside mistakes.”



“Then they should avoid making mistakes.”



Qiao Yue gave her a look.



“That is not entirely realistic.”



“No. But recording them is.”



The first group of twenty-eight was now internally accepted.



All twenty-eight had passed inspection after the necessary corrections.



They were not yet buyer-accepted.



The second group of twenty-eight had entered finishing.



If the schedule held, the workshop expected to complete its internal inspection the following day.



Mingzhao ordered the production sheet updated.



The total remained unchanged: one hundred and forty-four bolts accepted by the buyer, twenty-eight internally completed and awaiting buyer inspection, and twenty-eight still in production.



The order was close to completion.



Close was not the same as complete.

In the Marquisate, Liang Wen placed a revised purchasing procedure before Shen Mingyuan.



It was longer than the previous draft.



Mingyuan read the first page carefully.



The procedure now separated five responsibilities:

- The authorizing official approved the expenditure.
- The purchasing clerk placed the order.
- The receiving steward recorded the quantity and condition of goods delivered.
- The accounts clerk verified the supporting documents before payment.
- The register keeper maintained the consolidated record.

The document also described what should happen when deliveries were incomplete, goods were rejected, quantities differed from the order, or an entry required correction.



Mingyuan reached the section concerning corrections.



He stopped.



“Liang Wen.”



“Yes, Young Master?”



“You have written that an incorrect entry should be corrected by the responsible clerk and verified by the supervising steward.”



“Yes.”



“What happens if the responsible clerk has left the office?”



“The supervising steward can authorize another clerk to make the correction.”



“Can the second clerk alter the original entry?”



“No. The original must remain visible.”



“Where is that written?”



Liang Wen turned to the previous page.



“Here.”



Mingyuan read the paragraph.



It was there, but the wording could be clearer.



He pointed to the sentence.



“Add that the original clerk's identity must remain recorded even if another person enters the correction.”



Liang Wen considered it.



“That is reasonable.”



“And if the original clerk cannot be identified?”



“Then the entry should be marked as unidentified, and the correction should be supported by the available documents.”



Mingyuan nodded.



Steward Zhou looked over the page.



“You have become very fond of the word unidentified.”



“I have found it useful.”



“Some people dislike it.”



“Some people dislike accurate accounts.”



Liang Wen lowered his head and continued writing.



Mingyuan read the remaining sections.



There was still a problem with the procedure for urgent purchases. The draft allowed an urgent order to proceed before the full authorization form was completed, provided that the supervising steward approved it.



Mingyuan tapped the page.



“What counts as urgent?”



Liang Wen hesitated.



“An immediate household requirement.”



“That is too broad.”



Steward Zhou agreed.



“If every department calls its purchase urgent, the rule will become useless.”



Mingyuan looked at Liang Wen.



“Give examples. Necessary repairs after damage, essential supplies required to prevent interruption, or another matter that cannot reasonably wait for ordinary approval. Then require a written explanation and confirmation afterward.”



Liang Wen began making notes.



The procedure was becoming longer.



Mingyuan looked at the growing pile of pages.



“Perhaps we should have written a simpler document.”



Steward Zhou answered calmly.



“A simpler document would have been easier to finish.”



“And less useful.”



“Possibly.”



Mingyuan sighed.



He had once believed that administration consisted largely of telling people what to do.



Now he was discovering that telling people what to do was the easiest part.

The search for the unclear payment continued.



In the general purchasing archive, the clerks had located several bundles from the relevant period.



The records were arranged by category, not by the name of the person who had authorized a transaction.



That made the search slower.



One bundle contained purchases of household materials. Another concerned repairs. A third included payments to several outside suppliers.



None had yet been matched conclusively to the unclear entry.



Mingyuan examined the list.



“Have we found a payment with the same amount?”



“Yes,” Steward Zhou replied. “There are two entries with matching amounts.”



“Same date?”



“No. One is several days earlier. The other is from the following month.”



“Same supplier?”



“Not established.”



Mingyuan placed the list down.



“Then neither is sufficient.”



“Correct.”



“Have the original vouchers been checked?”



“The clerks are examining them.”



Mingyuan nodded.



He was tempted to make a connection.



The matching amounts were unusual enough to attract attention. If one belonged to the unclear payment, it might identify the recipient.



But a matching amount did not prove that two entries described the same transaction.



He had already learned that authorization did not prove receipt, and receipt did not prove delivery.



Now he was learning that matching numbers did not prove identity.



“Continue the search,” he said. “Do not combine the entries merely because the amounts are the same.”



Steward Zhou nodded.



Mingyuan returned to the cash books.



The estate had many records.



It had fewer explanations.

In the western guest residence, Yuru received another small note from the palace library.



It concerned the book that Third Princess Minglan had been examining.



The note stated that the library staff had checked the remaining catalogues available in the palace collection. None matched the book's full text.



The princess had therefore retained its temporary classification as unidentified.



Yuru read the note and placed it aside.



She was pleased that Minglan had not forced a conclusion merely to complete the catalogue.



It was a small matter, but a useful one.



A book did not acquire a title simply because someone wanted the shelf to look orderly.



Yuru did not send instructions.



She did not ask for further reports.



Minglan had already made the appropriate decision.



That was enough.



Fang Ru noticed the note.



“Has the princess found the book?”



“No.”



“Will she continue searching?”



“She may. But she has correctly recorded that it has not been identified.”



“Would you like to send her a reply?”



“Not today.”



Fang Ru nodded.



Yuru turned to the book she had been reading.



The palace library had its own staff, and the princess was learning how to use them without making their work dependent on one person's memory.



Yuru considered that a good development.



She returned to her reading.

By late afternoon, Mingzhao received the revised production report.



The first group of twenty-eight had passed internal inspection.



The second group was still in finishing, with its internal examination expected the following day.



The workshop had recorded the correction made by the experienced finisher and instructed him to follow the approved method without rushing the final stage.



Mingzhao read the report and signed the acknowledgment.



Qiao Yue brought another paper.



“The purchasing procedure from the Marquisate has not arrived.”



“I did not expect it today.”



“Your father may be waiting for you to ask.”



“He knows what I agreed to review.”



“Perhaps he wants to speak with you directly.”



“Then he can write what he wishes to discuss.”



Qiao Yue looked at her.



“Would you refuse to meet him?”



“No. I would refuse to attend a meeting whose purpose changes after I arrive.”



“That is a very specific refusal.”



“It is based on experience.”



Qiao Yue did not argue.



Mingzhao put the production report away.



The Marquisate's administrative transition was no longer her responsibility.



Her own business was progressing, though not without corrections and delays. She had refused a new contract because the payment terms were unsuitable. She had paid the workshop workers. She had recorded the expenses. She had made sure the current buyer's requirements were being followed.



These matters were not effortless.



But she understood them.



She knew where the money had gone and what remained to be done.



That was more than she could say about several years of managing the Marquisate's affairs.

That evening, the Marquis of Qingyuan asked whether the revised procedure had been completed.



Liang Wen answered honestly.



“It is close, my lord. The section on urgent purchases requires further clarification.”



Shen Guowei looked at the pages.



“How much further clarification can one procedure require?”



Liang Wen lowered his head.



“Enough to prevent disputes later.”



The Marquis frowned.



He had hoped the matter would be settled quickly.



He had imagined that Mingzhao would read the document, make a few corrections, and perhaps explain the arrangements to the household staff.



It had seemed reasonable when he first proposed it.



Now the procedure had become a substantial document, and Mingyuan was insisting that each responsibility be clearly assigned.



Shen Guowei looked at his son.



“Does this need to be so complicated?”



Mingyuan answered carefully.



“It does not need to be complicated. It needs to be clear.”



“Your sister managed these affairs without so many written conditions.”



Mingyuan paused.



“She also spent years answering questions that should have been answered by the people responsible for each stage.”



The Marquis's expression changed.



Mingyuan continued.



“If the procedure is unclear, the same disputes will return. If it is clear, the clerks will know what they are expected to do.”



Shen Guowei looked at the document again.



He could not deny the logic.



But he was beginning to understand that the transition would not be completed merely by asking Mingzhao to approve it.



His daughter had already left.



The family had to learn how to proceed without her.



“Finish the draft,” he said at last. “Then send her a copy.”



Mingyuan nodded.



He would do so.



The Marquis rose and left the room.



Liang Wen gathered his papers.



Steward Zhou remained seated.



“Your father wanted this finished quickly,” he said.



“He still does.”



“And now?”



“Now he knows that it will take as long as it takes.”



Steward Zhou looked at the unfinished document.



“Your sister would have said the same thing.”



Mingyuan did not answer immediately.



Then he said, “She would have said it more sharply.”



Steward Zhou allowed himself a small smile.



“That is true.”

The former Empress ended the day with a book, a cup of tea, and a letter from her mother.



The Marquis's eldest daughter ended it with a production report and a contract she had refused.



The palace library retained one unidentified book.



The Marquisate retained one unidentified payment.



The workshop retained no defective material from the day's corrections.



And the purchasing procedure remained unfinished.



In the past, Yuru and Mingzhao might both have been expected to resolve such matters personally.



One had been responsible for the inner palace.



The other had been responsible for a large aristocratic household.



Neither position had been designed to encourage the people around them to become independent.



Now the palace was learning to function without its former manager, and the Marquisate was learning that its eldest daughter had not been born with an unlimited supply of time.



The difference between the two households was becoming increasingly obvious.



The palace had begun to distribute responsibility.



The Marquisate had begun to write down why it had failed to do so.



Neither achievement was complete.



But at least one of them had stopped sending its problems to the woman who had already resigned.
