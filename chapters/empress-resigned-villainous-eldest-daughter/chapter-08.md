# **Chapter 8: The Brooms Have Been Counted Twice. The Marquis's Accounts Have Been Counted Three Times. Nobody Is Happier.**

**30th Day of the Third Month, 18th Year of Yonghe.**

The palace had reached a dangerous stage of administrative reform.

People had begun bringing documents voluntarily.

This was dangerous because, until recently, most officials had believed that a document became someone else's responsibility as soon as it crossed the threshold of their office.

Now the emperor wanted to know who had written it, who had checked it, who had approved it, and who would answer for it if the numbers did not agree.

The result was an alarming increase in paper.

At the Department of Palace Stores, an assistant clerk named Luo had spent most of the previous evening arranging three sets of broom records.

He had not been instructed to do so.

He had done it because the emperor had asked for an explanation, and because Minister Lu had made it very clear that no one should bring another report that merely said the matter had been investigated.

At dawn, Luo placed the records before his supervisor.

His supervisor examined them.

Then he examined Luo.

"How many brooms?"

"According to the purchase register, one thousand two hundred and forty."

"According to the distribution register?"

"One thousand one hundred and ninety-six."

"And according to the inventory?"

"None."

The supervisor slowly closed the register.

"That is not an explanation."

"No, sir."

"Where are the remaining forty-four?"

Luo hesitated.

"We have found a separate record."

"Then why did you not put it here?"

"Because it was kept by the repair office."

The supervisor stared at him.

Luo lowered his head.

The palace had discovered that a broom could travel through three offices without anyone being able to say where it had gone.

The supervisor stood.

"Bring it."

At Chengming Palace, Empress Xu Yuru was having breakfast.

She had specifically requested that nobody bring administrative documents to the breakfast table.

The request had been obeyed.

Instead, a palace attendant stood beside her with a small wooden tray containing a sealed report.

Yuru looked at it.

The attendant looked at her.

Neither spoke.

Finally, Yuru put down her spoon.

"Is it urgent?"

"The Department of Palace Stores has sent it, Your Majesty."

"That was not my question."

The attendant lowered her head.

"They said it concerns the broom records."

Yuru closed her eyes briefly.

"Put it on the side table."

"Yes, Your Majesty."

She returned to her breakfast.

The report remained on the side table, where it could not be mistaken for an invitation to work.

Her senior administrator, Fang Ru, entered a moment later.

"Your Majesty, the Crown Prince's household has submitted its preliminary review."

"Has he identified the office responsible for receiving the report?"

"Yes. The Eastern Palace administration sent it to the designated review office rather than to Chengming Palace."

"Good."

"He also requested that the review office clarify the procedure for damaged household goods."

"That is reasonable."

Fang Ru hesitated.

"The Second Prince has submitted a proposed division of household expenditure."

Yuru looked up.

"Has he distinguished personal expenditure from household expenditure?"

"He has."

"Has he defined who approves exceptional purchases?"

"He has proposed that the steward approve ordinary purchases and that exceptional expenses require his written authorization."

"Has he defined exceptional?"

"No."

"Then he has proposed a distinction without defining the distinction."

Fang Ru smiled faintly.

"The Department of Revenue made the same observation."

"Then the Department of Revenue has earned its salary today."

Fang Ru placed the two reports on the table.

Yuru looked at the first page of the Crown Prince's review.

The Crown Prince had identified three problems: unclear reserve usage, incomplete clothing inventory, and inconsistent lamp oil records.

He had not accused anyone of stealing.

He had asked for the original documents.

That was progress.

It was also the sort of progress that made officials uncomfortable, because an accusation could be denied, but a request for records required someone to find them.

"Send a note to the Crown Prince," Yuru said. "Tell him the review is useful. He should continue, but he must not allow the examination of household records to interfere with the ordinary duties of the Eastern Palace."

"Yes, Your Majesty."

"And tell him that if the designated review office requires clarification of the regulations, it should submit the question through the proper channel."

Fang Ru nodded.

Yuru looked at the Second Prince's proposal.

"He has improved."

"He was unhappy yesterday."

"Being unhappy does not prevent a person from learning."

Fang Ru's expression became slightly more cautious.

"Your Majesty, there is another matter."

"Go on."

"The Empress Dowager has requested to see you."

Yuru set down the paper.

"Has she said why?"

"She asked about the petition."

The empress was silent for a moment.

Then she resumed her breakfast.

"After the morning audience, I will go to her palace."

Fang Ru bowed.

The wooden tray remained untouched.

At the Hall of Mental Cultivation, Emperor Zhao Jingheng was reviewing the same report that Yuru had deliberately left unopened.

The report concerned the broom records.

Minister Lu stood before the desk, while Wei Zhong sat to one side with a second document.

The emperor read the summary twice.

"Forty-four brooms."

"Yes, Your Majesty," Minister Lu replied.

"Purchased, but not included in the ordinary distribution register."

"That is what the records currently indicate."

"Where were they?"

"The Department of Palace Stores believes they were issued to the temporary repair teams."

The emperor looked at him.

"Believes?"

"The repair office has maintained a separate record."

"Then why was it not included in the original report?"

Minister Lu's mouth tightened.

"The two offices use different inventory categories."

"That is an explanation for the discrepancy. It is not an explanation for why the categories were never reconciled."

Wei Zhong lowered his eyes.

He had learned that the emperor was particularly dangerous when he was not shouting.

Minister Lu continued.

"The repair office records tools and consumable materials under maintenance expenditure. The Palace Stores records them under household supplies."

"Who decided that?"

"The distinction appears to have existed for several years."

"That was not what I asked."

Minister Lu paused.

"The original authorization has not yet been located."

The emperor leaned back.

"So the brooms were purchased by one office, issued by another, recorded by a third, and their classification was established by nobody who can presently be identified."

Wei Zhong folded his hands.

"Your Majesty, the records suggest that the forty-four were distributed to repair crews during the maintenance of the eastern corridors and the southern kitchens. Some were later discarded."

"How many?"

"Twenty-seven are listed as worn out. Seventeen are listed as issued."

"Then why does the inventory say none?"

"Because the repair office recorded the materials as consumed upon issue."

The emperor looked at the document again.

"Seventeen issued. Twenty-seven discarded."

"Yes, Your Majesty."

"Do the records establish that the seventeen were actually used?"

"They establish that they were issued to named repair teams. They do not establish whether all seventeen were used."

"Do the records establish that the twenty-seven were discarded?"

"They establish that the repair office recorded them as discarded. There are no separate disposal certificates."

The emperor put the report down.

"Then we know where the discrepancy probably came from. We do not know whether the disposal record is accurate."

Minister Lu nodded.

"That is correct."

"Do not turn a likely explanation into a proven conclusion."

Wei Zhong looked at the emperor.

"Your Majesty, the Department of Palace Stores proposes that future purchases be recorded under one common inventory number, with separate entries for ordinary distribution, repair use, and disposal."

"Who will maintain the common register?"

"The Palace Stores office."

"Who will verify it?"

"An assigned clerk from the review office."

"Who will authorize the disposal?"

"That remains under discussion."

The emperor looked at him.

"Then it remains under discussion."

Wei Zhong bowed.

The emperor picked up the next document.

"At least this time, the brooms have been found."

Minister Lu was not sure whether that was praise.

He decided not to ask.

The Empress Dowager's palace was quiet.

It was a different kind of quiet from Chengming Palace.

There were fewer documents, fewer attendants moving in and out, and fewer people pretending not to listen to conversations.

Empress Dowager Wei sat beside a low table.

She was sixty-four, and had spent most of her life observing the difference between what people said and what they wanted.

When Xu Yuru entered, she looked at the empress for a long moment.

"You have grown thinner."

Yuru bowed.

"Your Majesty's concern is appreciated."

"Do not answer me like an official."

Yuru straightened.

"Then I will say that I have been sleeping less."

"Because of the petition?"

"Partly."

The Empress Dowager gestured to the seat opposite her.

"Sit."

Yuru sat.

Tea was poured.

For a while, neither woman spoke.

Then the Empress Dowager asked, "Do you truly intend to relinquish the empress's position?"

Yuru looked at her.

"I have submitted a formal petition."

"I can read."

"Then Your Majesty already knows my intention."

The Empress Dowager's eyebrows rose slightly.

"You have become rather direct."

"I have spent several years answering questions that could have been answered by reading the documents placed before me."

The older woman almost smiled.

Almost.

"An empress is not merely the manager of a household."

"I know."

"Her position carries duties."

"I know."

"Her responsibilities cannot be abandoned because the work is tiring."

Yuru lifted her teacup.

"I am not proposing to abandon the palace."

"Then what are you proposing?"

"That the palace stop depending on my personal intervention for every routine matter."

The Empress Dowager watched her.

"That sounds like a distinction made by someone who has already decided to leave."

"It is a distinction made by someone who has spent years discovering that a great many matters have no clear destination unless they are sent to my desk."

"That is because you have managed them well."

"That is also why they have not learned to manage themselves."

The Empress Dowager placed her cup down.

"You believe the palace can function without you?"

"It should."

"That is not the same answer."

Yuru considered this.

"No. It is not."

She looked at the tea.

"I believe the palace can function without my personal approval of every purchase, every repair, every allocation, and every dispute between offices. Whether it can function without me in the position of empress is a different question. That is why I have asked for a formal review rather than simply leaving."

The Empress Dowager did not immediately reply.

She had expected resentment, perhaps exhaustion, perhaps a private complaint about the emperor.

Instead, Yuru had brought her a problem of institutional design.

"You have been empress for many years," the older woman said.

"Eighteen."

"You have managed the inner palace through difficult years. You have handled the princes' households, ceremonies, appointments, and disputes. Do you think the palace will thank you by making your departure easy?"

"No."

"Do you think the emperor will agree?"

"I do not know."

"Then why begin?"

Yuru met her gaze.

"Because if I wait until everyone agrees that I may stop, I will never stop."

The Empress Dowager was silent.

Outside, a maid announced that a medicine tray had been brought for the Dowager's afternoon use.

The Empress Dowager glanced toward the door.

"Put it on the side table."

The maid withdrew.

Yuru's eyes briefly followed the tray.

The Empress Dowager noticed.

"Do not start examining my medicine accounts."

"I had no intention of doing so."

"You looked at it."

"I was looking at the tray."

"That is how it begins."

For the first time, Yuru smiled.

The Empress Dowager shook her head.

"Your petition will be considered. But do not mistake my willingness to hear you for agreement."

"I would not."

"And do not make the palace collapse merely to prove your point."

"I have no intention of doing that."

"Good."

The older woman picked up her tea.

"Then perhaps you should begin by ensuring that the Crown Prince understands what he is being asked to inherit."

Yuru lowered her eyes.

"That is already being done."

"Not only the throne."

The empress looked up.

"The household?"

"The people. The responsibilities. The arrangements that have continued because nobody wished to disturb them."

Yuru considered this.

"I will speak with him."

The Empress Dowager nodded.

"Then we understand each other."

They did not.

But they had reached a point at which disagreement could be discussed without pretending it did not exist.

For the moment, that was sufficient.

At the Qingyuan Marquisate, Sun Qiming had reached the stage of his examination where he no longer trusted anyone who used the phrase *it should be somewhere*.

The phrase had appeared in seven conversations.

It had not produced a single receipt.

He sat in a side room with three account books, two bundles of work orders, and a list of payments related to the northern warehouse.

Shen Mingzhao stood across from him.

Qiao Yue was arranging the papers according to the accountant's instructions.

Steward Zhou sat nearby, his expression increasingly troubled.

Sun Qiming adjusted his spectacles.

"The northern warehouse discrepancy is not yet a confirmed loss."

"I know," Mingzhao replied.

"The dispatch records show that forty-seven sacks were entered as leaving the main granary."

"Yes."

"The receiving estate has now supplied copies of two entries."

He opened a separate ledger.

"The ninth month records eighteen sacks received. The tenth month records fourteen."

"That accounts for thirty-two."

"Correct."

"And the remaining fifteen?"

"Not yet established."

Mingzhao looked at the copies.

"Do the dates match the dispatch records?"

"Not exactly."

"How far apart?"

"The ninth-month entry was recorded three days after the dispatch date. The tenth-month entry was recorded on the same day."

"Could the first difference be due to delayed recording?"

"It could."

"Could the remaining fifteen have been sent in a separate delivery?"

"It could."

"Could they have been entered under another warehouse account?"

"It could."

Mingzhao looked at him.

"That is a great many possibilities."

"Yes."

"And none of them is a conclusion."

"That is correct."

Steward Zhou cleared his throat.

"Miss, the northern warehouse was managed by a different steward at the time. He may have used a separate delivery register."

"Then we should obtain it."

"The former steward has retired to his home in the western district."

"Send someone to ask for the original register."

Steward Zhou nodded.

Sun Qiming turned another page.

"There is another difficulty. The receiving copies are not originals. They bear the receiving clerk's seal, but the dispatch office has not yet confirmed that the copies match its own entries."

"Then request confirmation."

"I have already drafted the request."

He handed it to her.

Mingzhao read it carefully.

The wording was plain, precise, and free of accusations.

She nodded.

"Send it."

Qiao Yue took the document.

Sun Qiming then turned to the next bundle.

"The three hundred taels charged to the Second Young Master's residence remain unsupported."

Mingzhao did not look surprised.

"Has he replied?"

"His written statement says that the money was used for necessary household matters. He has now supplied a list of possible expenditures."

"Possible?"

"He does not remember the exact dates."

"Then the list is a recollection, not evidence."

"That is how I have marked it."

Sun Qiming placed the list before her.

It included repairs to the study, furniture, a gathering for several guests, and a payment to an unnamed carpenter.

Mingzhao read the last entry twice.

"Who was the carpenter?"

"Second Young Master says he believes the payment was made through the steward."

"Which steward?"

"He does not specify."

"Then it cannot be verified."

Sun Qiming nodded.

"That is my assessment."

Steward Zhou looked uncomfortable.

"Miss, some of these matters may have been paid in cash. Receipts were not always issued for small work."

"Three hundred taels is not a small amount."

"No."

"Nor is a room repair necessarily a personal expense. We must establish what was done, who authorized it, and whether the work belongs to the estate or to his private residence."

Sun Qiming nodded again.

"That distinction is important. The account should not automatically charge the Second Young Master merely because his rooms benefited. If the work was an authorized estate repair, it may belong under estate maintenance. If it was a personal improvement, the classification may be different."

Mingzhao returned the list.

"Continue examining it."

Sun Qiming gathered the pages.

"One more thing. I would like to speak directly with the former steward who managed the northern warehouse."

"Do so."

"I will need the family to provide a travel allowance."

"Put it under the examination expenses."

"Very well."

Mingzhao glanced at the account book.

"Do not let the family classify it as a personal gift to you."

Sun Qiming looked up.

"I would not."

"Good. I dislike explaining the same transaction twice."

Qiao Yue lowered her head to hide a smile.

Sun Qiming did not smile.

He had begun to understand that Miss Shen did not consider repetition a form of conversation.

Shen Mingyuan was having a considerably less pleasant morning.

He had received a copy of the accountant's request concerning the three hundred taels.

He had also received a letter from his father.

The letter was brief.

It instructed him to provide whatever information he could remember and to stop treating the matter as an insult.

Mingyuan placed the letter on his desk.

His younger sister, Shen Mingxiu, sat by the window.

"You have been staring at that letter for a long time," she said.

"It is an irritating letter."

"Father's letters are usually irritating."

"He expects me to remember every expense from months ago."

Mingxiu turned a page of the book she was reading.

"Do you remember the expenses?"

"Of course I remember spending the money."

"Then perhaps you should write them down."

"I have."

"You wrote that the money was used for necessary household matters."

Mingyuan looked at her.

"Have you come to lecture me?"

"No. I came to ask whether the carpenter who repaired your study was paid from the estate account."

He frowned.

"I do not know."

"Did you ask him?"

"Why would I?"

"Because your sister is asking for the records."

Mingyuan leaned back.

"She has made this into a trial."

"Has she accused you of stealing?"

"No."

"Then what is the problem?"

"The problem is that she treats every missing receipt as if it is my fault."

Mingxiu closed her book.

"She has treated every missing receipt as a missing receipt. That is not the same thing."

He looked at her sharply.

"You are taking her side?"

"I am taking the side of not being asked to explain three hundred taels without knowing where they went."

Mingyuan's expression darkened.

Mingxiu stood.

"I am not defending her manner. She can make a simple question sound like a judgment from the Ministry of Justice. But if you know who received the money, say so. If you do not know, say that."

"That is easy for you to say."

"Yes. It is."

She walked toward the door.

Before leaving, she paused.

"Also, if you find the carpenter's name, tell me. I remember seeing someone working in your study."

Mingyuan looked up.

"When?"

"Last winter, perhaps. I cannot remember the exact month."

"That is hardly useful."

"It is more useful than pretending I know."

She left.

Mingyuan remained at his desk.

After a while, he called his attendant.

"Find the old repair ledger."

At the same time, Mingzhao was discovering that leaving the family estate would require more money than she had initially wanted to acknowledge.

The northern canal property had clear title.

It also had a roof that needed repairs, drainage channels that required work, and a kitchen that was too small for the household she intended to establish.

The eastern district property had better rooms and a more convenient location.

Its title documents, however, were incomplete.

A broker had sent a preliminary estimate for the northern house.

Mingzhao spread the paper beside her personal account.

Qiao Yue stood behind her.

"The broker says the roof repairs can be completed before the summer rains."

"At what cost?"

"One hundred and twenty taels, if the timber is sound."

"If."

"He says the kitchen drainage may require another thirty."

"If."

Qiao Yue looked at the paper.

"He has also listed a reserve of twenty taels for unexpected work."

"That is the only honest line in the estimate."

Qiao Yue laughed.

Mingzhao did not.

She was writing down expenses.

Purchase price.

Title verification.

Roof repairs.

Drainage.

Kitchen repairs.

Furniture.

Fuel.

Food.

Wages.

Clothing for servants.

Transport.

Lighting oil.

Maintenance.

Emergency reserve.

She stopped.

Then she added another line.

Administrative expenses.

Qiao Yue looked over her shoulder.

"What is that?"

"The cost of running a household."

"Is that not included in the wages?"

"Not all of it. A household needs account books, writing materials, seals, storage, deliveries, and people who can verify what has been purchased."

"Could we not manage without so many people?"

"We could. That does not mean we should manage badly."

Qiao Yue considered this.

"How many servants are you planning to employ?"

"Fewer than the Marquisate."

"That is not a number."

"One senior housekeeper, two general attendants, a cook, and a porter. I may need additional help during deliveries."

"That is still more than I expected."

"I am not opening a hermitage."

Qiao Yue looked at the account.

"Then the northern house may cost more than we thought."

"Of course it will."

"Should we consider the eastern property again?"

Mingzhao shook her head.

"Not until the title is clear."

"The broker says the seller is willing to negotiate."

"The seller is willing to negotiate because the title is incomplete."

"That may be true."

"It is certainly true."

She placed the eastern property's documents in a separate folder.

"I will not purchase a house and spend the next three years discovering that somebody else's cousin has a claim to its eastern courtyard."

Qiao Yue nodded.

Mingzhao returned to her budget.

The figure at the bottom was larger than she liked.

It was also more realistic than the figure she had first imagined.

She crossed out the initial total and began again.

For six years, she had managed a household whose resources were not her own. She had known the costs of grain, repairs, wages, and seasonal purchases. Yet she had rarely been required to distinguish her own personal expenses from those of the estate.

Now every tael had a destination.

And every destination had a cost.

She looked at the final estimate.

"Send the broker a request for a detailed repair inspection."

"Yes, Miss."

"And ask whether the house can be occupied safely before the roof work is complete."

Qiao Yue blinked.

"You are still considering it?"

"I am considering the facts."

"That sounds less decisive than usual."

"It is more expensive than usual."

In the afternoon, Lin Shuyun arrived at the Marquisate.

She was dressed in a dark blue robe, with her hair pinned simply and a small ledger tucked beneath one arm.

She had not come to offer comfort.

She had come to discuss a commercial decision.

Mingzhao received her in the side study.

"You received my letter?" Lin asked.

"I did."

"The buyer has repeated his inquiry."

"Who is he?"

"A merchant operating between Yongjing and the southern river ports. His name is Zhou Wenqi. He trades in finished silk and woven cloth, mostly through established wholesalers."

"Does he intend to buy the entire warehouse stock?"

"He has asked about it."

"That is not an answer."

Lin opened her ledger.

"He wants an estimated quantity, a delivery schedule, and a price range. He has not made a formal offer."

"How did he hear about the stock?"

"Through one of our existing buyers. He knows the warehouse has accumulated several batches of finished silk."

"Does he know our purchase costs?"

"Not from me."

Mingzhao looked at her.

"Could he obtain them elsewhere?"

"Possibly. Several suppliers know the approximate market price, but not our exact costs."

"Then we should not provide him with our internal account."

Lin nodded.

"I have not."

Mingzhao studied the ledger.

"What is the current stock?"

"After excluding the goods already reserved for existing customers, we have three hundred and eighty bolts available for negotiation."

"How many are ready for immediate delivery?"

"Two hundred and ten."

"And the rest?"

"One hundred and seventy require finishing checks and repacking."

"How long?"

"About ten days, assuming no delay in the dyeing workshop."

Mingzhao considered the figures.

"What payment terms does he expect?"

"That is what I want to establish before making an offer."

"Advance payment?"

"He has not said."

"Then we should not promise delivery dates until we know whether he is prepared to pay a deposit."

Lin closed the ledger.

"I agree."

Mingzhao leaned back.

"Do you want to sell the entire available stock?"

"I want to know whether the price is worthwhile. Our storage expenses are rising, and keeping the stock for another season is not necessarily profitable."

"Then calculate the cost of holding it."

"I have."

"Include storage, insurance, deterioration risk, and the opportunity cost of keeping the money tied up."

Lin raised an eyebrow.

"You have become very fond of that last phrase."

"It is a useful phrase."

"It is also a phrase that makes merchants dislike accountants."

"Merchants dislike accountants when the accountants are correct."

Lin laughed.

She took a sheet from her ledger.

"I have prepared three possible quantities. One hundred bolts, two hundred, or the entire three hundred and eighty."

Mingzhao read the figures.

The smaller sale would provide cash without disturbing existing arrangements. The larger sale would reduce storage costs but would require a lower unit price.

"Do not accept the entire sale merely because the buyer asks for it," Mingzhao said.

"I had no intention of doing so."

"Then why bring it?"

"Because I want to know whether you would support a larger sale if the price were favorable."

Mingzhao looked up.

"I would support a sale that benefits the partnership. I would not support a sale that leaves us unable to fulfill existing obligations."

"Good."

Lin put the ledger away.

"Then I will request a formal offer with a proposed deposit, delivery schedule, and inspection terms."

"Have him identify the destination warehouse."

"That is reasonable."

"And do not allow his men to inspect the entire stock without supervision."

Lin smiled.

"You are treating this as if he were already trying to cheat us."

"I am treating it as if he is a merchant who wants to buy our goods at the lowest possible price."

"That is a more charitable interpretation."

"It is also more accurate."

Lin stood.

"Then we have an agreement."

"We have an agreement to obtain an offer."

"An important distinction."

Mingzhao nodded.

Lin left with her ledger.

The partnership had not made a sale.

It had, however, taken one step toward making a decision based on figures rather than enthusiasm.

Mingzhao considered that a satisfactory afternoon.

That evening, the emperor summoned the Crown Prince.

Zhao Cheng'an arrived carrying a folder.

The emperor looked at it.

"Is that your household review?"

"Yes, Father."

"Have you completed it?"

"No."

"Good."

The Crown Prince placed the folder on the desk.

"I have reviewed the grain, clothing, and lamp oil records. The figures do not reconcile in several places, but I have not yet received all the original registers."

"What have you concluded?"

"That the Eastern Palace has insufficiently defined procedures for recording reserve usage and damaged goods."

"Have you found theft?"

"No."

"Have you found waste?"

"Possibly, but I cannot yet distinguish waste from poor recordkeeping."

The emperor nodded.

"Then do not write that you have found either."

"I have not."

"Good."

The Crown Prince hesitated.

"There is another issue."

"Speak."

"The Eastern Palace attendants have asked whether the review will change their duties."

"Will it?"

"Not immediately. I intend to keep existing arrangements until the examination is complete."

"That is sensible."

The emperor picked up the folder.

"Do you know why I asked you to examine your own household?"

"Because the palace administration should not depend on the Empress's office for routine matters."

"That is one reason."

The emperor looked at his son.

"Another is that you will eventually inherit more than the throne. You will inherit offices, officials, and arrangements that may have existed longer than you have been alive. Some will be useful. Some will be poorly designed. Some will have continued because nobody wanted to ask who was responsible."

The Crown Prince lowered his eyes.

"I understand."

"Do you?"

"I am beginning to."

The emperor returned the folder.

"Continue."

The Crown Prince accepted it.

Before he left, the emperor added, "And do not turn every household disagreement into an investigation."

"I will remember that."

"Your younger brother has already made that mistake."

The Crown Prince paused.

"Second Brother?"

"He tried to classify a disagreement over ceremonial seating as an administrative failure."

The Crown Prince's expression remained composed.

"Was it?"

"No. It was a disagreement over ceremonial seating."

The Crown Prince bowed.

The emperor watched him leave.

For a moment, he looked toward the empty seat beside his desk.

Yuru would ordinarily have reviewed the Crown Prince's report before it reached him.

She would have marked the unclear sections, identified the offices involved, and perhaps written a short note explaining which matters required his attention.

He had become accustomed to that.

He had also become accustomed to believing that the arrangement was natural.

Now, with every report arriving through a different channel, he was discovering how much of the palace's order had depended on one woman's ability to remember what everyone else had forgotten.

He did not particularly enjoy the discovery.

At Chengming Palace, Yuru was reviewing the latest draft of the responsibilities register.

Fang Ru stood beside her.

The register now distinguished between matters governed by existing regulations, matters requiring clarification, and informal practices that had no written authority.

Yuru turned a page.

"Who is responsible for the annual kitchen inspection?"

"The palace kitchens conduct routine inspections. The senior administrator verifies the results."

"Does the regulation say that?"

"No. It is an established practice."

"Then mark it as such."

Fang Ru wrote a note.

"And the replacement of household utensils?"

"Department of Palace Stores."

"Who determines whether an item should be replaced?"

"The receiving office."

"Who verifies that it is damaged?"

"The administrator of the relevant residence."

"Who checks that the replacement has been received?"

"The stores office."

Yuru looked at her.

"Then why does the request come to me?"

Fang Ru was silent.

Yuru placed the document down.

"That is the question we must answer."

Fang Ru looked at the growing register.

"Your Majesty, if every responsibility is divided this way, the palace will require a great many procedures."

"Perhaps. But not every procedure needs to be new. Some may already exist. We have to find them."

"And if they do not?"

"Then we decide whether they are necessary."

Fang Ru nodded.

Yuru glanced at the page.

There were now ninety-four listed responsibilities.

Twenty-six original responsibilities had been examined in the first review. Some had been assigned to existing offices, others required clarification or further regulation. The additional seven informal or newly discovered duties were being reviewed separately.

The numbers had become clearer.

The work had not become smaller.

Yuru looked at the last column.

"Add a note that no responsibility is considered transferred until the receiving office acknowledges it."

Fang Ru wrote it down.

"And ask the Crown Prince to review the proposed reporting procedure for the Eastern Palace. We should not impose a process on him without examining whether it works in practice."

"Yes, Your Majesty."

Yuru leaned back.

Outside, the palace lamps were being lit.

The day had been long.

She had spoken to the Empress Dowager, reviewed the Crown Prince's report, examined the household register, and answered three questions that should never have reached her desk.

She looked toward the unopened tray on the side table.

The broom report.

She considered it.

Then she turned back to the responsibilities register.

The Department of Palace Stores could finish its own investigation.

If it could not, that was precisely the problem.

At the Qingyuan Marquisate, Mingzhao received a note from her father.

He requested that she attend another family meeting the following morning.

The subject was the division of property.

She read the note once.

Then she placed it beside Sun Qiming's latest examination schedule.

Qiao Yue entered with a small tray.

"Miss, the kitchen has sent dinner."

"What is it?"

"Rice, fish, and two vegetable dishes."

"Did they send the same quantity as yesterday?"

"Yes."

Mingzhao looked at her.

"Why?"

"The kitchen says the family has not yet determined whether your meals should be charged to the estate or your personal account."

Mingzhao stared at the tray.

For a moment, she said nothing.

Then she picked up her chopsticks.

"Tell them to charge it to the estate for now."

"Yes, Miss."

"And tell the steward that the question of meals is not a reason to reduce the food."

Qiao Yue smiled.

"I will."

Mingzhao began eating.

She had spent the day calculating the costs of a separate household.

It appeared that the family had begun calculating the cost of allowing her to remain in the existing one.

Neither calculation was finished.

Neither side was particularly pleased.

On the palace side of the capital, the emperor was discovering that a broom could require three offices, two registers, and an authorization that nobody could locate.

At the Marquisate, Shen Mingyuan was searching for a carpenter who might remember a payment from the previous winter.

And in two separate studies, two women were preparing to leave arrangements that had been built around their continued presence.

Neither departure had been approved.

Neither woman had withdrawn her request.

For the moment, that was enough to keep everyone busy.

**End of Chapter 8.**
