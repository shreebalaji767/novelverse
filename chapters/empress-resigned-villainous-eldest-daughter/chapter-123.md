# **Chapter 123: The Former Empress Has Discovered That a Pleasant Visit May End Without a Return Invitation Being Scheduled Before Supper. The Marquis's Eldest Daughter Has Received a Merchant's Revised Terms and Has Learned That a Lower Price May Conceal a Higher Expectation. The Marquis's Heir Has Found a Reference to an Attached Sheet and Has Discovered That a Paper Mentioned in a Register Is Not Necessarily a Paper That Can Be Found.**

**27th Day of the Seventh Month, 18th Year of Yonghe.**

Xu Yuru's first decision that morning concerned a small wooden box.

It had been sitting beside her writing table for several days. Inside were the remaining pieces of dark-blue thread, two needles, a small pair of scissors, and the cream cloth on which she had been attempting to embroider a branch.

Fang Ru had placed it there the previous evening.

Yuru opened the lid.

The branch looked no better in the morning light.

She turned the cloth over, examined the stitches from the reverse, and discovered that one section had been pulled too tightly. The fabric had gathered slightly beneath the leaves.

"That is unfortunate," she said.

Fang Ru, who was arranging the breakfast tray, glanced over.

"Has something come loose?"

"No. I have made it uneven."

"Can it be corrected?"

"Probably."

"Would you like me to help?"

Yuru considered the cloth.

"Not yet."

She placed it back in the box.

Fang Ru brought the tea.

"Will you continue after breakfast?"

"Perhaps."

"Would you like me to prepare the thread?"

"No. I have enough."

Fang Ru set down the tray and looked at the box.

"Would you like to learn a different stitch?"

Yuru raised an eyebrow.

"Are you suggesting that the present one is beyond repair?"

"I would never say that."

"You are considering it."

Fang Ru lowered her head, but the corners of her mouth moved.

Yuru smiled and turned to breakfast.

The previous afternoon had been pleasant. She remembered the Dowager's remarks about the pear, the light tea, and the breeze passing through the western pavilion.

She also remembered that she had returned without being given a document to read.

It was a surprisingly pleasant way to spend an afternoon.

After breakfast, a servant brought a letter from her aunt.

The envelope was familiar. Her aunt's handwriting was broad and slightly hurried, with the final strokes of several characters extending farther than necessary.

Yuru opened it.

Her aunt confirmed the visit at the end of the month and asked whether Yuru preferred to arrive before the midday meal or shortly afterward. She also mentioned that several relatives might be present, though she had not yet decided who would attend.

The letter contained no request to examine accounts, settle a household disagreement, or advise on the arrangement of servants.

It did contain a complaint about a neighbour's chickens.

Yuru read that portion twice.

"Has she written about the chickens again?" Fang Ru asked.

"Yes."

"Are they still entering her garden?"

"Apparently they have developed a preference for her vegetables."

"Perhaps the neighbour should keep them enclosed."

"My aunt has already told him this."

"Did he listen?"

"She says he nodded."

Fang Ru waited.

"That does not sound promising."

"It was not."

Yuru placed the letter on the table and prepared a reply.

She said that she would arrive before the midday meal, thanked her aunt for the invitation, and expressed sympathy regarding the chickens.

She did not offer to resolve the matter.

When she finished, she read the reply once and sealed it.

Fang Ru placed the letter with the outgoing correspondence.

"Shall I prepare a small gift for your aunt?"

Yuru considered it.

"Some tea would be suitable."

"Which tea?"

"The mild one Father sent last month."

"How much?"

"A small packet."

Fang Ru nodded.

Yuru added, "Not the entire supply."

"I would not send all of it."

"You looked as though you were considering it."

"I was considering whether the small packet should be wrapped in the blue paper or the cream paper."

"Use the blue."

Fang Ru went to prepare it.

Yuru returned to the embroidery box.

She took out the cloth, loosened the thread beneath the gathered section, and tried again.

The branch remained crooked.

At least it was now crooked without wrinkling the cloth.

She considered this an improvement.

At the Shen workshop, Mingzhao received Guo Jian's second letter.

The merchant had revised his offer.

He accepted the proposed price, agreed that the supplied sample would serve as the inspection standard, and named the eastern warehouse in River Quarter as the delivery point.

He had also increased the deposit.

Mingzhao read the letter carefully.

The revised terms appeared more reasonable.

Then she reached the paragraph concerning payment.

Guo Jian proposed that the remaining balance be paid after the goods had been inspected by his representative. He asked for five days to complete the inspection and settle the account.

Mingzhao placed the letter beside the previous correspondence.

Five days was not an unreasonable period in itself.

But it was not what she had requested.

She had asked for payment within three days after inspection. The merchant had added two days without explaining why.

She read the rest.

The letter also stated that the merchant would accept a small variation in shade, provided the cloth remained suitable for everyday garments.

Mingzhao looked toward the sample.

"Has he given a measurement for the variation?" she asked.

Sun Qiao, standing across the table, shook his head.

"No."

"Has he described how the cloth will be judged?"

"Only that it must remain suitable for everyday garments."

"That is an opinion, not a standard."

Sun Qiao nodded.

Mingzhao tapped the letter with one finger.

"He has accepted the price and the sample, but he has changed the payment period and kept the colour condition vague."

"Would you like me to send another reply?"

"Yes."

She dictated a brief response.

The payment period would remain three days after inspection. If the merchant required five days, he must explain the reason and provide an arrangement that protected the workshop's cash flow. The sample would remain the standard. Any acceptable shade variation must be agreed before production, with a second sample approved by both parties if necessary.

She paused.

"Add that the workshop will not begin production until the agreement is signed and the deposit received."

Sun Qiao wrote it down.

"Should we reserve the material?"

"No."

"The supplier says he can provide enough."

"Has he given us a price that we have accepted?"

"No."

"Then we have not purchased anything."

Sun Qiao put the brush down.

"Understood."

Mingzhao examined the estimate again.

The twenty-bolt proposal was more promising than the six-bolt trial. The cloth was ordinary, the production method familiar, and the buyer had shown a willingness to negotiate.

But the workshop did not need an order merely to prove that its looms could be used.

She placed the papers in a separate folder.

"Keep the inquiry open," she said. "Do not enter it in the production schedule."

Sun Qiao bowed.

He took the folder away.

Mingzhao then opened the correspondence concerning the first supplier's third sample.

The supplier had sent another note. He was willing to adjust the loom and provide a more even width, but he still would not promise exact colour matching. He suggested that Mingzhao accept a reasonable difference, as many merchants did.

She read the sentence again.

Many merchants did not have to sell the finished goods under her name.

She wrote a reply asking for a measurable range and a sample produced under the same conditions as the intended batch.

Then she placed the letter with the other unanswered terms.

The six-bolt trial remained unapproved.

She had no intention of turning a small experiment into a larger problem simply because the supplier was tired of answering questions.

In the central records collection, Liang Wen stood before a clerk who was holding a bundle of loose papers.

The clerk had brought them from a cabinet marked with the general freight register's collection number.

"These were stored with the register," he explained. "They were not bound into it."

Liang Wen examined the outer wrapper.

It bore the same office designation as the general freight register, but the date was later than the dispatch entry.

"How many sheets?"

"Seven."

"Are they all receiving records?"

"Not all. Three are delivery acknowledgments, two are lists of damaged goods, one is a forwarding note, and one appears to be a copy of an instruction."

Liang Wen looked at the last sheet.

"Does it mention the four carts?"

"Not by number."

"Does it mention the date?"

"The date is written at the top, but the year is unclear."

Liang Wen leaned closer.

The paper had been folded several times. The ink was faded, and a stain obscured part of the heading.

He did not attempt to read the entire sheet while standing.

"Bring it to the examination table. We will compare the handwriting and dates with the register."

The clerk placed the bundle carefully in a carrying tray.

Liang Wen followed.

At the table, he arranged the papers in their original order and asked the clerk to record the sequence before any comparison began.

The first sheet was a delivery acknowledgment for two carts of timber.

The second concerned damaged ceramic goods.

The third listed a shipment of iron tools.

The remaining sheets were unrelated to the four-cart dispatch, at least on their visible faces.

The last sheet contained a brief instruction about a delivery whose receiving place had not been confirmed.

Liang Wen examined it.

The date might have been the same month.

The writing was not sufficiently clear to establish the year.

He compared the handwriting with the four-cart entry in the Northern Road Movement Supplement.

The strokes were different.

That did not prove the sheets were unrelated. Different clerks could have written the dispatch entry and the instruction.

But there was no basis to connect them.

"Record the sheet as an unidentified forwarding instruction," he said.

The clerk wrote it down.

"Should we place it with the four-cart entry?"

"No."

"Even though the wording concerns an unconfirmed delivery?"

"That is not enough."

Liang Wen returned the sheet to its wrapper.

The clerk looked disappointed.

"It may be the one we need."

"It may be."

"Then why not keep it with the inquiry?"

"Because we are searching for evidence, not collecting papers that resemble the answer."

The clerk nodded.

Liang Wen checked the collection index once more.

The temporary receiving book was still missing.

The general freight register had been found.

The loose sheets had been examined.

The missing attachment remained missing.

He prepared a report for Steward Zhou and sent it to the marquisate.

Mingyuan received the report before the evening meal.

He read it at the accounts table while Zhou De stood nearby.

"Seven loose sheets," he said.

"Yes."

"None identifies the four carts."

"Correct."

"One instruction has an unclear date."

"Yes."

Mingyuan put the report down.

"Then it remains unidentified."

Zhou nodded.

"Liang Wen has recorded it that way."

"Good."

Mingyuan took up the report about the temporary receiving book.

The collection clerks had not found it. They had located a second index entry, however, showing that temporary receiving books from several northern depots had been transferred to the central collection during the records reorganization.

The index listed the number of bundles but did not identify their contents.

Mingyuan rubbed his forehead.

"How many bundles?"

"Six."

"How many have been examined?"

"Two."

"And the other four?"

"Still in storage."

"Then have the remaining bundles brought out."

Zhou looked at him.

"That will take some time."

"Then let it take time."

"Should we request assistance from the Revenue Office?"

"No. This is a private estate inquiry concerning the records of the old transport office. We have no reason to involve the Revenue Office unless the documents establish that its records are relevant."

Zhou inclined his head.

Mingyuan continued reading.

The four carts were still only four carts.

The grain discrepancy remained separate.

The missing purchasing voucher remained separate.

The personal advances remained separate.

He had begun to see that the estate's old accounts had not been one great mystery waiting for a clever person to solve it. They were a collection of incomplete records, poor habits, lost papers, and decisions that had not been properly documented.

That was less dramatic than he had once imagined.

It was also more difficult to repair.

"Has my father asked about the inquiry?" he said.

"Not today."

"Has my mother?"

"She asked whether the revised property schedule was ready."

"What did you tell her?"

"That it was not final."

Mingyuan nodded.

"That is correct."

Zhou waited a moment.

"Young master, the household is asking whether the next family meeting will take place before the end of the month."

Mingyuan looked up.

"Has the agenda been prepared?"

"Not yet."

"Have the supporting documents been gathered?"

"No."

"Then there is no reason to schedule a meeting."

Zhou accepted the answer.

Mingyuan returned to the report.

He knew his mother wanted the division completed. His father wanted the matter settled without damaging the family's standing. His younger sister wanted to know which properties she would be responsible for inspecting. The clerks wanted clear instructions. And he wanted to avoid making a decision that would later have to be corrected.

None of those desires made the documents appear more quickly.

He signed the instruction to continue the search.

At the Marquisate's western courtyard, Shen Mingxiu was examining a maintenance estimate.

The document concerned repairs to a small outbuilding near the southern storehouse. Its roof tiles had shifted during the last rain, and the carpenter had proposed replacing several sections.

Mingxiu had asked for a second estimate.

The first carpenter's price was higher than she expected.

The second estimate was lower, but it omitted the cost of removing damaged timber.

She placed both documents before Madam Liang.

"The cheaper estimate is incomplete," she said.

Madam Liang looked at the pages.

"Can the carpenter not include the timber removal?"

"He can. I have asked him to revise it."

"Then why did he submit an incomplete estimate?"

"He says he thought the estate would arrange the removal separately."

"Did anyone tell him that?"

"No."

Madam Liang sighed.

"Then he should have asked."

Mingxiu nodded.

She had begun to notice how often people expected someone else to fill in the missing parts of an arrangement.

The estate had been accustomed to relying on Mingzhao to discover those omissions after the work had already begun.

Now Mingxiu had to ask the questions herself.

"Do you think the work should proceed?" Madam Liang asked.

"Not until the full price is clear. The roof is leaking, but the damage is limited. We can cover the exposed section temporarily while the carpenter revises his estimate."

Madam Liang looked at her daughter.

"You are becoming careful."

"I am trying not to pay for something twice."

Madam Liang returned the estimates.

"That is sensible."

Mingxiu gathered the papers.

She did not mention that she had learned the habit from watching her elder sister refuse to approve incomplete requests.

It was not necessary.

At the temporary residence, Yuru received the small packet of tea from Fang Ru.

The blue paper had been folded neatly and tied with a narrow cord.

"That is suitable," Yuru said.

"Shall I place it with the other things for your aunt?"

"Yes."

Fang Ru set it aside.

Yuru looked at the embroidery box.

"Bring the cloth."

Fang Ru opened the box and took out the cream fabric.

Yuru examined the branch again.

"Do you think the leaves are too close together?"

Fang Ru studied it.

"The second and third are close."

"Which should I move?"

"The third."

"Why?"

"Because the second is already stitched more securely."

Yuru looked at the cloth.

It was a reasonable answer.

She took up the needle and loosened the thread beneath the third leaf.

Fang Ru watched.

"Would you like me to hold the cloth?"

"Yes."

They worked together for several minutes.

The leaf shifted slightly.

It was still not perfect, but the fabric no longer gathered beneath it.

Yuru set the needle down.

"Better."

Fang Ru smiled.

"Much better."

Yuru examined the branch from a distance.

"Perhaps it will be finished before the visit."

"Your aunt's visit?"

"Yes."

"Then you could bring it."

Yuru considered that.

"Perhaps."

Fang Ru folded the cloth carefully.

"Would you like to take the embroidery box with you?"

"No. If I bring it, I may spend the visit correcting leaves."

"Then it is better to finish it here."

"That is what I thought."

The room grew quiet again.

Yuru looked toward the window.

At the end of the month, she would visit her aunt. She would take a small packet of tea, eat a meal, hear about the neighbour's chickens, and return to her own residence.

There was no need to decide anything else today.

She had begun to understand that a day did not become wasted merely because it contained no important decision.

At the workshop, Mingzhao received a final note from Guo Jian shortly before the lamps were lit.

The merchant accepted the three-day payment period and agreed that the sample would be the inspection standard.

He did not agree to a measurable shade variation.

Instead, he proposed that any difference be judged by his representative at the warehouse.

Mingzhao read the note.

Then she read it again.

The merchant had accepted nearly every condition except the one that mattered most.

He wanted to be the person who decided whether the goods met the sample.

That would make the sample little more than a decoration.

She set the note aside.

"Send him one final reply," she said to the correspondence clerk. "We will accept inspection by his representative only if the sample is retained by both parties and the finished cloth is compared directly against it. Any permitted variation must be written down before production."

The clerk wrote the instruction.

"And if he refuses?"

"Then we do not take the order."

The clerk nodded.

Mingzhao looked at the workshop ledger.

The completed orders were closed.

The current cash reserve was intact.

No new material had been purchased for the six-bolt trial.

No production had been authorized for the twenty-bolt proposal.

The workers had been paid according to their agreements.

The workshop was not idle because she had failed to find work.

It was waiting because she had not yet found work worth accepting.

She closed the ledger.

That night, Mingyuan received a short message from the central records collection.

The clerks had opened the third bundle of temporary receiving records.

They had found a register from the northern depot.

Its date range included the month of the four-cart dispatch.

The register had not yet been examined page by page.

Mingyuan read the message and looked at Zhou.

"At least we have found the book."

"Yes, young master."

"Does it contain the four cart numbers?"

"The message does not say."

Mingyuan placed the note beside the earlier reports.

"Then tomorrow we examine it."

Zhou nodded.

Mingyuan glanced at the other files on the table.

The four-cart inquiry had now produced a dispatch register, a general freight register, seven loose sheets, and a newly located temporary receiving book.

The answer was still unknown.

He wondered, briefly, whether his sister had ever been able to complete an account without discovering three additional accounts hidden beneath it.

Then he remembered that she had not been able to complete many of them.

That was why she had left.

He put the new message into the transport inquiry file.

For the first time in several days, he felt that the search had moved forward.

He did not yet know whether the register would answer anything.

But at least the clerks had found the book.

The book, unlike the missing sheet, had the courtesy to be present.
