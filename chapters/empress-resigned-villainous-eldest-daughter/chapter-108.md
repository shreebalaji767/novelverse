# **Chapter 108: The Former Empress Has Discovered That a Book About Trees Can Explain Why Some Things Need Space Without Recommending That She Personally Measure It. The Marquis's Eldest Daughter Has Been Asked Whether a Successful Order Means She Is Ready for Another, and Has Explained That Being Ready and Being Obliged Are Different Matters. The Marquis's Heir Has Found That an Old Transport Roster May Be More Difficult to Locate Than the Four Carts It Was Supposed to Record.**

**12th Day of the Seventh Month, 18th Year of Yonghe.**

Xu Yuru decided to visit her parents after breakfast.

The decision was made without consulting a household calendar, requesting an attendant to compare three possible routes, or asking whether the Duke's residence had arranged a suitable meal.

She simply told Fang Ru that she wished to go.

Fang Ru looked pleased.

"Shall I send word ahead?"

"Yes. Tell my mother I will arrive before noon."

"Should I mention whether you will stay for the midday meal?"

Yuru considered this.

"Tell her I will not stay long."

Fang Ru waited.

Yuru looked at her.

"Why are you waiting?"

"Because your mother may ask whether you will stay for the midday meal."

"Then I shall answer when she asks."

Fang Ru bowed her head, though the corners of her mouth had begun to rise.

The former Empress had spent much of her life making arrangements before anyone else had thought to request them. She had anticipated meals, ceremonies, visitors, delays, and the consequences of a servant forgetting to send a message.

Now she was learning that a visit to her parents did not require a preliminary examination of every possible inconvenience.

The carriage was prepared.

The garden book remained on the table, its place marked by a narrow strip of paper.

Before leaving, Yuru glanced at the illustration of the tree roots once more.

She had not finished the book.

There was no urgency.

At the Duke Xu residence, Madam Xu had received the message and immediately ordered tea.

She also ordered fruit.

Then she reconsidered the fruit and asked whether the preserved plums had been brought out the previous day.

A maid answered that they had.

Madam Xu instructed her to bring a different plate.

The maid left.

Duke Xu, who had been reading in the adjoining room, heard the exchange.

"She is coming to visit, not to inspect the kitchens."

Madam Xu looked at him.

"Did I say she was coming to inspect the kitchens?"

"No."

"Then why are you speaking as though I have?"

Duke Xu returned to his book.

He had learned that the safest way to avoid becoming involved in the arrangement of refreshments was to avoid commenting on it.

Yuru arrived shortly before noon.

Her mother greeted her in the main courtyard and immediately examined her face.

"You look well."

"I am well."

"Are you eating properly?"

"Yes."

"Sleeping?"

"Yes."

"Is the guest residence comfortable?"

"Comfortable enough."

Madam Xu looked as though she had expected a longer answer.

Duke Xu emerged from the adjoining room.

"Have you finished the book I lent you?"

"Not yet, Father."

"Which one?"

"The garden book."

He nodded with satisfaction.

"That one has useful illustrations."

"It does."

"Have you reached the section on pruning?"

"Not yet."

"Then you have not reached the most important part."

Madam Xu interrupted.

"She has only just arrived. You need not assign her reading."

Duke Xu looked mildly offended.

"I am discussing a book."

"You are discussing what she has not yet read."

Yuru removed her outer shawl and sat down.

"I have reached the section on trees."

Her father looked pleased.

"Then you have learned something about roots."

"Yes."

"And soil?"

"Yes."

"And the distance between trees?"

"Not yet."

Duke Xu nodded as though this were a reasonable sequence of events.

Madam Xu poured tea.

"Are you staying for lunch?"

Yuru glanced at her.

"I told Fang Ru I would not stay long."

"That was not an answer to my question."

Yuru paused.

Then she smiled.

"I can stay for lunch."

Madam Xu looked satisfied.

Duke Xu returned to his seat.

"Good. I had asked the kitchen to prepare a few dishes."

Madam Xu turned toward him.

"How many?"

"Not many."

"That is not a number."

"Six."

"That is not a few."

Duke Xu considered the matter.

"Five, then."

Yuru looked into her teacup.

She had been away from the residence for less than an hour, and the household had already begun negotiating the definition of a modest meal.

It was a familiar arrangement.

The difference was that she no longer had to settle it.

Madam Xu gave the Duke a look.

He lowered his book.

"Four dishes," he said.

"Four is sufficient," Madam Xu replied.

Yuru drank her tea.

The meal, when it arrived, contained four dishes and a soup.

No one asked her to approve the arrangement.

She found that particularly pleasant.

In the eastern market, Mingzhao received an inquiry from a trader named Luo Cheng.

He had heard that her workshop had completed an eighty-bolt order for Gao Wen. He wanted to know whether she would accept another order of similar size.

The inquiry arrived through a merchant who had previously dealt with Lin Shuyun.

It contained no written specification, no delivery date, and no proposed deposit.

It did, however, mention that the buyer could provide a substantial quantity of raw silk.

Mingzhao read the message twice.

Qiao Yue stood beside the desk.

"Is this a serious offer?"

"It is an inquiry."

"The trader says he has a buyer."

"He may."

"He says the buyer needs eighty bolts."

"He may need them."

Qiao Yue looked at the paper.

"Then what is missing?"

"Almost everything that would allow us to decide whether the work is profitable."

Mingzhao placed the message on the table.

The trader had not named the final buyer. He had not stated whether the raw silk would be supplied at a fixed valuation or merely delivered for processing. There was no mention of responsibility for damaged material, the approved sample, inspection, correction, or the date on which payment would become due.

The offer was not necessarily dishonest.

It was simply incomplete.

"Send a reply," Mingzhao said. "Ask for the buyer's name, the exact specification, the proposed quantity, the delivery date, and the payment arrangement. Ask whether the raw silk is being sold to us or entrusted to us for processing."

Qiao Yue wrote the instructions.

"Should we mention that the workshop has capacity?"

"Not yet."

"Why?"

"Because capacity is not the same as willingness."

Qiao Yue looked up.

Mingzhao continued.

"If the terms are unsuitable, I do not want the trader believing that we have already promised to take the work."

The distinction was important.

A merchant who heard that a workshop had available looms might begin making arrangements with his buyer before the workshop had agreed to anything. Later, if the terms proved unacceptable, the refusal would be described as a failure to honour an understanding that had never existed.

Mingzhao had seen that method before.

It was not always deliberate.

People frequently mistook a conversation about possibility for an agreement.

She was not interested in helping them make the mistake.

Qiao Yue finished writing.

"Anything else?"

"Ask whether the buyer will inspect the goods personally or through a representative."

"That is all?"

"For now."

Qiao Yue folded the reply.

Mingzhao returned to the closed account file.

The eighty-bolt order had been a useful transaction. It had shown that the workshop could manage a larger quantity under a clear contract, that the revised training arrangement had improved the trainees' work, and that the inspection procedure could identify defects before delivery.

It had not established that every buyer would be reliable.

Nor had it established that the workshop should immediately expand.

She preferred to keep the lessons separate.

At the Marquisate, Mingyuan received an answer from the transport office.

The old roster had been located.

It was not the original bound register but a copied list prepared during a review of transport personnel. It covered most of the relevant period, although several entries were faint.

Two drivers with the surnames Sun and Wei appeared on it.

The Sun entry included a given name that might have been Shou. The Wei entry was more legible, but the dates of his service did not perfectly match the dispatch period.

Mingyuan read the copy carefully.

"Can we identify them?"

Liang Wen, who had brought the document, shook his head.

"Not conclusively. The names are incomplete in one case, and the dates are uncertain in the other."

"Are they the only drivers with those surnames?"

"No. The original roster may have contained additional names."

"Then do not write that they were the drivers."

Liang Wen nodded.

Mingyuan placed the copy beside Li Shun's letter.

The roster provided possible names.

It did not establish that either man had driven the four carts.

"Ask the transport office whether it has any wage records or assignment slips for these two men," he said. "We need full names and service dates."

"Should I also ask for the daily dispatch list?"

"Yes. The reply did not say whether one survives."

Liang Wen wrote it down.

Mingyuan looked at the roster again.

The document had been copied during a later review. It was possible that the original had been damaged, moved, or lost. It was also possible that the copy omitted temporary workers.

He did not want to turn two partial names into a conclusion.

"Has the notebook register been checked?"

"Liang Wen's assistant found three references to the transport supervisor's office receiving movement records. None identifies the notebook."

"Do the dates match?"

"One is from the same month. The other two are several months later."

Mingyuan considered this.

"Keep the same-month entry with the inquiry. The others can remain in the supporting notes, but do not treat them as evidence of the notebook's transfer."

Liang Wen bowed.

He had begun to appreciate that Mingyuan was not merely asking for more documents.

He was asking for documents that answered a particular question.

This made the work slower.

It also made it harder for anyone to claim that the answer had been established when it had not.

The Marquis received the new report that afternoon.

He examined the copied roster and frowned.

"Two names?"

"Two possible names," Mingyuan replied.

"Can they be summoned?"

"We do not yet know their full identities or whether either drove the carts."

"Then why are they listed?"

"Because Li Shun remembered two drivers with those surnames, and the roster contains two possible entries."

Guowei set the paper down.

"Can we not ask the transport office to determine which one it was?"

"We have asked them to check their wage records and assignment slips."

"And how long will that take?"

"I cannot say."

The Marquis looked at his son.

Mingyuan remained standing.

"Your sister managed these matters more quickly."

The words escaped before Guowei had considered their effect.

Mingyuan did not answer immediately.

He knew that Mingzhao had managed the estate's accounts and commercial arrangements for years. He also knew that many of the records now being examined had been maintained under the old system, with responsibilities divided among clerks and supervisors whose procedures had not always been consistent.

His sister might have known where to search.

She might also have known which person had handled a particular document.

But she had left the management of the estate.

"She may remember some of the old arrangements," Mingyuan said. "She has already answered questions about the matters she handled. But she has not agreed to resume the investigation."

Guowei's expression hardened.

"She could at least help."

"She has helped where the records and her own knowledge allowed."

"That is not the same as helping the family settle the matter."

"No," Mingyuan said. "It is not."

The Marquis looked at him.

Mingyuan continued carefully.

"She has asked us to prepare the property schedule and the supporting documents before any meeting. If we want to discuss a specific payment or authorization, we should identify it first."

Guowei did not reply.

He was beginning to see that his son's position was not the same as Mingzhao's. Mingyuan was still responsible for the estate's administration. Mingzhao was not.

That distinction had once seemed inconvenient.

Now it was becoming difficult to ignore.

"Send the inquiry," Guowei said at last.

Mingyuan bowed.

"And have the property schedule prepared."

"Yes, Father."

At the Duke Xu residence, Yuru finished lunch and remained with her parents for another hour.

Her mother asked about the guest residence.

Her father asked about the garden book.

Neither asked about the palace.

The absence of that subject was not accidental.

Madam Xu had once been accustomed to hearing about ceremonies, appointments, disputes among attendants, and the endless complications of imperial households. She still worried that her daughter might be lonely without the responsibilities that had occupied so much of her life.

But she had promised herself not to turn every visit into a discussion of what Yuru had left behind.

Instead, she asked whether the guest residence had enough sunlight.

"It has enough," Yuru answered.

"Is the courtyard pleasant?"

"Yes."

"Do you spend time there?"

"Sometimes."

"Then you should bring the garden book outside."

Yuru looked at her.

"I do not want to damage it."

"Then put it on a small table."

Duke Xu nodded.

"That is sensible."

Madam Xu glanced at him.

"You need not agree with every suggestion I make."

"I agree when they are sensible."

Yuru smiled.

Her father had a habit of treating a minor agreement as though it were an official endorsement.

Madam Xu ignored him.

"Would you like to take another book when you leave?"

"No, Mother. I have not finished the ones I already borrowed."

"Then do not take another."

The answer came so easily that Yuru looked at her in surprise.

Madam Xu poured more tea.

"What?"

"Nothing."

"Your father will survive if one book remains on his shelf."

Duke Xu looked toward the adjoining room.

"I have never suggested otherwise."

"You suggested that she borrow three books last time."

"That was because she had asked about the subject."

"She asked about one subject. You brought her three books."

"They were related."

Yuru set down her cup.

"Father, I am not starting a library."

Duke Xu considered this.

"That would be an expensive undertaking."

Madam Xu looked at him.

He lowered his eyes to his tea.

The visit ended without further discussion of books.

Yuru returned to the guest residence with the garden book she had brought and no additional reading material.

She considered this a successful visit.

Mingzhao received the trader's reply before the evening meal.

It was brief.

The buyer's name would be provided after an initial understanding. The raw silk would be supplied for processing, but the valuation would be determined after the goods were finished. The delivery date would be discussed once the workshop confirmed its capacity. Payment would be made after the buyer's own customer had accepted the goods.

Mingzhao read it without expression.

Qiao Yue waited.

"Is it acceptable?"

"No."

"Because the buyer is unnamed?"

"That is one reason."

"And the raw silk valuation?"

"Another."

Qiao Yue glanced at the paper.

"They are offering us the material."

"They are asking us to accept responsibility for processing material whose value has not been settled, for a buyer whose identity has not been disclosed, with a delivery date that has not been agreed, and payment dependent on another transaction."

Qiao Yue considered the arrangement.

"It would keep the workshop occupied."

"It would also make the workshop responsible for a chain of decisions we do not control."

Mingzhao took a fresh sheet.

She wrote a short reply.

The workshop would consider processing work if the buyer's identity, material valuation, specification, delivery schedule, inspection conditions, and payment responsibility were provided in advance. It would not accept an arrangement in which payment depended on the buyer's resale or another person's acceptance.

She read the reply once.

Then she handed it to Qiao Yue.

"Send it."

"Do you expect him to agree?"

"No."

"Then why send it?"

"Because he may have misunderstood what we are willing to accept."

Qiao Yue folded the letter.

Mingzhao returned to the accounts.

The workshop had no new order.

That was not a failure.

It was an ordinary result of refusing an unsuitable offer.

Near dusk, Mingyuan received a brief message from Liang Wen.

The transport office had acknowledged the request for wage records and assignment slips. It would search its remaining papers and respond when the examination was complete.

There was no daily dispatch list attached.

There was no new evidence concerning the notebook.

Mingyuan placed the message in the four-cart folder.

He then opened the separate grain discrepancy file.

The figures remained unchanged.

Forty-seven sacks recorded.

Twenty covered by the instruction.

Eighteen received.

Two unconfirmed.

Twenty-seven unexplained.

He closed that file and moved to the old purchasing voucher inquiry.

The original voucher had still not been located.

The estate purchasing seal had been held by Chen Rui on the payment date, but the surviving record did not establish the recipient or purpose of the payment.

Three separate matters.

Three separate folders.

He looked at the shelves.

The folders were beginning to occupy more space than the reports that had originally been prepared to summarize them.

Mingyuan sighed.

He had wanted to learn how to manage the estate.

He had not expected to learn how many different ways a document could disappear.

Still, the papers were now being kept in a more orderly fashion. The new purchasing procedure had begun operating. The clerks were recording corrections instead of quietly replacing old entries. The receiving steward and accounts clerk were required to confirm different parts of a transaction.

It was slow.

It was also better than pretending the old records were complete.

He placed the latest message in the proper folder and closed the cabinet.

That night, Xu Yuru sat beside the window with her garden book.

She had reached the section about pruning.

The author explained that cutting away a branch was not always a punishment for poor growth. Sometimes it was necessary to allow the rest of the tree to develop properly.

Yuru read the passage twice.

Then she closed the book.

The explanation was sensible, although she suspected the author would soon find a way to turn it into another long instruction.

Fang Ru brought a small lamp.

"Would you like to continue reading?"

"Tomorrow."

"Shall I mark the page?"

Yuru placed the paper strip between the pages.

"I have marked it."

Fang Ru adjusted the lamp.

The courtyard had become quiet.

Yuru looked toward the small tree beside the wall. Its branches were dark against the evening sky. She could no longer see the shape of its leaves clearly, but she knew where they were.

She did not wonder whether the gardener had finished his work.

She did not ask whether the younger worker had understood his instructions.

She did not make a note for the following morning.

The garden would continue to exist without her supervision.

That was a reassuring discovery.

Across the city, Mingzhao had also finished her work for the day. Her workshop had no new order, her accounts were closed, and the trader's proposal had been declined unless its terms changed.

At the Marquisate, Mingyuan had another inquiry waiting for an answer.

The three households had reached different conclusions.

The former Empress could leave a tree to the gardener.

The independent workshop owner could leave an unsuitable offer unanswered.

The Marquis's heir could leave an empty space in the record empty.

None of these actions looked particularly impressive.

But each prevented someone from taking responsibility for something that was not theirs to carry.
