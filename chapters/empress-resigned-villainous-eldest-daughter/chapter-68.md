# **Chapter 68: The Former Empress Has Received a Question About a Book and Has Not Been Asked to Catalogue the Entire Library. The Marquis's Eldest Daughter Has Received a Family Letter and Has Not Been Asked to Return to the Estate. The Workshop Has Discovered That a Completed Bolt Is Not the Same as a Paid Bolt.**

**2nd Day of the Sixth Month, 18th Year of Yonghe.**



The Third Princess Zhao Minglan had two problems.



The first was that the old library catalogue contained two books with nearly identical titles.



The second was that the person who might have helped her identify them was no longer responsible for the palace library.



She had received the former Empress's reply the previous evening. It suggested comparing the chapter headings in the surviving volume with the two catalogue entries.



Minglan had done so.



Unfortunately, one of the books had lost its first several pages, while the other had a catalogue description that was so vague it might have referred to almost anything.



She sat at her desk, surrounded by three books, two catalogue copies, and a sheet of notes.



Her attendant, Chun Tao, placed tea beside her.



“Your Highness, would you like me to ask the library clerk to come?”



“I already asked him yesterday.”



“Then perhaps he has found something.”



“He found another catalogue.”



Chun Tao looked at the papers.



“Is that bad?”



“It is not helpful.”



Minglan picked up the oldest catalogue.



The clerk had copied the entries from an earlier register. One title was partially illegible. The other had a description referring to a collection of household regulations, but the surviving volume contained several chapters on the management of granaries and storehouses.



It might be the same work.



It might not.



Minglan remembered the former Empress's instruction.



Compare the headings.



She turned to the second chapter.



Then the third.



The headings did not match.



She turned to the fourth.



That one was different too.



Minglan set the book down.



“I cannot identify it yet.”



Chun Tao glanced at the catalogue.



“Should we write that it is unidentified?”



Minglan hesitated.



The old register had a blank space for the title.



It would be easy to select the closest match and complete the entry.



It would also be wrong if the book belonged to another work.



“Yes,” she said at last. “Write that the identification remains uncertain.”



Chun Tao prepared the paper.



Minglan watched her write the words.



It was a small decision.



No one would praise it.



No one would notice that she had refused to fill a blank merely because the blank looked untidy.



But the former Empress had taught her that an incomplete record could be more honest than a convenient answer.



Minglan placed the new note beside the old catalogue.



“Keep both.”



“Yes, Your Highness.”



“And ask the clerk whether any other copies of the book exist in the palace collection.”



Chun Tao bowed.



Minglan returned to the volume.



She had wanted to identify it before breakfast.



Now she was beginning to suspect that it might take several days.



The book, unfortunately, had not expressed any concern about her schedule.

At the temporary guest residence, Xu Yuru received the Third Princess's second message before noon.



Fang Ru brought it with the morning correspondence.



“The princess says the chapter headings do not match either catalogue entry. She has recorded the title as unidentified and asks whether that is acceptable.”



Yuru read the message.



A faint smile appeared.



“She has done the correct thing.”



“Would you like to send a reply?”



“Yes.”



Fang Ru prepared the paper.



Yuru dictated only a few lines.



*The catalogue should preserve what is known and distinguish it from what remains uncertain. Continue looking for other copies if convenient, but do not force an identification merely to complete the entry.*



She paused.



“Add that she should not spend the entire day searching for it.”



Fang Ru looked up.



“Should I write that?”



“Yes. The book has survived for many years without being identified. It can survive another week.”



Fang Ru smiled as she finished the reply.



Yuru signed it.



The message would go to the princess's residence, not to the library office.



It was a personal answer.



Nothing more.



After the attendant left, Yuru returned to the letter she had begun writing to her mother.



She had reached the part about the fruit seller.



The price of pears had increased, according to her mother, despite no apparent shortage.



Yuru considered the matter.



She had once been responsible for comparing prices from several suppliers, reviewing the quality of deliveries, and determining whether a change in price was justified.



She could probably have written a detailed explanation of seasonal supply and transport costs.



Instead, she wrote:



*If the pears are too expensive, buy fewer. There is no need to argue with the seller unless he has changed the weight as well as the price.*



She read the sentence.



Then she added:



*Please do not buy them simply because Father likes them.*



She set down her brush.



Fang Ru, who had returned to collect the finished message, glanced at the last line.



“Duke Xu likes pears?”



“He likes anything that can be served after a meal.”



“Then Madam Xu will probably buy them anyway.”



Yuru looked at her.



“That is possible.”



For a moment, neither spoke.



Then Yuru smiled.



The family could manage its own fruit purchases.



She did not need to supervise them.

At the Marquisate of Qingyuan, Shen Mingyuan had reached the fourth of the seven principal cash books.



He was not enjoying the experience.



The entries were written in several different hands. Some were neat. Others had been added in narrow spaces between earlier lines. A few carried small marks that referred to supporting sheets stored elsewhere.



Steward Zhou De sat opposite him.



Mingyuan examined a payment entry.



“This one says the money was issued to the household office.”



“Yes.”



“Does the household office have a receipt?”



“It has an acknowledgment.”



“From whom?”



“The former assistant steward.”



“Is he still employed here?”



“No. He left three years ago.”



Mingyuan looked at the entry again.



“Can we find him?”



“Possibly.”



“Then write his name in the list of people to contact.”



Steward Zhou made a note.



Mingyuan turned another page.



“And this one?”



“That payment was for a delivery.”



“What delivery?”



“The entry does not specify.”



“Then why is it listed under transport?”



“The clerk who prepared the account may have considered it a transport expense.”



Mingyuan stared at the page.



“May have?”



Steward Zhou remained calm.



“The clerk is no longer alive, Young Master.”



Mingyuan leaned back.



He had expected the books to contain mistakes.



He had not expected so many entries to depend on the intentions of people who could no longer explain them.



He rubbed his forehead.



“Could the supporting papers identify the delivery?”



“We will check.”



Mingyuan nodded.



The next entry was more familiar.



It concerned a payment made during the period when Mingzhao had been managing the estate.



Her name appeared beside the authorization.



Mingyuan looked at it carefully.



“Does this prove she received the money?”



“No.”



“Does it prove she authorized the payment?”



“It proves the entry records her authorization.”



“Is there a receipt?”



“There is a supporting sheet, but it names the recipient as the estate purchasing office.”



Mingyuan studied the document.



“Then this is not a personal repayment.”



“Not on the evidence presently available.”



Mingyuan set it aside.



He had begun to understand that the same person could authorize a payment, supervise its use, and still not be its recipient.



It was an obvious distinction when stated aloud.



It had somehow become less obvious in the family's old records.



He looked at Steward Zhou.



“Keep this separate from the repayment inquiry.”



“Yes, Young Master.”



“And make a copy of the supporting sheet.”



Steward Zhou bowed.



Mingyuan returned to the next page.



The fourth book was not yet finished.



Neither was his patience.

Mingzhao received Sun Qiming's reply shortly after midday.



The accountant had examined the first group of cash-book entries and identified several that required supporting documents.



He had also found one payment that was recorded as an issue from the estate account to the household purchasing office.



Mingzhao read the description.



She remembered the expenditure.



It had been made during her period of management, but the goods had been purchased for the estate.



The record did not show that she had received any personal money.



She placed the report beside the previous day's letter from her father.



Qiao Yue brought tea.



“Have they found a repayment?”



“No.”



“Have they found something that is not a repayment?”



“Yes.”



Qiao Yue looked at the report.



“That seems less useful.”



“It is useful.”



Mingzhao pointed to the entry.



“This payment was made under my authorization. The recipient was the estate purchasing office. It cannot be used to claim that I was repaid.”



“Will the family accept that?”



“They have not yet claimed otherwise.”



Qiao Yue sat down opposite her.



“Then what happens next?”



“The accountant continues examining the remaining entries.”



“And after that?”



“Someone will probably ask another question.”



Qiao Yue sighed.



“Your family has many questions.”



“They have many accounts.”



“Could they not simply agree that you should receive the money?”



Mingzhao looked at her.



“Which money?”



“The money they owe you.”



“That is why we are examining the records.”



Qiao Yue considered the answer.



“Sometimes I think you would rather win an argument than finish it.”



Mingzhao's expression changed slightly.



“That is not entirely untrue.”



Qiao Yue looked surprised.



Mingzhao picked up the report again.



“But finishing it incorrectly would not be useful.”



She was not pleased by the slow pace.



She was not pleased by the family's old habits.



She was not even pleased by the possibility that some of her own claims might prove weaker than she believed.



But she had no intention of replacing uncertainty with a convenient conclusion.



That was one of the reasons she had left the estate.



She had spent too long being expected to make everything appear orderly, even when the records were not.



She would not do it again.

At the silk workshop, Lin Shuyun was dealing with a complaint from one of the senior finishers.



The worker stood beside the production table, holding two completed bolts.



“The revised folding method is faster,” he said, “but the new inspection requirements are slowing us down.”



Lin looked at the bolts.



“Which inspection?”



“The repeated edge comparison.”



Mingzhao, who was reviewing the day's production sheets, looked up.



“Is the edge inconsistent?”



“No.”



“Then why is the inspection taking longer?”



The worker shifted his weight.



“Because the supervisor checks the sample against every group.”



“That is the requirement.”



“It is excessive.”



Mingzhao put down her brush.



“The buyer approved the revised method on the condition that the specifications remain consistent across the order.”



“I understand that.”



“Then what do you propose?”



The worker hesitated.



“We could inspect every fifth bolt instead of every bolt.”



Lin considered the suggestion.



“That would save time.”



“It might,” Mingzhao said.



The worker looked at her.



She picked up one of the bolts.



“If the first four are consistent, and the fifth is not, what happens to the four before it?”



“They would be checked again.”



“And the group after it?”



The worker did not answer.



Mingzhao placed the bolt down.



“The inspection is not meant to punish the workers. It is meant to catch a change before it affects an entire batch.”



The senior finisher frowned.



“We have been making silk longer than the buyer has been purchasing it.”



“That is true.”



“Then we should know how to fold it.”



“You know how to fold it according to the old method. The buyer has approved a revised method with specific conditions. We are responsible for following those conditions.”



The worker looked toward Lin.



Lin did not intervene.



She understood the complaint.



The workshop had spent years relying on the judgment of experienced workers. Some of them were accustomed to treating their own methods as proof of quality.



The new order required something different.



The buyer was not purchasing the workers' confidence.



He was purchasing a defined product.



Lin turned to the worker.



“Continue with the current inspection. We can review the time required after another group has been completed.”



The worker bowed, though his expression remained dissatisfied.



When he had left, Lin looked at Mingzhao.



“You could have explained it more gently.”



“I explained it.”



“You also made him look foolish.”



“He proposed reducing inspection without explaining how defects would be detected.”



“He has worked here for twenty years.”



“Then he should know why inspection matters.”



Lin sighed.



“You are not wrong.”



“I am aware.”



“That does not mean you must always sound as though you are delivering a judgment.”



Mingzhao looked at the production sheet.



For a moment, she did not reply.



Then she said, “I will speak to him again after the next group.”



Lin nodded.



It was not an apology.



It was, however, a willingness to reconsider how the matter was handled.



That was enough for the moment.

The next twenty-four bolts were completed before the afternoon ended.



The workshop's internal inspection found one edge that required adjustment.



The correction was made without damaging the material.



The rest of the group passed.



The production register now recorded:

- Forty-eight bolts accepted by the buyer.
- Twenty-four bolts completed and internally inspected, awaiting buyer inspection.
- Twenty-four additional bolts completed and internally inspected, awaiting buyer inspection.
- One hundred and four bolts remaining to be produced.

Mingzhao checked the arithmetic.



The total was two hundred.



She signed the page.



Lin looked at the tally.



“We are making progress.”



“Yes.”



“If the buyer accepts the next forty-eight, we will have completed almost half the order.”



“Completed is not the same as accepted.”



Lin gave her a tired look.



“And accepted is not the same as paid.”



Mingzhao glanced up.



“That is correct.”



Lin laughed.



“You are becoming contagious.”



Mingzhao returned to the ledger.



The deposit had been received.



The next payment would depend on the agreed inspection and delivery conditions.



The workshop could not count the remaining contract amount as available money simply because the order had been signed.



Wages still had to be paid.



Materials still had to be purchased.



Transport still had to be arranged.



The new folding method might reduce labour time, but the cost of training and supervision had to be included.



They had a promising order.



They did not yet have a final profit.



Mingzhao closed the ledger.



“Have the next group prepared for inspection tomorrow.”



Lin nodded.



“And the senior finisher?”



“I will speak to him.”



“Politely?”



Mingzhao looked at her.



“I will attempt to be understood.”



“That is not quite the same thing.”



“No,” Mingzhao said. “It is not.”

In the evening, Shen Guowei received the first summary from Steward Zhou concerning the cash-book review.



It was not a final report.



Several entries had been identified as estate expenditures authorized by Mingzhao. Others required receipts or further confirmation. No entry in the examined group established a personal repayment to her.



The Marquis read the summary twice.



Madam Liang sat nearby.



“How much have they found?”



“Nothing conclusive.”



“Then why are they spending so much time on it?”



“Because the records are incomplete.”



Madam Liang looked displeased.



“Your daughter was the one who managed the accounts.”



“She was.”



“Then she should know where the money went.”



Shen Guowei folded the report.



“She managed the accounts. That does not mean every missing receipt can be reconstructed from memory.”



Madam Liang frowned.



“You are defending her again.”



“I am reading the report.”



He placed it on the table.



There were still matters to settle with Mingzhao.



There were also matters that the family had not handled well.



He had once assumed that because she had been responsible for the estate, she could answer any question about it.



He was beginning to understand that the assumption itself had been unreasonable.



Steward Zhou entered.



“My lord, the former warehouse steward has completed the first comparison of the grain sheets.”



Shen Guowei looked up.



“Has he identified the destination of the twenty-seven sacks?”



“No, my lord.”



“Then what has he established?”



“He confirms that the records distinguish total warehouse stock from stock immediately available for issue. This explains the difference between two of the original figures. It does not explain the twenty-seven sacks.”



The Marquis was silent.



“Has he named anyone?”



“No. He has asked for the remaining certified copies before making any further statement.”



“Send them.”



“Yes, my lord.”



Steward Zhou withdrew.



Madam Liang looked at her husband.



“Will this never end?”



Shen Guowei looked at the report.



“It will end when we establish what happened.”



“And if we cannot?”



“Then we will have to record what we cannot establish.”



Madam Liang did not answer.



The Marquis had no satisfaction to offer her.



The family's records were not becoming simpler.



They were becoming more honest.

At the western guest residence, Yuru finished her evening tea.



Fang Ru brought in a small note from the palace.



It was from the Third Princess.



The princess reported that she had recorded the book as unidentified and had requested a search of the other palace collections.



She also thanked the former Empress for reminding her not to spend the entire day searching for it.



Yuru read the message.



“Did she find another copy?”



“No, Madam.”



“Then she has made no progress.”



Fang Ru looked at her.



“Would you call that no progress?”



Yuru considered the question.



“She has established that the two catalogue entries are not reliable matches. That is progress.”



Fang Ru nodded.



Yuru placed the note beside her mother's letter.



The day had passed without a single administrative report being sent to her.



The palace had continued its work.



The princess had made a decision.



The Marquisate had continued examining its accounts.



The workshop had completed another group of bolts.



None of those events required Yuru to take charge.



She looked toward the courtyard.



The evening light had begun to fade.



“Fang Ru.”



“Yes, Madam?”



“Tomorrow, I would like to walk outside the residence.”



“Shall I arrange a carriage?”



“No. Just the nearby path, if the weather is suitable.”



Fang Ru nodded.



Yuru picked up her tea.



She had not chosen a permanent home.



She had not decided what her life would look like after leaving the palace entirely.



But she had begun making small plans that belonged to her.



For the moment, that was sufficient.



The sixth month had only just begun.



There was no reason to hurry through it.
