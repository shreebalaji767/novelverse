# **Chapter 74: The Former Empress Has Arrived at Her Parents' Home Without a Procession and Has Been Seated Before Her Father Could Arrange One. The Marquis's Eldest Daughter Has Received a Seal Register and Has Discovered That Custody Was Recorded More Carefully Than the Payments. The Workshop Has Been Asked to Prepare for Another Inspection and Has Refused to Count Unfinished Bolts as Finished.**

**8th Day of the Sixth Month, 18th Year of Yonghe.**



Duke Xu had been waiting in the eastern courtyard since before noon.



He had not intended to wait.



He had merely finished his morning affairs early, discovered that the courtyard was pleasant, and decided that sitting there would be more comfortable than sitting in his study.



The fact that his daughter was expected that afternoon had nothing to do with it.



At least, that was what he told himself.



Madam Xu, who had watched him move the same chair three times, had a different opinion.



"Your daughter is coming to eat," she said.



"I know."



"She is not attending an official reception."



"I know that too."



"Then why have you asked the servants to arrange the chairs in two rows?"



Duke Xu looked at the chairs.



"They were not arranged in two rows."



"They were."



"They were arranged in a manner that allows people to sit comfortably."



Madam Xu examined the arrangement.



There were six chairs facing one another, with two additional chairs placed at the head of the courtyard.



"Who are the two chairs at the head for?"



Duke Xu hesitated.



"Us."



"We do not need two chairs at the head."



"We are the hosts."



"We are her parents."



"That is why we are hosting her."



Madam Xu looked at him.



Duke Xu cleared his throat.



"Would you prefer that I move them?"



"Yes."



He waved to the servants.



The chairs were moved.



Madam Xu watched the servants finish, then turned toward the kitchen.



"Have they prepared the dishes?"



"Yes."



"How many?"



Duke Xu answered too quickly.



"Six."



Madam Xu stopped.



"Six?"



"Six."



"Not eight?"



"No."



"Not ten?"



Duke Xu looked offended.



"I said six."



Madam Xu nodded.



She had personally spoken to the kitchen. There were six dishes, one soup, and rice. The soup did not count as an additional dish, and she had made that distinction clear.



Duke Xu had also been instructed not to request extra dishes.



He had obeyed.



Mostly.



He had asked whether a small plate of seasonal fruit could be placed on the table.



Madam Xu had permitted it because fruit was not a dish.



The matter had therefore been settled.



At least until Duke Xu suggested that the kitchen prepare another small plate in case the first one was insufficient.



Madam Xu had refused.



The Duke had not argued further.



He had learned that his wife could be more difficult to negotiate with than a provincial governor.

Yuru arrived shortly after the midday meal.



Her carriage stopped outside the eastern courtyard entrance.



There was no procession, no ceremonial music, and no line of household servants waiting to announce her arrival.



Fang Ru stepped down first.



Then Yuru emerged, carrying a small wrapped packet.



Madam Xu came forward.



"You have arrived."



"Mother."



She greeted her mother and father in turn.



Duke Xu looked at her carefully.



"You came comfortably?"



"Yes."



"Was the road crowded?"



"Not particularly."



"Was the carriage too warm?"



"No."



"Did the driver take the western route?"



Yuru smiled.



"Father, I arrived safely."



Duke Xu nodded.



"Good."



Madam Xu took her daughter's hand.



"Come inside. The meal is ready."



Yuru glanced at the courtyard.



The chairs were arranged simply. A small table stood near the shaded wall, and the servants had placed a vase of flowers at one end.



It was not a reception.



She looked at her father.



"You did not arrange anything elaborate."



Duke Xu straightened.



"Of course not."



Madam Xu said nothing.



Yuru's eyes moved briefly toward the servants.



One of them was carrying away a spare chair.



She understood.



Her mother had probably prevented an elaborate reception before she arrived.



Yuru did not mention it.



She handed the wrapped packet to her mother.



"I brought some tea."



Madam Xu accepted it.



"You did not need to bring anything."



"I wanted to."



Madam Xu looked at the packet, then at her daughter.



"Then I will have it prepared another day."



"That would be nice."



Duke Xu glanced at the packet.



"Is it a rare tea?"



"No."



"From the palace?"



"No. It was purchased from a merchant."



Duke Xu appeared slightly disappointed.



Madam Xu gave him a warning look.



He immediately became interested in the courtyard flowers.

The meal was served in the eastern courtyard.



There were six dishes, soup, rice, and the small plate of seasonal fruit.



Duke Xu counted them.



He did so discreetly.



Madam Xu noticed.



Yuru noticed Madam Xu noticing.



Fang Ru, who sat with the household attendants at the side, lowered her head to hide a smile.



The first dish was a simple preparation of fish with ginger.



The second was tender greens.



The third was a braised chicken dish Yuru had liked as a child.



The remaining dishes were familiar household preparations.



Nothing was excessively rich.



Nothing had been arranged to impress a guest.



Yuru tasted the chicken and paused.



"Mother, this is the same preparation as before."



Madam Xu smiled.



"You used to ask for it whenever you returned from school."



"I remember."



"You always said the sauce was better when it had been left to rest."



"Was I correct?"



"Your father says you were."



Duke Xu put down his chopsticks.



"You were correct. Your mother used to prepare it early and then complain that you arrived too late to eat it."



Madam Xu looked at him.



"I complained because you kept asking the servants whether the meal was ready."



"I was making sure it was warm."



"You asked three times."



"Warm food is important."



Yuru laughed.



The sound was quiet, but it changed the atmosphere of the courtyard.



Her father looked pleased.



Madam Xu served her another small portion.



"Eat while it is warm."



Yuru accepted it.



For several moments, the conversation remained ordinary.



Her mother asked about the guest residence. Yuru told her that the rooms were comfortable and that the western path had become familiar.



Duke Xu asked whether she had decided where she would live permanently.



"Not yet," Yuru replied.



"Your eastern courtyard is available."



"I know."



"It is quiet."



"I know that too."



"Your mother has already had the rooms inspected."



Madam Xu looked at him.



"I had the rooms inspected because they had been unused for a long time. I did not ask her to move there."



Duke Xu cleared his throat.



"I only mentioned that the rooms were available."



Yuru smiled.



"I am grateful, Father. I have not decided yet."



Duke Xu nodded.



He did not press the matter.



That was progress.



Madam Xu poured more soup into Yuru's bowl.



"Then do not hurry. You have been making decisions for other people for a long time. You may take time to make one for yourself."



Yuru looked at her mother.



The words were simple.



They were also more generous than any promise of a furnished residence.



"Thank you."



Madam Xu nodded.



She did not ask whether the palace had appointed a new Empress.



She did not ask whether the Emperor had sent documents.



She did not ask whether the Dowager Empress had been displeased.



She knew that Yuru would speak about such matters if she wished.



Instead, she asked whether the preserved fruit she had sent had been enough.



Yuru smiled.



"It was more than enough."



"Then I will send another small packet next time."



"Mother, you do not need to send something every time."



"I know."



Madam Xu did not promise to stop.



Duke Xu looked as though he might offer to send something too.



Yuru looked at him.



He picked up his chopsticks.



The fruit was not joined by a second packet.

After the meal, they moved to the shaded part of the courtyard.



Duke Xu had arranged a low table with tea.



He had also arranged a small selection of books.



Yuru looked at them.



"Father, did you choose these?"



"I thought you might enjoy reading."



She examined the titles.



One was a collection of essays she had read years earlier. Another was a historical account of a provincial administration. The third was a book of poetry.



"Thank you."



Duke Xu looked pleased.



"I thought the administrative account might interest you."



Yuru turned the book over.



"Does it contain anything particularly unusual?"



"Not that I know."



"Then I will read it."



Duke Xu sat back.



For a while, they spoke about the Xu family.



Her younger cousins had been busy with their studies. One had recently passed an examination in calligraphy and was now convinced that he possessed the skill to write official documents.



Duke Xu considered this confidence premature.



Madam Xu considered it a normal stage of youth.



Yuru listened, asked a few questions, and laughed when her father described the boy's latest attempt to write a letter to a distant relative.



"Did the relative understand it?" she asked.



"Not entirely."



"Was the handwriting at least beautiful?"



"Very beautiful," Duke Xu admitted.



Madam Xu smiled.



"Then perhaps the letter achieved half its purpose."



Duke Xu sighed.



"That is not how correspondence should work."



Yuru looked at him.



"Father, you have received letters from ministers that were beautifully written and difficult to understand."



"Those are different."



"How?"



"They are official."



Madam Xu laughed.



The conversation continued.



No one asked Yuru to review a household account.



No one requested an opinion on a personnel appointment.



No one asked her to settle a disagreement between two relatives.



At one point, Duke Xu began to describe a dispute between two branches of the family concerning a small piece of inherited land.



He stopped halfway through.



Yuru looked at him.



"Is there a particular question you want to ask me?"



Duke Xu hesitated.



"No. I was merely telling you."



"Then I am listening."



He continued.



The dispute was not especially serious. One branch believed that a boundary marker had been moved; the other believed that the marker had been placed incorrectly in the first place.



The matter had been referred to the family steward.



Yuru asked whether the old land records were available.



"They are," Duke Xu said. "The steward is comparing them."



"Then perhaps it is best to wait for the comparison."



"That is what I told them."



Madam Xu looked at her husband.



"You told them that after you had spent half an hour arguing with both sides."



Duke Xu frowned.



"I was explaining the importance of patience."



Yuru lowered her eyes.



She had missed this.



Not the argument itself.



The ordinary family habit of disagreeing about something that did not require her to solve it.

At the Marquisate of Qingyuan, Shen Mingyuan received the relevant seal-register pages shortly before noon.



The accounts office had found the original register.



It was not a particularly large book.



The entries were arranged by date, seal type, and custodian. Each transfer of custody required the outgoing holder and incoming holder to sign.



Mingyuan examined the pages carefully.



Steward Zhou stood beside him.



"The register is clearer than the payment book," Mingyuan said.



"It was maintained separately."



Mingyuan found the relevant period.



The estate purchasing seal had been held by the senior accounts clerk, then transferred to a deputy clerk during a period of leave.



The transfer had been recorded.



The incoming and outgoing signatures were both present.



Mingyuan checked the date of the payment entry.



The seal was in the custody of the senior accounts clerk on that day.



"Then the clerk processed the voucher," Mingyuan said.



"That is what the custody record indicates."



"Does it establish that he made the payment?"



"No. It establishes that he held the seal."



Mingyuan nodded.



He examined the following pages.



There was another entry.



The seal had been returned to the senior clerk after the deputy's leave.



The dates were consistent.



He placed the register beside the copied payment record.



The information was useful.



It still did not identify the recipient.



"Can we find the clerk?" Mingyuan asked.



Steward Zhou considered the question.



"The senior clerk retired several years ago. He may still live in the southern district."



"Do we know his name?"



"Chen Rui."



"Can we send a respectful inquiry asking whether he remembers the transaction?"



Steward Zhou nodded.



"We can. But we should send the copy of the entry and the date. A general question about a payment from several years ago may not help him remember."



"Then include the amount and the seal-register entry."



Mingyuan paused.



"Do not suggest that he is responsible for the missing money. We are asking him to identify the process, not accuse him."



Steward Zhou looked at him.



"That is a sensible distinction."



Mingyuan wrote the instruction.



He had begun to understand how easily an investigation could become an accusation if the questions were phrased carelessly.



The family needed information.



It did not yet have evidence of wrongdoing.

When Mingzhao received the copied seal-register pages that afternoon, she examined them without speaking.



Qiao Yue waited beside her.



The pages confirmed that the estate purchasing seal had been held by Chen Rui on the relevant date.



The deputy clerk had not held it.



Mingzhao read the transfer entries.



"At least the custody record was maintained properly," Qiao Yue said.



"Yes."



"Does this answer the question?"



"It answers one question."



"Which one?"



"Who held the seal."



Qiao Yue glanced at the payment entry.



"And the money?"



"Still unidentified."



Mingzhao placed the seal register beside the old voucher copy.



She remembered Chen Rui.



He had been a careful clerk, though not especially quick. He had disliked being interrupted while checking figures and had often insisted that the steward wait until he finished a page before asking for a signature.



She did not remember the particular payment.



That was not surprising.



There had been hundreds of transactions.



But she could remember the clerk's habits.



"Chen Rui was strict about vouchers," she said.



"Does that mean the original receipt should exist?"



"It means he usually insisted on one."



Qiao Yue looked at the papers.



"Then perhaps he will remember."



"Perhaps."



Mingzhao took up her brush.



She wrote to Mingyuan.



*The seal register confirms that Chen Rui held the estate purchasing seal on the recorded date. I remember him as a clerk who generally required supporting vouchers before processing payments. This may help identify the relevant procedure, but it does not establish the recipient or purpose of the transaction. If he is contacted, please provide the exact date and amount and ask whether he recalls the voucher or its filing location.*



She read the letter again.



Then she added:



*I do not presently recall the payment itself.*



She sealed it.



Qiao Yue collected the letter.



"Your brother is making progress."



"Yes."



"Would you like to tell him so?"



Mingzhao considered it.



"No."



Qiao Yue looked at her.



"Why not?"



"He has enough work without receiving praise for every page he reads."



Qiao Yue smiled.



"That is true."



Mingzhao returned to the seal register.



The records were beginning to form a sequence.



The authorization had been recorded.



The seal had been held by a particular clerk.



The voucher had been indexed.



The original voucher had not yet been found.



The recipient remained unknown.



It was not a complete answer.



But it was no longer an entirely shapeless question.

At the silk workshop, Lin Shuyun received a message from Zhou Wenqi's office.



The buyer had arranged another inspection for the following afternoon.



Zhou Jian would arrive with the inspection list.



Lin Shuyun read the message twice.



Then she called for the workshop supervisor.



"The buyer will come tomorrow."



The supervisor nodded.



"How many bolts?"



"The next group of forty-eight."



"Are all forty-eight ready?"



"Not yet. Twenty-four have passed internal inspection. The remaining twenty-four are being finished."



"Then we must complete the second group today."



"Yes."



Lin Shuyun looked at him.



"Not by reducing the inspection."



The supervisor immediately understood.



"No. We will use the agreed process."



"Good. The buyer is coming to inspect the work, not to admire how quickly we can move unfinished bolts from one table to another."



The supervisor bowed.



He returned to the workroom.



Mingzhao arrived shortly afterward.



Lin Shuyun handed her the message.



"The buyer is coming tomorrow."



Mingzhao read it.



"Forty-eight?"



"That is the planned group."



"How many have passed internal inspection?"



"Twenty-four. The second group is being finished."



"Can it be ready by tomorrow?"



"Yes, if the workers complete the current schedule."



"Then do not change the schedule unnecessarily."



Lin Shuyun nodded.



She showed Mingzhao the production record.



The first twenty-four bolts were fully inspected.



The second group was still being finished.



Several lengths were awaiting final folding.



The workshop had enough material and enough workers to complete the group, but it had not yet done so.



Mingzhao checked the figures.



"Do not include the unfinished group in the ready total."



"I won't."



"And do not list the forty-eight as ready until all forty-eight have passed internal inspection."



"That is understood."



Lin Shuyun looked at the record.



"Some of the workers have begun referring to the group as the forty-eight."



"Then remind them that it is the planned group of forty-eight."



Lin Shuyun laughed.



"There is a difference?"



"There is a difference between what we intend to finish and what we have finished."



"That sounds like something the accounts office would say."



"It is something the accounts office should have said years ago."



Lin Shuyun shook her head.



The workshop returned to work.



The senior finisher checked the approved folding sample.



The trainees worked under their assigned supervisors.



The worker responsible for the previous uneven fold completed another inspection without incident.



There was no need to make the day's production sound more dramatic than it was.



The work was progressing.



That was sufficient.

At the Xu residence, Yuru remained in the eastern courtyard until late afternoon.



Her mother had brought out a small box of preserved fruit.



Her father had brought a book from his study.



Neither had brought a document for her to review.



When the time came to leave, Madam Xu walked with her daughter to the carriage.



"You may come again whenever you wish," she said.



"I will."



"Do not wait for an invitation every time."



Yuru looked at her.



"I will try."



Duke Xu stood a little behind them.



He appeared to be considering whether there was something else he should say.



Finally, he asked, "Would you like me to have the eastern courtyard prepared more fully?"



Yuru smiled.



"Not yet, Father."



He nodded.



"Very well."



She took her mother's hand.



"Thank you for the meal."



Madam Xu squeezed her fingers.



"Come again when you want to eat."



Yuru stepped into the carriage.



Fang Ru followed.



As the carriage moved away, Duke Xu remained at the entrance.



Madam Xu watched him.



"You did well."



"I did nothing."



"Exactly."



Duke Xu considered this.



He was not certain whether it was praise.



But he decided not to ask.

At the palace guest residence, Yuru returned before sunset.



Fang Ru helped her remove her outer robe.



"Was the meal to your liking?" she asked.



"Very much."



"Your mother seemed pleased."



"She was."



"And your father?"



Yuru smiled.



"He managed not to arrange a reception."



"That is an achievement."



"It is."



Fang Ru placed the small box of preserved fruit on the table.



"Your mother insisted that you take this."



Yuru opened it.



Inside were several neatly wrapped portions.



She looked at the box.



"She said she would not send anything every time."



Fang Ru lowered her eyes.



"Perhaps she meant that she would not send anything every time she wrote."



Yuru laughed.



"That sounds like my mother."



She set the box aside.



There would be time to share it later.



For now, she was content to sit quietly and remember the afternoon.



Her father had spoken about a land boundary dispute.



Her mother had served the chicken she liked as a child.



They had argued about the arrangement of chairs.



No one had asked her to make a decision.



It had been an ordinary visit.



She found that she wanted another one.

By the end of the eighth day of the sixth month, the former Empress had returned from her parents' home with a box of preserved fruit and a book.



Mingyuan had identified the clerk who held the estate purchasing seal on the date of an unclear payment, but the recipient and purpose remained unknown.



Mingzhao had confirmed the clerk's name and remembered his usual procedure, without claiming to remember the transaction.



The silk workshop had prepared for the buyer's next inspection, but the planned group of forty-eight had not yet been completed.



The palace had continued its ordinary administration.



The Marquisate had continued its examination of the accounts.



And the former Empress had enjoyed an afternoon in which the most difficult matter had been persuading her father that six dishes were enough.



It was, by every reasonable measure, a successful visit.



Duke Xu would probably describe it as a modest family gathering.



Madam Xu would probably describe it as a meal.



Yuru would remember it as an afternoon in which she had been allowed to be someone's daughter without being responsible for anything else.



That was enough.
