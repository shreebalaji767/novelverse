# **Chapter 117: The Former Empress Has Received an Invitation That Requires Her to Decide Whether a Visit Can Be Pleasant Without Becoming a Family Inspection. The Marquis's Eldest Daughter Has Learned That a Third Sample Is Not Necessarily Better Merely Because It Arrives Third. The Marquis's Heir Has Opened Another Box and Discovered That a Repair Receipt May Explain a Cart Without Explaining What It Carried.**

**21st Day of the Seventh Month, 18th Year of Yonghe.**

The former Empress Xu Yuru received the morning's first surprise in the form of a small wooden box.

It was not particularly large. It had been polished, tied with a plain cord, and delivered by a young palace attendant who stood at the entrance to the western guest residence with the careful expression of someone carrying an object whose contents had not been disclosed to him.

Fang Ru accepted it.

"Who sent this?" she asked.

"The Imperial Household Administration, Madam."

Fang Ru looked at the box.

The attendant immediately added, "It is not a document."

"That is reassuring."

"I was told to say that it contains no account books, registers, or official correspondence."

Fang Ru's expression became more thoughtful.

"Then whoever sent it knows the household has been receiving too many of those."

The attendant lowered his eyes.

Xu Yuru, who had been reading beside the window, looked up.

"Bring it here."

Fang Ru carried the box to the table. The cord was tied in a simple knot. Yuru loosened it and lifted the lid.

Inside lay a small porcelain tea jar, a folded piece of paper, and a wooden scoop.

The porcelain was pale green, with a narrow band of dark blue around its rim. It was neither particularly expensive nor particularly plain. The lid fitted neatly, and the jar had clearly been made for everyday use rather than display.

Yuru unfolded the note.

It contained a short message from the Dowager's household.

The Dowager had received the tea Yuru sent in thanks for the fragrant wood. She had discovered that the tea was pleasant enough to drink but that the jar in which it had arrived was inconvenient to use. The porcelain jar enclosed with the note was an old piece from her private collection. She was sending it because the lid fitted properly.

There was no request attached.

Yuru examined the jar.

"The Dowager has sent me a container because she dislikes the one I sent her."

Fang Ru smiled.

"Perhaps she thought you might find it useful."

"Or perhaps she has decided that exchanging tea jars is a more manageable occupation than exchanging palace regulations."

"That sounds much more agreeable."

Yuru turned the jar once in her hands.

It was well made. The glaze had a faint unevenness near the base, but the rim was smooth. She placed it beside the tea tray.

"Send my thanks. Tell her I shall use it."

Fang Ru nodded.

"Would you like to send tea in return?"

Yuru glanced at her.

"Not immediately. If I send another jar, she may send another one back."

"Then the exchange will continue."

"At this rate, we shall eventually possess enough jars to open a shop."

Fang Ru laughed.

Yuru returned to her book.

The small box remained on the table, its lid placed beside it. The exchange had taken less time than most palace courtesies, and that was precisely what made it pleasant.

There had been no list of recipients, no ceremonial order, no question about whether the jar belonged in one inventory or another.

Someone had sent her something because they thought she might use it.

She found that she liked the distinction.

At the Marquisate of Qingyuan, Shen Mingyuan had arrived at the records room before Steward Zhou.

He had not slept particularly well.

The four carts had become an increasingly irritating subject, partly because the surviving records were numerous enough to suggest that an answer should exist, and partly because each new paper explained one small portion while leaving the important questions untouched.

The fifth box stood on the floor beside the long table.

Its wooden sides were marked with faded ink. The old label described its contents as miscellaneous transport and repair papers transferred during the reorganization of the northern warehouse records.

Mingyuan had asked Liang Wen to prepare a list of the papers already examined. He did not want another clerk searching the same bundles merely because nobody had recorded which ones had been opened.

When Steward Zhou entered, he found Mingyuan comparing two lists.

"You have begun?"

"Only the labels."

Zhou placed a cup of tea on the table.

"That is a sensible beginning."

Mingyuan looked up.

"Do you know what is in this box?"

"No."

"Then why do you sound pleased?"

"Because you have asked before opening it."

Mingyuan gave him a look.

Zhou ignored it.

Liang Wen arrived with the register of previously examined boxes. He placed it beside the papers and waited.

Mingyuan checked the number.

"Fifth box. Contents to be recorded as found, not as expected."

Liang Wen dipped his brush.

"Yes, Young Master."

They began.

The first bundle contained repair accounts for two handcarts used by the eastern granary. The second contained a list of temporary labourers hired to move timber. The third was a set of receipts for axle grease, rope, and replacement wheel pins.

None concerned the four carts.

The fourth bundle contained several damaged transport slips. Their dates belonged to different months, and the destinations were incomplete. Mingyuan read each one before allowing Liang Wen to place it in the examined pile.

Then Steward Zhou lifted a narrow packet tied with faded thread.

"This appears to be a repair record."

Mingyuan took it.

The packet contained three papers.

The first was a general repair account for carts belonging to the northern transport office. The second was a list of replacement parts. The third was a small receipt, folded twice and stained near one corner.

Mingyuan opened it carefully.

The receipt recorded the repair of a cart axle.

The date was consistent with the period during which the fourth cart had been marked under repair.

The cart number matched.

For several moments, nobody spoke.

Mingyuan placed the receipt beside the fourth-box register.

"Is this the same cart?"

Liang Wen examined the number.

"It appears to be."

"Appears?"

"The final character is clear. The preceding mark is damaged, but the number as a whole is consistent with the other record."

Mingyuan looked at Zhou.

"Can we establish that this receipt belongs to the cart in the return register?"

"We can establish that it concerns a cart with the same recorded number and that the date is compatible. We should compare the repair details before treating the papers as one continuous record."

Mingyuan nodded.

He had begun to dislike the word *therefore*.

It was often used when a clerk had become impatient with the word *possibly*.

He read the receipt again.

The repair had been carried out by a workshop outside the northern gate. The work consisted of replacing a damaged axle pin and strengthening the wooden housing around it. The payment had been made by the transport office.

There was no mention of cargo.

No mention of the journey.

No mention of the driver.

"Who signed for the repair?" Mingyuan asked.

Liang Wen pointed to the lower corner.

"A receiving mark. The name is partly damaged."

"Can it be read?"

"Perhaps the last character. The first is uncertain."

Zhou examined it.

"It may be a surname, but I would not rely on it."

Mingyuan placed the receipt beside the register.

"Record the match as a possible supporting record. Do not identify the signer."

Liang Wen wrote.

The next paper was a workshop account listing the same axle work, but the date had been written in a different hand. It also contained a payment amount.

The amount matched the receipt.

That strengthened the connection between the two papers. It did not establish who had delivered the cart to the workshop.

Mingyuan looked at the account.

"Can we find the workshop?"

"The name is still visible," Liang Wen said. "It was called Ren's Wheelwright Shop."

"Is it still operating?"

Zhou considered.

"Not under that name, I believe. There may be a successor workshop, but we should not assume its present owner knows anything about work done years ago."

"Then we shall first look for its old business register."

Liang Wen recorded the proposed inquiry.

Mingyuan returned to the receipt.

The cart had been repaired. That was useful.

It was also much less than he had hoped.

He had imagined that the fifth box might contain the missing dispatch book, a driver's acknowledgment, or an entry naming the person who ordered the journey. Instead, it had produced an axle pin.

He placed the receipt in a separate protective sleeve.

"At least we know why the fourth cart did not return to the yard with the other three."

"Not quite," Zhou said.

Mingyuan looked at him.

"We know that a cart with the same number was repaired during the relevant period. We have not yet established that it was the same cart, nor when it was returned."

Mingyuan exhaled.

"Yes. I know."

Zhou's expression remained mild.

"Then the record is useful."

"It is useful in the manner of a man who answers a question by telling me that he once saw the person who might know the answer."

"That is sometimes the beginning of an investigation."

"It is also sometimes the beginning of a very long afternoon."

Liang Wen lowered his head over the register, though his mouth moved slightly.

Mingyuan noticed.

"Do you find something amusing?"

"No, Young Master."

"Then your brush must have become unusually entertaining."

Liang Wen continued writing.

In the eastern market district, Shen Mingzhao was examining a written statement that had arrived from the supplier.

It was shorter than the previous correspondence and considerably less satisfactory.

The merchant had acknowledged that the second dyed sample differed slightly from the reference. He had agreed to prepare a third sample but refused to guarantee that the finished material would match the reference exactly.

His explanation was that the original reference cloth might have been produced from an earlier dye batch. Small differences in raw material and weather could affect the final colour.

Mingzhao read the statement twice.

Then she placed it beside the second sample.

The cloth was close.

Close enough that a careless buyer might accept it.

Not close enough that she would promise a customer it was the same.

She called for Qiao Yue.

"Send for the alternate merchant."

"Today?"

"If he is available."

Qiao Yue looked at the written statement.

"Has the first supplier refused?"

"He has refused responsibility for the difference."

"Will he still provide the third sample?"

"Yes."

"Then he has not refused everything."

"That is true. But he wants me to accept the risk before I have accepted the material."

Mingzhao folded the statement.

"If the customer rejects the cloth, the workers will still have been paid, the dye will still have been used, and the supplier will still have received his money. I would be the only person left explaining why a sample was treated as a promise."

Qiao Yue nodded.

"Shall I tell him we will not proceed?"

"No. Tell him the third sample may still be examined, but the terms remain unsettled."

Qiao Yue made a note.

Mingzhao turned to the workshop account book.

The previous two orders were closed. The wages had been paid, the suppliers settled, and the ordinary reserve remained intact. There was money available for a small trial, but money available was not the same as money that needed to be spent.

She had already told the foreman that the six-bolt trial was conditional.

She had no intention of changing that merely because the supplier had sent another letter.

The foreman entered after Qiao Yue left.

"Madam, the workers have asked whether there will be a new order."

Mingzhao looked up.

"What did you tell them?"

"That the six-bolt trial is not yet approved for production."

"Good."

"They asked whether the workshop will continue employing them."

"Did you tell them what I said before?"

"That existing agreements will be honoured, but no further work has been promised."

"Then you answered correctly."

The foreman hesitated.

"Some of them are worried."

"They are entitled to be worried. I would be worried too if my income depended on an order that had not been placed."

He nodded.

"I understand."

"Do not reassure them with promises I have not made. If we receive a proper order, we shall discuss the work. If we do not, they must be free to seek employment elsewhere."

The foreman looked uncomfortable.

He had been hoping for a more comforting answer. Mingzhao understood that, but she did not believe comfort justified an inaccurate promise.

She opened the small notebook in which she recorded possible work.

Beside the six-bolt trial, she wrote:

*Third sample awaited. Written variation terms unresolved. No material purchase. No production.*

The foreman glanced at the note.

"Would it not be better to make six bolts while we wait? We could finish them quickly if the merchant agrees."

"No."

"Even if the sample is nearly correct?"

"Especially then."

He looked at her.

Mingzhao placed the second sample beside the written statement.

"If the material is wrong, making six bolts will not make it right. It will merely turn six pieces of cloth into six more expensive arguments."

The foreman considered that.

"Then we wait."

"We wait for an acceptable sample and clear terms. Not merely for the merchant to become tired of writing letters."

The foreman bowed and left.

Mingzhao returned to her accounts.

The workshop had become more orderly because she had learned to distinguish activity from progress. She did not intend to abandon that lesson merely because a small order seemed convenient.

At the temporary palace residence, Xu Yuru had reached the end of the garden book's chapter on paths.

She closed it and looked toward the courtyard.

The stepping stones described in the book were intended to guide visitors across damp ground without forcing them to walk in a straight line. The author had discussed their placement at length, including the distance between stones and the direction in which a person was likely to turn.

Yuru had read the passage the previous evening.

This morning she found herself looking at the stones outside her window.

The courtyard's path had been laid many years earlier. Several stones were uneven, but none appeared dangerous. A visitor who walked slowly could cross without difficulty.

She wondered whether the original designer had considered the view from the sitting room.

Then she decided that the question did not require an answer.

Fang Ru entered with the tea jar.

"The Dowager's attendant has returned."

Yuru looked up.

"Did she bring another jar?"

"No. She brought a message."

Fang Ru placed the message beside the book.

The Dowager had received Yuru's thanks. She was pleased that the jar would be used. She also invited Yuru to join her for a quiet afternoon of tea later in the week, if Yuru had no other plans.

Yuru smiled.

"That is a pleasant invitation."

"Shall I reply that you will attend?"

"Yes. Thank her."

Fang Ru folded the message.

"Would you like to bring something?"

Yuru thought for a moment.

"Perhaps the tea she sent me."

"The stronger one?"

"No. The second package."

Fang Ru looked amused.

"Then the Dowager may discover that you have remembered her preference."

"She may also discover that I have remembered the first package was too strong."

"That is a dangerous amount of knowledge to possess."

"Only if she asks me to prepare the tea."

Fang Ru laughed.

Yuru picked up the porcelain jar and examined its lid again.

"Have the tea leaves been transferred?"

"Not yet."

"Then let us do that after breakfast."

Fang Ru looked at the jar.

"Would you like me to prepare it?"

Yuru considered the question.

"Show me how you would arrange it first. I have never used this shape of jar."

Fang Ru nodded.

They moved the tea leaves into the new container together. The wooden scoop fitted neatly inside. The lid closed without catching, and the jar sat steadily on the tray.

Yuru was satisfied.

It was a small improvement, but a useful one.

She returned to the garden book, while Fang Ru carried the old jar to the side table.

After a moment, Yuru spoke.

"Would you like to keep that one?"

Fang Ru turned.

"The old jar?"

"It is still usable. You may use it for your own tea."

Fang Ru looked at the plain porcelain vessel.

"It is not particularly valuable."

"That is why I thought you might use it without worrying about damaging it."

Fang Ru smiled.

"Then I will keep it."

Yuru nodded and reopened her book.

The former Empress had once owned more ceremonial vessels than she could remember. Many had been selected for her by people who understood that the palace expected her to possess beautiful things.

This jar had been sent because its lid fitted.

She preferred it.

At the Marquisate, the search through the fifth box continued.

After the axle receipt, Liang Wen found a bundle of transport office wage statements. Mingyuan examined the dates, then compared the names with the driver and temporary labour lists already collected.

There were several familiar names.

None established who had driven the four carts.

One paper recorded a payment to a cart repair workshop. Another showed that the northern transport office had received a small allowance for replacement parts.

Mingyuan read the figures.

"Does this amount include the axle repair?"

Liang Wen compared the entries.

"The repair account records the same sum, but the allowance covers several parts and more than one cart."

"So we cannot say the whole allowance was spent on this repair."

"No, Young Master."

"Then record it as a related financial entry, not as proof of the repair payment."

Liang Wen wrote it down.

Steward Zhou examined the next packet.

"This may be a transport office inventory."

Mingyuan took it.

The paper was a list of equipment issued to the office during a previous year. It included ropes, wooden supports, wheel pins, two spare axles, and several iron fittings.

At the bottom, a note stated that damaged equipment should be returned to the yard storehouse or accounted for through a repair receipt.

Mingyuan looked at the repair receipt again.

"That is consistent with the procedure."

"Yes," Zhou said.

"But it still does not tell us who returned the cart."

"No."

Mingyuan put the inventory aside.

The records were beginning to reveal the ordinary life of the transport office. Equipment was issued, carts were repaired, money was paid, and goods were moved.

The difficulty was that the particular movement they were investigating had been recorded incompletely.

He looked at the fifth box.

"How many bundles remain?"

"Three," Liang Wen replied.

"Continue."

The next bundle contained a series of warehouse transfer slips. Most belonged to unrelated dates. One had a reference to the northern road, but the date was outside the period under investigation.

Mingyuan read it, marked it as unrelated, and moved on.

The final bundle contained a thin paper register with several pages missing.

Its surviving entries concerned the return of equipment from the northern transport office to the central yard. The dates were spread across two months.

Mingyuan turned the pages carefully.

One entry recorded the return of a spare axle.

Another recorded a damaged wheel.

A third mentioned a cart brought back from a repair workshop.

The date matched the repair receipt.

The cart number was partly obscured by a stain.

Liang Wen leaned closer.

"That may be the same number."

"Can you compare it?"

"I can compare the visible marks, but I cannot restore the damaged character."

Mingyuan examined the entry.

The surviving strokes were consistent with the number on the receipt.

He looked at Zhou.

"Does this establish that the cart returned to the yard after repair?"

"It establishes that a cart with a similar number was recorded as returned from a repair workshop on that date. The connection is strong enough to investigate further, but we should preserve the damaged character and the uncertainty."

Mingyuan nodded.

"Then we have a possible sequence: a cart assigned to the northern office, later recorded under repair, repaired by the workshop, and subsequently recorded as returned."

"Yes."

"And still no cargo."

"Correct."

"No destination."

"Correct."

"No driver."

"Correct."

Mingyuan set the papers down.

"At least this time the records agree about something."

Zhou smiled faintly.

"That is progress."

Mingyuan glanced at the old register.

"Provided the cart number is correct."

"Provided the cart number is correct."

Mingyuan leaned back.

He had learned enough from the previous inquiries to resist turning a plausible sequence into a complete story. The four carts had been recorded. Three had been returned. A fourth had apparently been repaired. The surviving records still did not explain what the carts had carried or where they had gone.

The missing dispatch book might answer those questions.

Or it might contain only another list of dates and incomplete names.

"Record the fifth box as examined," he said. "Preserve the receipt and the return register together, but keep their original order and separate descriptions."

Liang Wen finished the entry.

"Should we ask the workshop's successor about the old repair?"

"First find out whether the shop still has its records. If it does not, there is no reason to send someone there merely to ask whether they remember a cart from years ago."

Zhou nodded.

Mingyuan looked at the stack of papers.

"Then the sixth box tomorrow."

Liang Wen hesitated.

"Young Master, there are still several loose bundles from the fifth box that may need to be checked against the transfer list."

Mingyuan closed his eyes briefly.

"Then we shall finish those first."

Steward Zhou picked up his tea.

"That is also progress."

Mingyuan gave him a tired look.

"Please do not use that word again today."

By late afternoon, Shen Mingzhao had received a reply from the alternate merchant.

He could provide a similar material, but not the exact weave used in the reference sample. He was willing to prepare a dyed piece for comparison. His price, however, was higher than the first supplier's quotation.

Mingzhao read the letter and placed it beside the first merchant's statement.

Neither option was satisfactory.

The first supplier offered a lower price but refused responsibility for variation. The second offered a different material at a higher price without guaranteeing an exact match.

She asked Qiao Yue to summon the foreman.

When he arrived, she showed him both letters.

"Which material would be easier to work with?"

The foreman read the descriptions.

"The second merchant's material may be easier to handle. But we cannot know until we see it."

"Would the difference in price affect the six-bolt trial?"

"It would reduce the margin."

"By how much?"

He calculated the difference using the quotation.

"Not enough to make the trial impossible, but enough that we should not proceed unless the sample is acceptable."

Mingzhao nodded.

"Then we shall wait for the second sample as well."

The foreman returned the letters.

"That means we may have to wait several more days."

"Yes."

He looked toward the workshop courtyard.

"The workers are asking again."

"I know."

"Some have been approached by another workshop."

"Then they should consider the offer."

The foreman looked surprised.

"Even though we may need them?"

"We may need them if the order is placed. We do not need them to refuse other work while we are still deciding whether to buy material."

The foreman was silent.

Mingzhao continued.

"If they leave, we may have to hire and train replacements. That will cost us time. But it is still better than asking them to remain idle on the strength of a possibility."

He bowed.

"I will tell them."

"Tell them the truth. If they choose to remain available, we shall discuss it when the order is confirmed. If they accept other work, we shall not treat that as disloyalty."

The foreman left with the two letters.

Mingzhao returned to her desk and reviewed the workshop's cash reserve.

The six-bolt trial remained a possibility, not a commitment.

She wrote the same conclusion in the order notebook.

Then she closed it.

For once, the most important business decision of the day had been to purchase nothing.

That evening, Xu Yuru received a short reply from the Dowager.

The Dowager approved of the proposed tea and added that Yuru need not bring anything else. She had enough gifts to fill several cabinets and did not intend to turn an afternoon visit into a formal exchange.

Yuru read the note and smiled.

"She has anticipated my concern."

Fang Ru looked at the message.

"Then you may attend without preparing anything."

"Yes."

"That should make the visit easy."

"It should."

Yuru placed the note inside her correspondence box.

She had not yet decided where she would live after leaving the temporary residence. The eastern courtyard at her parents' home remained available. The guest residence remained comfortable. Neither choice needed to be made merely because another person might prefer a settled answer.

She could visit her parents at the end of the month, spend an afternoon with the Dowager, and continue reading the books she had borrowed.

Those were sufficient plans.

At the Marquisate, Mingyuan was still examining the last loose papers from the fifth box. Mingzhao was still waiting for two merchants to provide samples neither had yet proved satisfactory.

None of the three had obtained the answer they wanted.

But each had made a decision about what to do next.

Yuru would take the Dowager's tea.

Mingzhao would not purchase uncertain material.

Mingyuan would preserve the repair records without claiming they explained the missing journey.

In the former Empress's residence, the new tea jar sat beside the old one.

In Mingzhao's workshop, the six-bolt trial remained unwritten on the production board.

And in the Marquisate's records room, the four-cart investigation had gained an axle pin, a repair receipt, and another reason not to declare victory.

Steward Zhou, passing the open door on his way out, glanced at the papers.

"At least the cart has been repaired."

Mingyuan looked up from his register.

"That is not the same as finding out where it went."

"No," Zhou agreed. "But it is better than discovering that the axle was never broken."

Mingyuan considered this.

Then he looked back at the papers.

"Tomorrow, we shall find out whether the sixth box contains the dispatch book."

Zhou nodded.

"And if it does not?"

Mingyuan dipped his brush.

"Then I shall be grateful that the box is not large enough to contain a seventh."
