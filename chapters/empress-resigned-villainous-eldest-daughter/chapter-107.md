# **Chapter 107: The Former Empress Has Discovered That a Garden Book Can Contain More Useful Advice Than a Household Manual Without Once Suggesting That She Should Personally Inspect Every Flower. The Marquis's Eldest Daughter Has Finished an Order, Closed Its Accounts, and Learned That a Profitable Workshop Still Requires a Decision About What It Should Do Next. The Marquis's Heir Has Received Another Question About Four Carts and Has Discovered That a Man May Remember Sending Them Somewhere Without Remembering What They Carried.**

**11th Day of the Seventh Month, 18th Year of Yonghe.**

The former Empress had reached the section of the garden book that discussed trees.

She had expected something about pruning.

Instead, the author began by explaining that a tree planted in the wrong place might grow for many years without becoming healthy. Its leaves could remain green, its branches could lengthen, and an inexperienced observer might declare it successful. Yet its roots could be crowded, its trunk weakened by poor drainage, and its growth distorted by the wall beside it.

Xu Yuru paused.

She read the passage again.

Then she looked out through the open window of the western guest residence.

A small tree stood beside the courtyard wall. Its branches extended toward the sunlight, while the lower portion remained relatively bare. It had not appeared remarkable to her before. Now she wondered whether it had been planted there deliberately or whether the gardener had inherited an arrangement made by someone else.

She did not summon him.

The distinction mattered.

She was permitted to be curious without turning curiosity into an instruction.

Fang Ru entered carrying a small tray of tea and noticed that the book was open beside the window.

"Madam, have you found something interesting?"

"The author has begun discussing trees."

"Is it a difficult subject?"

"Not especially. He appears to believe that planting a tree in the wrong place is a mistake that may take ten years to become obvious."

Fang Ru considered this.

"That is a long time to wait before discovering an error."

"Some people are fortunate enough to discover their errors sooner."

Fang Ru poured the tea.

The former Empress glanced at her.

"That was not a criticism of anyone."

"I did not say it was."

"Your expression suggested that you had already identified several candidates."

Fang Ru lowered her eyes.

"I was thinking about the garden."

"Of course."

Yuru turned another page.

The book contained a description of soil, roots, water, shade, and the different needs of trees that might appear similar above the ground. The author recommended observing a place through the seasons before deciding which species should be planted there.

Yuru found this advice sensible.

She also found it reassuring.

For years, people had brought her arrangements that were already in motion. A household needed a new storehouse. A ceremony required additional attendants. A dispute between servants had grown complicated. A supply contract had been delayed. A senior official's wife had requested a particular audience.

Every matter arrived with an implied expectation that the person receiving it should immediately determine what ought to happen.

Yet a garden did not become healthier because someone issued instructions every morning.

Sometimes the sensible course was to observe.

Sometimes it was to leave the gardener alone.

She closed the book halfway and took her tea.

"Has the Duke sent another letter?" she asked.

"No, Madam. Only the note from your mother yesterday, and the package of preserved plums."

"Then there is nothing requiring a reply today."

Fang Ru smiled faintly.

"There is also the question of whether you wish to visit your parents tomorrow."

Yuru considered it.

"I have not decided."

"Your mother did not request an answer."

"Then she has learned to leave a question unanswered."

"Perhaps she has always known how."

Yuru looked at the attendant.

Fang Ru had been with her long enough to understand that a gentle remark could be more pointed than a formal rebuke.

"Perhaps," Yuru said.

She returned to the book.

Outside, the gardener carried a shallow basket across the courtyard. He stopped near the tree, examined the earth around its base, and moved a small stone away from the roots.

Yuru watched for a moment.

Then she looked back at the page.

She did not need to know whether the stone had been there for three days or three years.

For once, the garden was allowed to remain the gardener's responsibility.

At the eastern market, Shen Mingzhao had begun the morning by refusing to look at a finished account.

This was not because the account was unsatisfactory.

It was because she had already examined it twice the previous evening.

The eighty bolts had been delivered. The warehouse count had matched the carrier's count. Gao Wen's representative had inspected the goods against the approved sample and accepted all of them. The remaining balance had arrived. The workers had been paid, the suppliers settled, and the carrier's fee discharged.

The order was finished.

There was no missing payment waiting to be discovered, no unfinished group requiring a new inspection, and no unsettled supplier demanding an explanation.

Mingzhao had closed the account.

She had also written the final profit in the proper column.

It was not an extraordinary sum.

It was, however, a genuine profit earned under a contract whose conditions had been clear.

She had no intention of diminishing that achievement merely because the number was modest.

Qiao Yue placed a fresh sheet of paper beside her.

"Miss, the workshop foreman has sent the final list of available materials."

"Has he included the material reserved for existing household use?"

"Yes. It is listed separately."

"And the unused dye?"

"Also separate."

"Good."

Mingzhao examined the figures.

The workshop had used less material than the initial estimate, partly because the first trial had exposed a problem before the full quantity was committed. The correction had cost time, but it had prevented a much larger loss.

The extra workers hired for the order had been paid according to the agreed arrangement. The two trainees had improved. Neither had become an expert merely because one order had ended, but both could now perform their assigned tasks with less supervision.

The experienced workers were also available.

That did not mean the workshop could accept any quantity offered to it.

Mingzhao placed the material list beside the closed account.

"How many days can the workshop operate at its present pace without purchasing additional material?"

Qiao Yue answered from the foreman's note.

"That depends on whether the next work requires the same silk and dye. The available stock could support a small order, but not another eighty bolts of the same specification."

"How much would it cost to replenish the materials?"

"The foreman has given an estimate. The dye merchant has not confirmed his price."

"Then the estimate is not a purchase price."

"No, Miss."

Mingzhao tapped the paper.

"Do not let the workshop buy merely because the money is available."

Qiao Yue nodded.

The previous order had been profitable, but the temptation to treat a successful transaction as proof that every following transaction would be equally sensible was precisely the kind of mistake Mingzhao disliked.

A workshop could have skilled hands, available looms, and a willing buyer.

It could also have too much stock, an unreliable payment arrangement, or a delivery date that left no room for correction.

The fact that the last order had gone well did not excuse careless terms in the next one.

"Ask the foreman to prepare three separate estimates," she said. "One for maintaining the present workforce, one for a small order using existing materials, and one for replenishing enough stock to undertake a larger order."

"Should he include the cost of additional workers?"

"Only as a separate possibility. We are not hiring anyone before we know what work is available."

Qiao Yue took the instruction.

After a moment, she asked, "And if a buyer approaches us today?"

"Then we listen."

"Without accepting?"

"Without accepting before we know what is being offered."

Qiao Yue smiled.

"The foreman may be disappointed."

"The foreman is welcome to be disappointed after receiving his wages."

That was not entirely fair to the foreman, who had never suggested that he wished to be paid in advance for an imaginary order.

Still, Mingzhao was not inclined to apologize for requiring the workshop to distinguish between work and expectation.

She gathered the finished account and placed it in the closed-order file.

The eighty bolts had earned their place there.

The next eighty, if there were to be any, would have to earn theirs separately.

At the Marquisate of Qingyuan, Shen Mingyuan was reading a letter that did not contain the answer he wanted.

He read it twice.

Then he placed it beside Li Shun's earlier reply.

The new letter answered the questions sent after the first inquiry.

Li Shun remembered two drivers who had regularly taken northern-road assignments during the relevant period. He believed their surnames were Sun and Wei, though he could not provide their full names with certainty.

He also remembered that the dispatch had been requested through the transport supervisor's office.

He did not remember which supervisor had given the instruction.

As for a personal copy of the movement entry, he had kept occasional rough notes when the office was busy, but he could not say whether he had retained one for this particular dispatch.

The letter ended with an apology for the uncertainty.

Mingyuan set it down.

Steward Zhou De stood across the desk.

"Is that enough to identify the drivers?"

"No."

"Can we find them through the old transport roster?"

"If the roster survives."

"And the orderer?"

"Not yet."

Zhou glanced at the two letters.

"Li Shun remembers preparing the entry, remembers the direction, and remembers the office through which the instruction came. He does not remember the cargo, the final destination, the person who ordered it, or whether he kept a copy."

"That is what he remembers."

Zhou paused.

"It is not much."

"It is more than we had before."

Mingyuan did not sound satisfied, but he was not dismissive.

The four-cart entry had been found in the northern warehouse movement papers. It recorded a movement of four carts, contained an incomplete destination, left the cargo field blank, and included a note that confirmation would be made upon arrival.

There was no receipt attached.

Li Shun's letters made it more likely that he had prepared the entry. They did not prove what the carts carried or where they had gone.

Mingyuan had learned that the difference between a useful lead and a completed explanation was often several missing pages.

He was beginning to suspect that the estate's records had been maintained by people who regarded blank spaces as a temporary inconvenience.

The trouble was that temporary inconveniences had survived longer than the people who created them.

"Write to the transport office again," he said. "Ask for the roster covering that month. Search for the two drivers under both surnames, but do not assume that the names are correct. Ask whether the office retained a daily dispatch list."

Zhou nodded.

"Should we ask Li Shun to come in person?"

"Not yet. A written answer is sufficient for these questions. If we identify the drivers, we can ask whether their recollections agree with his."

"And if the roster is missing?"

"Record that it is missing."

Zhou looked at him.

Mingyuan met his gaze.

"We will not write down that the roster was never made merely because we cannot find it."

The steward inclined his head.

"Understood."

Mingyuan picked up the first letter again.

There was another matter.

"The damaged notebook register," he said. "Has anyone examined the remaining pages for other issues to the transport supervisor's office?"

"Liang Wen has begun checking them. The surviving entries are incomplete, but there may be other references."

"Have him make a list. Do not let him reconstruct the torn signature column from memory."

"I will tell him."

Mingyuan placed both letters in a separate folder.

He wrote the folder's heading carefully:

**Four-Cart Movement: Dispatch Entry and Custody of Notebook.**

Below it, he added:

**Cargo and destination remain unconfirmed.**

He stared at the sentence.

It looked unsatisfactory.

That was precisely why it needed to remain.

In the accounts room, Liang Wen had a different difficulty.

He had been instructed to prepare a consolidated list of the purchasing records that remained incomplete. The list was not intended to settle the property division, nor was it intended to explain every disputed payment in the estate.

It was supposed to show what had been found, what had not been found, and what further evidence might be required.

This sounded simple until he began writing.

A missing voucher could mean that the original had been misplaced. It could mean that it had been filed in another bundle. It could mean that the payment entry was inaccurate. It could also mean that the payment had been made under a different description.

Each possibility required a different investigation.

He had already found that writing "unverified" was easier than writing a confident explanation that later had to be withdrawn.

Steward Zhou entered and reviewed the sheet.

"You have separated the grain discrepancy from the four-cart movement?"

"Yes."

"And the old purchasing voucher?"

"Separate again."

"Why?"

Liang Wen looked up.

"Because the documents do not establish that they concern the same transaction."

Zhou examined the headings.

The grain discrepancy still showed the original figures:

- Forty-seven sacks recorded.
- Twenty sacks covered by the surviving instruction.
- Eighteen sacks recorded as received.
- Two sacks not yet confirmed.
- Twenty-seven sacks unexplained.

The four-cart movement appeared on another page.

The old purchasing voucher appeared on a third.

No total had been adjusted.

No missing quantity had been assigned to the carts.

No payment had been attributed to Mingzhao merely because her name appeared in the old estate accounts.

Zhou read the entries once more.

"Good. Keep them separate."

Liang Wen hesitated.

"Some people may ask why the inquiries are taking so long."

"Then explain what remains to be established."

"That may not satisfy them."

"Your task is to prepare an accurate record, not a satisfying one."

Liang Wen lowered his head.

Zhou returned the sheet.

"One more thing. The former manager's personal advances remain separate from the estate's ordinary expenditure. Do not place a payment in her account simply because she once managed the office."

"Yes, Steward."

"And the furnishing expenditure?"

"Still classified as estate expenditure. It was paid from the furnishing account."

"Good."

Liang Wen gathered the papers.

The work was less dramatic than he had expected when he first entered the accounts office. He had imagined that a search for missing money would end with the discovery of a single concealed ledger and a name written in the margin.

Instead, he was spending his days identifying the limits of what could be established.

He had begun to understand why Mingzhao had demanded written evidence before agreeing to a family meeting.

A meeting could settle a question.

It could not manufacture the documents required to answer it.

At the Marquis's residence, Shen Guowei received the latest report from Zhou.

He was not pleased.

The family division had already taken longer than he expected. The revised schedule was more careful than the first, but every correction seemed to reveal another document that had to be located.

He put the report down.

"How many more inquiries are necessary?"

Zhou stood before him.

"Several remain open, Marquis."

"That is not a number."

"No, Marquis."

Guowei looked at him.

"Can the estate not simply agree on the property that is clearly ours and divide the rest later?"

"Some of the property can be classified. The disputed payments and missing records affect the final balance."

"Then separate those matters."

"That is what the revised schedule does."

Guowei's expression tightened.

He looked again at the papers.

The schedule did not accuse Mingzhao of taking anything. It did not establish that the estate owed her every amount she had listed. It simply preserved the distinctions between confirmed advances, payments with unclear repayment arrangements, uncertain claims, and unverified sums.

He disliked the uncertainty.

He disliked even more that the uncertainty could not be removed by ordering someone to write a cleaner figure.

"Has Mingzhao agreed to attend a meeting?"

"No, Marquis. She has asked for a clear agenda and the supporting documents first."

"She is still making demands."

"She is asking to know what the meeting is intended to decide."

Guowei looked up sharply.

Zhou did not retreat from the statement.

For a moment, the room was quiet.

At last, the Marquis leaned back.

"Send her the revised schedule when it is ready."

"Yes, Marquis."

"And tell her that I expect this matter to proceed."

"I will convey that."

Zhou bowed and withdrew.

Guowei remained at his desk.

He had once believed that the division of the estate would be a matter of determining what belonged to each person and then recording the result.

He was now learning that the estate had not kept its own history in a form that made such a decision easy.

He could not blame every missing document on Mingzhao.

He could not comfortably blame every unclear payment on the clerks.

And he could not order his daughter to resume managing the records when she had already left the position.

The family had not yet found a way to make the past simpler.

Mingzhao received the workshop foreman's three estimates shortly before midday.

The first described the cost of keeping the present workforce available for ordinary production.

The second calculated how many bolts could be produced from existing materials without further purchases.

The third estimated the amount required to replenish stock for a larger order.

The figures were not final.

The dye merchant had not confirmed his price, and one supplier had offered a rate that depended on the quantity purchased.

Mingzhao examined the three sheets.

The first estimate was conservative.

The second was practical but limited.

The third offered the greatest opportunity and the greatest exposure.

She placed them in that order.

Qiao Yue stood beside the table.

"Which does Miss prefer?"

"The first, for now."

"Even though it produces nothing?"

"It preserves the workshop without committing us to materials we may not need."

"The workers may ask whether there will be another order."

"Tell them the last order has been completed and paid. We are reviewing the next arrangement."

"Will they remain?"

"Those whose agreements cover this period will remain. Any extension must be discussed before the present term ends."

Qiao Yue nodded.

Mingzhao looked again at the second estimate.

A small order might be possible with the existing materials. It would keep the workshop occupied without requiring a large purchase. But no buyer had made a suitable offer.

There was no reason to create an order simply to avoid an idle loom.

She had spent six years managing a household that treated activity as proof of usefulness. If a room was quiet, someone found a task for its occupants. If a storehouse had spare capacity, someone filled it. If an account had an unused column, someone entered a figure.

She had no intention of recreating the same habits in her own business.

"Ask the foreman to review the condition of the looms and the storage rooms," she said. "The workshop should remain ready for work, but no additional material is to be purchased until the prices are confirmed."

"And the trainees?"

"Continue their ordinary instruction. Do not give them a new task merely to keep them busy."

Qiao Yue looked amused.

"That may be difficult for them."

"Then they may learn to sit quietly when there is nothing to do."

Mingzhao returned the estimates.

The foreman had done what she asked. The workshop had finished an order, recorded its costs, and identified its present capacity.

That was enough for one day.

A profitable workshop did not need to become an impatient one.

In the western guest residence, Xu Yuru had reached the end of the chapter on trees.

She had marked only one passage.

The author recommended that a gardener should know the condition of the soil before selecting a tree, because a tree that flourished in one place might struggle in another.

Yuru closed the book.

Fang Ru was folding a clean cloth near the table.

"Would you like another book from the library?" she asked.

"No."

Fang Ru glanced at the book.

"Have you finished this one?"

"I have finished the chapter."

"That is not the same thing."

"No. I am learning not to confuse the two."

Fang Ru placed the folded cloth in a basket.

Yuru looked toward the courtyard again.

The gardener had finished tending the tree. He was speaking to a younger worker, pointing toward a patch of ground near the wall. The younger man listened, then carried away a small basket.

Neither looked toward the window.

Yuru was pleased by this.

A garden was not improved by having its former supervisor observe every movement.

She picked up her tea.

"Tomorrow," she said, "I may visit my parents."

"Shall I prepare a carriage?"

"Not yet. I will decide in the morning."

Fang Ru accepted the answer without making another suggestion.

Yuru opened the book once more, not because she intended to continue reading, but because she wanted to look at the illustration of the tree roots.

The drawing showed how the roots spread beneath the earth, far beyond the circle visible at the trunk.

She studied it for a moment.

Then she turned the page.

There was no report to review, no account to reconcile, and no household waiting for her to decide whether a particular tree had been planted correctly.

For the first time in many years, she could read about something without being expected to manage it.

She found that she rather liked the arrangement.

That evening, Mingyuan received no further answer from the transport office.

The roster had not yet been located.

Liang Wen's consolidated list was still being checked.

The property schedule had not been sent to Mingzhao.

The grain discrepancy remained unresolved.

The old purchasing voucher remained missing.

The four-cart movement remained a four-cart movement whose cargo and destination were unconfirmed.

Mingyuan read the notes one last time before putting them away.

He had once thought that becoming heir meant learning how to make decisions.

He was now discovering that it also meant learning when a decision was not yet justified.

This was less satisfying than he had expected.

It was, however, preferable to making a mistake and having the entire family inherit it.

He closed the folder.

Across the estate, the Marquis was still waiting for the accounts to become simpler, while Mingzhao was waiting for the property schedule to become sufficiently complete to discuss.

Neither had received what they wanted.

The workshop, at least, had finished its work.

And in the western guest residence, the former Empress had reached a chapter about the proper distance between trees and walls.

She had decided that she would read the next chapter tomorrow.

There was no reason to hurry.

A tree, the author had explained, did not grow faster because someone stood beside it and watched.

Xu Yuru thought this was excellent advice.

She hoped the author would continue writing in that manner.
