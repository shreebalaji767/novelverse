# **Chapter 26: The Empress Has Six Days Left. The Marquis's Eldest Daughter Has Two. The Neighbour Has Brought a Measuring Rope and an Opinion.**



**18th Day of the Fourth Month, 18th Year of Yonghe.**



The imperial palace had discovered that transferring responsibility was considerably easier on paper than in practice.



This was not a new discovery.



It was merely becoming increasingly difficult to ignore.



At Chengming Palace, Empress Xu Yuru sat before a ceremonial schedule that had already passed through the hands of three departments.



The first version had been prepared by the Department of Rites.



The second had been amended by the Imperial Household Administration.



The third had been returned by the Department of Personnel because two attendants listed for the ceremony had been transferred to different duties several weeks earlier.



Yuru read the third version.



Then she read the attached explanation.



Then she put the papers down.



"Who checked the names?"



Fang Ru, standing beside the desk, answered carefully.



"The Department of Rites checked the ceremonial order. The Imperial Household Administration checked the arrangements. The Department of Personnel checked the current assignments."



"Then why are the names wrong?"



Fang Ru hesitated.



"The three departments checked different registers."



Yuru looked at her.



"Did none of them think to compare the registers?"



"They have now."



"How reassuring."



Fang Ru lowered her eyes, although the corners of her mouth threatened to move.



The ceremony was five days away after today. Six, if one counted the remaining full days before the twenty-third.



The official seals had already been transferred into the custody of the Imperial Archives. The records of the Empress's administrative responsibilities had been distributed among the offices assigned to receive them. The transition was proceeding.



And yet, every day produced another small reminder of how many people had grown accustomed to asking Chengming Palace for permission before doing something they were already authorized to do.



Yuru picked up the schedule again.



"Do not send this back to me after correcting the names."



Fang Ru looked up.



"Your Majesty?"



"Send the corrected schedule to the Department of Rites. They are responsible for the ceremony. If the attendants' assignments are wrong, the Department of Personnel must confirm them directly."



"Should Chengming Palace retain a copy?"



"A copy of the final schedule, yes. Not a copy of every department's correspondence."



Fang Ru nodded.



Yuru turned to the next document.



It was a request for approval of additional ceremonial silk.



The original allocation had been calculated according to the expected number of participants. The revised request included an additional twenty lengths, apparently because the ceremonial officers had decided that several officials might require replacements if the fabric was damaged during preparation.



Yuru read the explanation twice.



"How many lengths have already been issued?"



"One hundred and forty."



"How many have been damaged?"



"Three."



"How many replacements are required?"



"Three, according to the original report."



"Then why are they requesting twenty?"



Fang Ru consulted the attached note.



"The Department of Rites says it would be prudent to have additional material."



"Prudence is not a quantity."



"No, Your Majesty."



"Ask them to submit the number of additional participants, the intended use, and the existing reserve. If they cannot explain those figures, they may conduct the ceremony with the silk already issued."



Fang Ru accepted the papers.



Yuru returned to her writing.



She had not become less capable of administering the palace.



That was the difficulty.



People continued to bring her matters because she could resolve them quickly. She could read three contradictory reports, identify the missing figure, and return a clear instruction before the person who submitted them had finished explaining the problem.



It would have been easy to continue.



It would also have defeated the entire purpose of her departure.



"Fang Ru."



"Your Majesty."



"When the corrected ceremonial schedule arrives, who will approve the final arrangements?"



"The Department of Rites, under the authority of the responsible ceremonial officials."



"And if they disagree with the Imperial Household Administration?"



"They must settle the division of responsibility according to the approved arrangements."



"And if they cannot?"



"Then the matter should be referred to the appropriate superior office."



"Not here."



"Not here."



Yuru nodded.



"Good."



Fang Ru took the papers and prepared to leave.



At the door, she paused.



"Your Majesty, the Crown Prince has requested permission to observe the final rehearsal."



"Has he been given the schedule?"



"Yes."



"Then he may observe it."



"Should he receive any special instructions?"



Yuru considered the question.



"Tell him to observe the ceremony, not manage it."



Fang Ru bowed.



That instruction, she suspected, would be useful to more than one person.



In the Hall of Mental Cultivation, Emperor Zhao Jingheng was discovering that the Crown Prince had begun to ask questions that could not be answered with a simple order.



Zhao Cheng'an stood before the emperor's desk with a report from the Eastern Palace.



The report concerned the allocation of servants, household expenditure, and the distribution of provisions to several study halls.



The Crown Prince had marked three entries.



"The Eastern Palace has been receiving additional lamp oil from the central stores," he said.



"How much?"



"Enough to make the quarterly expenditure appear higher than the original allocation."



"Was it necessary?"



"Possibly. The number of evening study sessions increased."



"Then what is the problem?"



"The additional issue was recorded under the Crown Prince's household, but the order was submitted by the central stores."



The emperor looked at the report.



"Who authorized it?"



"The records identify the receiving office but not the person who approved the additional quantity."



"Ask the stores."



"I did. They say the Eastern Palace requested it."



"Ask the Eastern Palace."



"I did. They say the stores suggested it."



Zhao Jingheng leaned back.



For a moment, neither spoke.



Then the emperor looked at his son.



"What do you intend to do?"



"Compare the original request with the issue register and the delivery record."



"Good."



"If the additional oil was necessary, the expenditure should be recorded correctly. If it was not, the allocation should be adjusted."



"Also good."



The Crown Prince hesitated.



"Father, should the Empress's office be informed?"



"No."



"Even if the records were previously reviewed there?"



"Especially then."



Zhao Cheng'an lowered his eyes.



The emperor picked up his brush.



"Your mother's departure does not mean that every unfinished matter must be sent back to her. You have an office. The stores have an office. The people who approved the expenditure have names. Find them."



"Yes, Father."



The Crown Prince bowed and withdrew.



Zhao Jingheng looked at the report again.



His son was becoming more competent.



Unfortunately, competence had a habit of producing additional questions.



He called for Gao Shun.



"How many ceremonial matters are still being sent to Chengming Palace?"



"Some routine matters, Your Majesty."



"How many?"



Gao Shun did not answer immediately.



The emperor looked up.



"That many?"



"Your Majesty, the number has decreased."



"That is not a number."



Gao Shun bowed.



"I will have the office prepare an exact count."



"Do not have the office prepare a count of the count. I want the actual number."



"Yes, Your Majesty."



Zhao Jingheng returned to the report.



He had begun to understand that his wife's resignation was not a single decision.



It was a long series of small refusals.



Each refusal required someone else to perform the work they had previously assumed she would continue performing.



The emperor had not yet decided whether he found this admirable or inconvenient.



He suspected it was both.



At the Qingyuan Marquisate, Shen Mingzhao had a different problem.



The neighbour had arrived with a measuring rope.



He had also arrived with two assistants, a folded boundary record, and the confidence of a man who believed that a property line could be established through the strength of his own voice.



Mingzhao stood beside the northern boundary of the family's agricultural holding.



Steward Zhou De stood a few steps behind her.



Master Luo, the property assessor, had brought his own measuring instruments. A local survey assistant was present to record the measurements.



The neighbouring landowner, a man named Mr. Qian, had arrived with his nephew.



"Miss Shen," Mr. Qian greeted her. "I understand you wish to have the boundary examined."



"I do."



"Surely the boundary has been established for many years."



"That is why I asked for it to be measured."



Mr. Qian looked faintly offended.



"Are you suggesting my family has taken land belonging to the Marquisate?"



"I am suggesting that the old record and the current markers should agree."



"Landmarks move."



"Then we should establish where they were and where they are now."



Mr. Qian's nephew looked toward the measuring rope.



"Is all this necessary? The old stone marker is still there."



Master Luo answered before Mingzhao could.



"The old marker is present. Its position does not, by itself, establish the full boundary."



The nephew frowned.



"Why not?"



"Because the record identifies three points, not one."



Master Luo unfolded the old survey.



He placed it beside the newer land register.



The northern boundary ran along a shallow drainage channel before turning toward an old mulberry tree. The tree had been removed several years earlier, leaving a stump that was now difficult to identify.



The drainage channel had also been widened.



The neighbouring family maintained that the widening had followed the original boundary.



The Marquisate's old record suggested otherwise.



Mingzhao examined the documents.



"Which record is older?"



"The original land registration is older," Master Luo said. "The drainage alteration is recorded in a later maintenance note, but the note does not include a revised boundary measurement."



"Who requested the alteration?"



Steward Zhou consulted his papers.



"The former estate steward approved the maintenance work. The neighbouring family contributed labour."



"Was the land exchange documented?"



"No."



"Was compensation recorded?"



"No."



Mr. Qian cleared his throat.



"It was a small adjustment. Both families benefited from the drainage improvement."



Mingzhao looked at him.



"Then the adjustment should have been documented."



"Your father never objected."



"My father did not personally measure every field."



Mr. Qian's expression tightened.



Steward Zhou looked down.



Master Luo, who had spent many years assessing property for families that disliked hearing the word discrepancy, calmly took out his measuring rope.



"We should begin with the eastern marker."



The men started the measurement.



It was slow work.



The rope had to be aligned with the old registration points. The survey assistant recorded each distance. The drainage channel was examined at several locations. The remains of the mulberry tree were measured against the description in the old document.



After nearly an hour, Master Luo reviewed the figures.



"The present drainage channel does extend slightly beyond the line described in the older record."



Mr. Qian immediately raised his voice.



"That is because the channel was widened for proper drainage!"



"That may be the reason," Master Luo replied. "It does not establish ownership."



"Then what do you propose?"



"Further examination of the original survey points and the maintenance record."



Mingzhao looked at the measurements.



"How much land is affected?"



"Not enough to determine a final figure yet. The width varies along the channel."



"Then do not give me an estimated area as though it were confirmed."



Master Luo nodded.



"I will record the measured sections separately and mark the remaining section as disputed."



Mr. Qian appeared dissatisfied.



"You intend to leave the matter unresolved?"



"I intend to leave it accurately recorded."



Mingzhao turned toward him.



"If the old boundary was changed by agreement, we can examine the agreement. If it was changed by maintenance, we can examine the maintenance record. If neither exists, the matter remains disputed until the evidence is sufficient."



Mr. Qian folded his arms.



"And if the Marquisate refuses to accept the present arrangement?"



"Then the two families may resolve the matter through the proper local authority."



The nephew muttered something under his breath.



Mingzhao heard it.



She did not respond.



There was no advantage in turning a measurement into an argument about manners.



Master Luo wrote the preliminary result into his report.



The boundary was not settled.



But it was no longer merely a disagreement between two families.



It was a documented discrepancy requiring further evidence.



For the purposes of the property valuation, that was progress.



For the purposes of Mr. Qian's mood, it was a disaster.



While the survey continued, Shen Mingyuan sat in the Marquisate's account room with three ledgers open before him.



He had spent much of the previous evening comparing household expenditure with the issue records.



He was beginning to understand why Mingzhao had always insisted that a number without supporting documents was not a reliable number.



This was unfortunate.



It meant he was now spending his mornings looking for documents.



He had found the original authorization for the seventy-two taels spent on repairing his study. He had also found the carpenter's receipt and the record of materials supplied.



That matter had been settled.



The remaining two hundred and twenty-eight taels were not so cooperative.



He had found several entries connected to the household, but none could yet be confidently assigned to his personal expenditure.



He rubbed his forehead.



A servant entered with a small bundle of old receipts.



"Young Master, these were found in the western cabinet."



Mingyuan looked up.



"Who found them?"



"Steward Zhou's clerk. They were inside an envelope marked miscellaneous household repairs."



"Put them there."



The servant placed them beside the ledger.



Mingyuan opened the envelope.



Most of the receipts were small.



A repair to a window frame. A replacement lock. Two deliveries of charcoal. A payment to a carpenter.



He began comparing them with the account entries.



One receipt matched a payment already recorded.



Another appeared to relate to a different building.



The third had no corresponding entry.



He frowned.



"Who authorized this?"



The servant shook his head.



"I do not know, Young Master."



Mingyuan looked at the receipt again.



It was for twelve taels.



The description was vague: *additional repairs and fittings.*



He could have guessed.



He could have assigned it to his study.



He could have placed it under general estate maintenance.



Instead, he wrote a note.



*Unmatched receipt. Twelve taels. Purpose and authorization unconfirmed.*



The servant watched him.



"Should I ask Miss Mingzhao?"



Mingyuan looked up sharply.



"No."



The servant lowered his head.



"Yes, Young Master."



Mingyuan returned to the ledger.



He had asked Mingzhao questions for years.



Sometimes she had answered. Sometimes she had told him to read the records himself. He had usually considered the latter response unnecessarily irritating.



Now he understood that the answer had been sitting in front of him.



He simply had not wanted to do the work required to find it.



That did not make him particularly happy.



He continued reading.



In another part of the Marquisate, Shen Mingxiu was discovering that a secondary residence could not be managed through good intentions.



She sat with a property report before her.



The residence had been used by members of the family during visits to the southern district. It was not the principal estate, but its maintenance expenses were charged to the Marquisate.



Mingxiu had been asked to prepare a list of repairs and current expenses.



She had made the list.



She had also made two errors.



The first was counting a repair expense twice because it appeared in both the contractor's account and the household issue record.



The second was treating the cost of replacing a broken water jar as a permanent improvement.



Steward Zhou's assistant had pointed out the first error.



Mingxiu had found the second herself.



She stared at the report.



"Why does everything have two records?"



The assistant answered cautiously.



"One record shows the payment. The other shows the goods or work received."



"Then why are they not written together?"



"Sometimes they are."



"Sometimes?"



"Different offices keep different records."



Mingxiu put down her brush.



"That is an extremely inconvenient arrangement."



The assistant did not disagree.



She crossed out the duplicate expense and corrected the classification of the water jar.



Then she reviewed the rest of the report.



It was slower than she had expected.



She had once thought that Mingzhao's insistence on exact accounts was an expression of temperament.



Now she was beginning to suspect it was also a survival mechanism.



Mingxiu had no intention of becoming her elder sister.



But she was starting to understand why the family had depended on her.



That understanding did not automatically make her grateful.



It did, however, make the work harder to dismiss.



At the eastern market, Qiao Yue was inspecting a lodging house.



The building stood on a narrow street behind a row of shops. It had two upper rooms, a small receiving area, a shared courtyard, and a kitchen that was adequate rather than impressive.



The owner, an older woman named Madam Wu, showed Qiao Yue the rooms.



"The upper room receives the morning light," she said. "The lower room is quieter. There is a separate place for servants in the rear courtyard."



Qiao Yue examined the windows.



The upper room had a loose latch.



The lower room had a damp patch near the wall.



The courtyard drain was clear, but the kitchen stove needed cleaning.



"How much?"



Madam Wu named the monthly rent.



Qiao Yue considered it.



The amount was not unreasonable.



It was also not so low that she could accept it without consulting Mingzhao.



"Does the rent include the use of the kitchen?"



"Yes."



"Water?"



"From the shared well."



"Are there additional charges for the servants?"



"No, but they must keep the courtyard clean."



Qiao Yue nodded.



"How long is the agreement?"



"Three months initially. It can be renewed."



"Can the deposit be returned if the building has a structural problem?"



Madam Wu looked at her.



"That depends on the problem."



"Then it should be written down."



The older woman sighed.



"You people have many conditions."



Qiao Yue smiled politely.



"My mistress has many ledgers."



Madam Wu did not appear reassured.



Qiao Yue inspected the rooms once more.



It was not a grand residence.



It was not particularly beautiful.



It was close to the market, had enough space for Mingzhao and the four servants accompanying her, and could be rented without committing her to a permanent purchase.



That was sufficient.



When Qiao Yue returned to the Marquisate, Mingzhao was reviewing the preliminary boundary report.



"The lodging house is available," Qiao Yue said. "Three-month agreement. Two upper rooms, one lower room, and a small rear courtyard."



"Condition?"



"Acceptable. The upper room needs a new latch. The lower room has some dampness. The kitchen needs cleaning."



"Rent?"



Qiao Yue gave her the amount.



Mingzhao considered it.



"Deposit?"



"One month."



"Refund conditions?"



"The owner has not agreed to a full refund for every possible defect. I asked her to put structural defects and existing damage in writing."



"Good."



"Would you like to inspect it?"



"Yes. Before the agreement is signed."



Qiao Yue nodded.



"There is another matter."



"What?"



"The owner asked whether you were bringing a large household."



Mingzhao looked up.



"What did you tell her?"



"Four servants, personal belongings, and no ceremonial procession."



"Did she ask about the Marquis?"



"She asked whether the Marquis would visit."



Mingzhao returned to the report.



"Tell her that if he visits, he will use the front entrance like everyone else."



Qiao Yue smiled.



"I did not think it necessary to explain that."



"Good."



Mingzhao marked the lodging house as a possible temporary arrangement.



She would not purchase a property merely because her departure date was approaching.



The northern canal house still required repairs. The eastern property still had an unresolved title and boundary question. Neither was suitable for immediate occupation.



A rented room was not an ideal solution.



It was, however, a solution that did not require her to pretend an incomplete decision was a completed one.



In the afternoon, the two former drivers who had been summoned in connection with the northern warehouse transport records arrived at the Marquisate.



They were older men.



One had retired from regular service two years earlier. The other now worked occasionally as a cart contractor.



Neither seemed eager to revisit journeys made several years before.



Mingzhao sat with Sun Qiming and Steward Zhou while the statements were taken.



The first driver, a man named Wang, examined the transport notebook.



"This is my handwriting," he said.



"Can you identify the journey?" Sun Qiming asked.



Wang leaned closer.



"The southern road. Three carts."



"Do you remember what they carried?"



"Grain, I believe."



"How many sacks?"



Wang shook his head.



"I cannot say."



"Do you remember the destination?"



"Not exactly. There were several storage places along that road."



"Did you deliver to the southern warehouse?"



"I may have."



"May have?"



"It was a long time ago. I remember the road and the carts. I do not remember the receiving clerk."



Sun Qiming made a note.



"Did you make any other journey on the same day?"



Wang studied the next entry.



"There is another one here. The outer road."



"Was it a grain delivery?"



"I cannot confirm that."



"Do you remember what you carried?"



"No."



The second driver, Liu, was questioned separately.



His recollection was similar.



He remembered the southern road and the three carts. He remembered a stop where one wheel had required attention. He could not identify the receiving warehouse.



He also remembered a separate trip toward the outer road.



He could not confirm whether that trip carried grain.



Mingzhao listened without interrupting.



When the statements were complete, Sun Qiming read them aloud.



Both drivers confirmed that the three-cart journey had taken place.



Neither could establish the number of sacks.



Neither could confirm the final receiving record.



The separate outer-road journey remained unidentified.



Mingzhao looked at the notebook.



"The southern warehouse ledger confirms eighteen sacks received."



"Yes," Sun Qiming said.



"The movement instruction covers twenty."



"Yes."



"Then two remain unconfirmed."



"Correct."



"And the other twenty-seven?"



"Still unexplained."



Mingzhao closed the notebook.



"Record the statements as supporting evidence of transport activity, not proof of delivery."



Sun Qiming nodded.



The drivers signed their statements.



No one accused them of theft.



No one declared the matter solved.



The records had become slightly clearer, but the missing grain remained missing from the documented chain of custody.



Mingzhao would not allow a convenient conclusion to replace an accurate one.



At the palace, the Empress Dowager Wei received a visit from Xu Yuru.



The Empress Dowager was seated near the window, reviewing a list of ceremonial offerings.



She glanced at Yuru.



"The preparations are proceeding?"



"They are."



"Are the departments cooperating?"



"They are learning to."



The Empress Dowager gave a quiet snort.



"That sounds less reassuring."



"It is more accurate."



The older woman put down the list.



"Your ceremony is in five days."



"Yes."



"And after that?"



"The assigned offices will continue their responsibilities."



"And you?"



"I will no longer administer the inner household."



The Empress Dowager studied her.



"You speak as though the matter is already finished."



"The decision has been accepted. The ceremony will mark the formal departure."



"Yet you will remain in the palace until then."



"Yes."



"And the emperor has not appointed another empress."



"That is his decision."



The Empress Dowager looked toward the courtyard.



"People will say the inner palace has been left without its proper head."



"People may say that."



"You do not intend to answer them?"



Yuru considered this.



"If they ask who is responsible for the stores, the answer is the stores. If they ask who is responsible for the ceremonial schedule, the answer is the Department of Rites. If they ask who is responsible for household appointments, the answer is the office authorized to make them."



"And if they ask who is responsible for the palace?"



"The emperor."



The Empress Dowager looked at her sharply.



Yuru met her gaze without hostility.



For a long moment, the room was quiet.



At last, the older woman said, "You have made the emperor responsible for a great many things."



"He has been responsible for them all along."



The Empress Dowager did not answer immediately.



Then she looked down at the list of offerings.



"These quantities are incorrect."



Yuru leaned forward slightly.



"Which ones?"



"The number of ceremonial candles. The list counts the reserve stock as though it were already issued."



Yuru examined the document.



The Empress Dowager was right.



Yuru did not reach for her own writing brush.



She handed the list to the senior attendant.



"Please return this to the office that prepared it. They should correct the distinction between issued and reserve stock."



The Empress Dowager watched her.



"You are not going to correct it yourself?"



"No."



"Even though you have already identified the error?"



"Yes."



The older woman gave her a long, unreadable look.



Then she returned to the list of offerings.



"Very well."



It was not approval.



But it was no longer an argument.



That evening, Mingzhao inspected the temporary lodging house herself.



The street was busy, though the building stood far enough from the main market road to avoid the worst of the noise.



She examined the upper rooms, tested the window latches, and inspected the kitchen.



The damp patch in the lower room was old but not spreading. The roof above the rear corridor had been repaired recently, though the owner could not produce a written receipt.



Mingzhao asked for one.



Madam Wu sighed.



"You really do want everything written down."



"Only the things I may be asked to pay for."



The owner looked at her for a moment.



Then she laughed.



"At least you are honest."



Mingzhao did not smile.



"Can you agree to the three-month rent, with the existing damage listed before I take possession?"



"Yes."



"And no additional charge for the four servants?"



"Yes."



"The deposit must be returned if the house is unfit for occupation because of an existing structural defect."



Madam Wu hesitated.



"I will agree to that if the defect is confirmed by an independent carpenter."



"That is reasonable."



They settled the principal terms.



The agreement would be written the next morning, after the existing damage was recorded.



Mingzhao did not consider the matter an achievement.



She considered it a temporary arrangement that had cost her an afternoon.



Qiao Yue, however, appeared satisfied.



"It is close to the market," she said as they left.



"It is also small."



"We do not need a large residence."



"We need sufficient storage."



"There is a rear room."



"It has no lock."



"I noticed."



"Then we will have one installed."



Qiao Yue glanced at her.



"Anything else?"



"The kitchen shelves need repair. The upper window latch needs replacing. We should bring only the furniture that belongs to me."



"That is already on the list."



"Then check it again."



Qiao Yue sighed.



"Of course."



Mingzhao looked down the street.



Two days remained before her departure.



The property division was unfinished. The accounts were unfinished. The boundary dispute was unfinished. Her independent residence was not yet established.



But the date had been agreed.



She would leave on the twentieth.



The rest would continue through the proper procedures.



For once, she intended to let something remain unfinished without treating that fact as a personal failure.



Back at the Marquisate, Shen Guowei received the preliminary report from Master Luo.



He read the boundary findings.



Then he read the notes from the two former drivers.



Finally, he examined the lodging agreement's proposed terms.



"She has rented a house?"



"For three months," Steward Zhou said.



"Why not purchase one?"



"The eastern property has an unresolved boundary issue. The northern canal property requires repairs."



Shen Guowei put the papers down.



"So she is leaving without a permanent residence."



"She has chosen temporary accommodation."



The Marquis frowned.



"Will people not talk?"



Steward Zhou remained silent for a moment.



Then he said, "People will talk whether she rents or purchases."



Shen Guowei looked at him.



The steward lowered his head.



"I mean, my lord, that the property question should be settled according to the documents."



The Marquis did not respond immediately.



He was beginning to recognize the difference between a decision that preserved the family's reputation and a decision that merely delayed an uncomfortable conversation.



"Have the servants' transfer arrangements been completed?"



"Most of them. The four accompanying Miss Mingzhao have received their new wage and duty arrangements."



"And the rest?"



"They will remain under the Marquisate's household administration."



"Good."



He looked again at the boundary report.



"Send a copy to the family legal adviser."



"Yes, my lord."



"And do not let anyone change the old records."



Steward Zhou bowed.



"That instruction has already been given."



Shen Guowei looked toward the window.



Two days.



His eldest daughter would leave in two days.



He had once believed that the household could not function without her.



Now the household was beginning to function without her.



He could not decide whether that was a relief or an accusation.



In Chengming Palace, Xu Yuru reviewed the final report of the day.



The ceremonial schedule had been corrected.



The Department of Rites had accepted responsibility for the final arrangements. The Department of Personnel had confirmed the attendants' current assignments. The Imperial Household Administration had confirmed the reserve stock.



The additional twenty lengths of silk had been reduced to three replacement lengths, supported by the recorded damage.



The Crown Prince had begun reviewing his own household expenditure.



The Empress Dowager had identified an error in the ceremonial candle inventory.



The palace had not collapsed.



Yuru set down the report.



Fang Ru stood nearby.



"Your Majesty, the offices have submitted their confirmations."



"Good."



"There are still several matters requiring attention."



"How many?"



"Four."



Yuru waited.



Fang Ru continued.



"One concerns the final ceremonial seating arrangement. One concerns the attendants' meal allocation. One concerns a request for additional transport. The last concerns the storage of the temporary ceremonial screens after the event."



Yuru looked at her.



"Who is responsible for them?"



"The Department of Rites, the Imperial Household Administration, the responsible transport office, and the Palace Stores."



"Then send them to those offices."



"Yes, Your Majesty."



Fang Ru gathered the documents.



At the door, she stopped again.



"Your Majesty, may I ask something?"



"Go ahead."



"When the ceremony is finished, will you miss this work?"



Yuru looked at the papers on her desk.



She thought of the countless small decisions that had filled her days.



The records. The servants. The disputes. The schedules. The accounts. The complaints. The endless requests for approval.



She had been good at this work.



She had also been tired.



"Some of it," she said.



Fang Ru waited.



"Not all of it."



The senior attendant bowed.



Yuru turned toward the window.



Six days had not yet passed.



There was still a ceremony to prepare, a household to hand over, and a life beyond the palace to consider.



But for the first time in many years, she was no longer required to answer every question simply because someone knew she could.



Across the capital, Shen Mingzhao was preparing to sign a three-month rental agreement, while the family accounts remained unsettled and twenty-seven sacks of grain remained unexplained.



Neither woman had finished her work.



Both had begun to understand that unfinished work did not necessarily mean failure.



It could also mean that other people would finally have to do theirs.



And that, in both the imperial palace and the Marquisate, was proving to be an extraordinarily expensive lesson.
