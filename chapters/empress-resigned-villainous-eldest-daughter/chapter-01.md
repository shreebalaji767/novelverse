# The Empress Has Resigned! The Villainous Eldest Daughter Has Divided the Family Fortune and Left!

## **Chapter 1: The Empress Has Submitted Her Resignation. The Emperor Has Developed a Headache.**

**23rd Day of the Third Month, 18th Year of Yonghe.**

The first problem of the morning was a dispute over a cushion.

The second was a dispute over a prince's attendant.

The third concerned the storage of winter supplies.

By the time the fourth problem arrived, Empress Xu Yuru had begun to suspect that the imperial palace was not, in fact, an institution of government.

It was a particularly expensive household in which thousands of people had somehow forgotten how to behave without asking permission.

She sat behind a broad rosewood desk in Chengming Palace, a brush resting between her fingers. Morning sunlight passed through the lattice windows and fell across the neatly arranged documents before her. Beyond the windows, the palace gardens were beginning to turn green. A pair of servants crossed the courtyard carrying a tray of fresh tea, their footsteps quiet against the stone.

Inside the study, the atmosphere was considerably less peaceful.

"Your Majesty," said Madam Fang, the senior administrator of the inner palace, "the Second Prince's chief attendant has submitted another request."

Xu Yuru did not look up.

"Another?"

"Yes, Your Majesty."

"Is the first request still unanswered?"

Madam Fang hesitated. "It was answered yesterday."

"And the answer?"

"The regulations state that the Crown Prince's attendants enter the eastern ceremonial hall before those of the other princes during formal family gatherings."

"Then why has he submitted another request?"

"He believes the regulation is unfair."

Xu Yuru dipped her brush into the ink.

"Has the regulation changed?"

"No, Your Majesty."

"Has the Crown Prince ceased to be the Crown Prince?"

"No, Your Majesty."

"Has the Second Prince become the Crown Prince?"

Madam Fang lowered her eyes. "No, Your Majesty."

"Then the regulation remains unchanged."

Xu Yuru wrote a short instruction on the document and placed it in the completed pile.

Madam Fang accepted it with both hands.

"Should I explain the matter to the Second Prince's attendant again?"

"You may explain it as many times as necessary. But do not rewrite the regulations merely because someone dislikes standing behind another person."

"Yes, Your Majesty."

The attendant's request was removed from the desk.

A second document took its place.

This time, Madam Fang's expression became more cautious.

"The Department of Palace Stores has submitted its proposal for winter supply procurement."

Xu Yuru finally raised her eyes.

"It is the third month."

"Yes, Your Majesty."

"Spring has barely begun."

"Yes, Your Majesty."

"Why are we purchasing winter supplies?"

Madam Fang carefully unfolded the proposal.

"The department wishes to begin preparations early. They believe that if the procurement begins now, the palace will avoid delays when the cold season approaches."

Xu Yuru read the first page.

Then the second.

Then she turned back to the first.

"Madam Fang."

"Your Majesty?"

"How many winter blankets did the palace purchase last year?"

"According to the final inventory, eleven hundred and twenty."

"How many were distributed?"

"Nine hundred and sixty."

"How many remain in storage?"

"One hundred and sixty, Your Majesty."

Xu Yuru tapped the paper with the end of her brush.

"And how many does this proposal request?"

"One thousand and two hundred."

The room became quiet.

Xu Yuru looked at the proposal again, as though the number might change if she gave it sufficient attention.

It did not.

"Are the remaining blankets damaged?"

"Some require repairs, but most are usable."

"Then why are we purchasing another twelve hundred?"

"The department says the new order would allow them to replace the older stock."

"All of it?"

"Yes, Your Majesty."

"Even the blankets that have not been used?"

Madam Fang lowered her head.

"The proposal does not distinguish between them."

Xu Yuru put the paper down.

"Return it. They may submit a new proposal after checking the inventory. Blankets that can be repaired should be repaired. Blankets that cannot be repaired should be replaced. Blankets that remain perfectly usable should not be purchased again merely because someone wishes to complete a procurement schedule."

"Yes, Your Majesty."

"And remind the department that winter arrives in its own season. It does not require us to spend the entire spring preparing to be cold."

Madam Fang accepted the document.

She did not smile, but her shoulders relaxed slightly.

The Empress was not difficult to understand. She was difficult to persuade into approving something unreasonable.

That distinction had become increasingly important to the people who worked under her.

Xu Yuru reached for the next document.

It concerned a disagreement between two palace kitchens over the allocation of premium cooking oil.

She read the first paragraph.

Then she closed her eyes.

For a brief moment, she imagined a world in which two kitchens could divide cooking oil without requiring the intervention of the Empress of Great Yong.

It was a pleasant world.

Unfortunately, it did not appear to exist.

"Your Majesty," Madam Fang said gently, "the Emperor has requested the latest expenditure report for the inner palace. Chief Eunuch Gao sent word that His Majesty wishes to review it this morning."

Xu Yuru opened her eyes.

"Has the report been prepared?"

"Yes. It is on the lower shelf."

"Bring it."

Madam Fang retrieved a bound ledger and placed it on the desk.

Xu Yuru opened it.

The figures were familiar. Palace expenditure had risen slightly over the previous quarter, largely because of repairs to several storage buildings and additional grain allocations. There were also expenses associated with the upcoming seasonal ceremonies.

She turned several pages, checked the totals, and made two small annotations.

"Send the report to the Emperor's study. I will go there after the morning administrative session."

"Yes, Your Majesty."

Madam Fang gathered the completed documents.

Before leaving, she glanced at the Empress.

"Your Majesty, you have not yet taken breakfast."

Xu Yuru looked at the remaining stack.

"I will eat after these."

"Your Majesty said that yesterday."

"And I ate yesterday."

"At the hour of You."

Xu Yuru paused.

That was later than she had intended.

Madam Fang's expression remained respectful, but there was an unmistakable firmness in her eyes.

Xu Yuru put down her brush.

"Bring breakfast."

Madam Fang bowed.

"Your Majesty is wise."

"Do not praise me for eating."

"Then I shall praise the kitchen for preparing it."

"That is also unnecessary."

Madam Fang withdrew before the Empress could find another reason to postpone her meal.

Xu Yuru looked at the documents.

The palace had more than enough people to manage its affairs. It had administrators, clerks, supervisors, accountants, stewards, eunuchs, palace maids, and departments whose entire purpose was to maintain a functioning system.

Yet every disagreement, every missing receipt, every ceremonial inconvenience and every badly written proposal somehow found its way to her desk.

She had once believed that this was simply what it meant to be Empress.

A position carried responsibilities. A person who held authority could not complain whenever someone needed a decision.

But responsibility and personal intervention were not the same thing.

Somewhere along the years, the palace had forgotten the difference.

Xu Yuru returned her attention to the ledger.

She had not yet decided what to do about it.

But the thought had begun to take shape.

The Hall of Mental Cultivation was quiet when Xu Yuru arrived.

Emperor Zhao Jingheng was seated behind his desk, reviewing a memorial from the Ministry of Revenue. His outer robe was dark blue, embroidered with restrained patterns of clouds. A cup of tea sat near his right hand.

At forty-three, he had the composed bearing of a man who had spent eighteen years ruling an empire. His face rarely revealed surprise before he had decided whether surprise was useful.

He looked up when the Empress entered.

"Yuru."

"Your Majesty."

She greeted him according to the appropriate etiquette. He motioned for her to sit.

"I received the expenditure report," he said. "The inner palace's spending has increased by three percent."

"Yes. The largest increases are grain allocation and storage repairs. The figures are documented in the attached schedules."

"I saw them. The Ministry of Revenue asked whether the inner palace could reduce its expenses before the next quarter."

"It can."

Zhao Jingheng looked up.

"That quickly?"

"The seasonal ceremonial budget can be reviewed. Some decorative purchases are unnecessary, and the palace stores have sufficient supplies in several categories. There is no need to replace usable goods simply because a new procurement period has begun."

The Emperor glanced at the report.

"Have you already reviewed the proposals?"

"Most of them."

"Most?"

Xu Yuru considered the question.

"There are still several documents on my desk."

He gave a quiet laugh.

"Then I should be careful about asking you for more."

It was meant lightly.

Xu Yuru did not laugh.

Zhao Jingheng noticed.

He placed the memorial aside.

"Is something troubling you?"

For a moment, she looked at the report between them.

She had rehearsed the words in her private study the previous evening. She had considered whether to begin with the demands of the inner palace, the repeated failures of its departments, or the growing number of matters that could not proceed without her approval.

None of those explanations seemed sufficient.

They described the symptoms.

Not the decision.

Xu Yuru reached into her sleeve and took out a folded document.

"Your Majesty, I have something to submit."

The Emperor accepted it.

He unfolded the paper.

His eyes moved across the first lines.

Then they stopped.

He read the title again.

**Petition to Relinquish the Position of Empress and Withdraw from the Administration of the Inner Palace.**

For several breaths, Zhao Jingheng said nothing.

He looked at the document, then at his wife.

"What is this?"

"My petition."

"I can see that."

"Then Your Majesty understands its purpose."

He set the paper down carefully.

"Yuru, are you unwell?"

"No."

"Has someone offended you?"

"No."

"Has there been a disagreement with the Empress Dowager?"

"No."

"Then why are you submitting this?"

Xu Yuru folded her hands in her lap.

"Because I wish to resign."

The Emperor stared at her.

For eighteen years, he had known Xu Yuru as a woman who rarely made decisions without first considering their consequences. She was not impulsive. She did not threaten to leave when dissatisfied, nor did she use her position to demand attention.

If she had submitted a formal petition, she had already thought about it.

That made the matter considerably more serious.

"Resign from the administration of the inner palace," he repeated. "Or resign from the position of Empress?"

"Both."

"Both?"

"Yes."

Zhao Jingheng leaned back.

"Have you considered what that means?"

"I have."

"The position of Empress is not an ordinary office. It is tied to the imperial ancestral institution, the imperial family, and the stability of the inner palace."

"I understand."

"And the inner palace cannot be left without administration."

"Nor should its administration depend on one person."

The Emperor's expression changed slightly.

Xu Yuru continued in a calm voice.

"Your Majesty, I have managed the inner palace for many years. I have supervised expenditure, household regulations, ceremonies, the arrangements of the princes and princesses, the appointments of servants, disputes between the palace departments, and the daily operation of the imperial residences."

"I know."

"Yet many of these responsibilities have gradually become matters that cannot proceed without my personal approval."

"That is because you are capable."

"Capability is not a system."

Zhao Jingheng did not answer.

Xu Yuru looked at him directly.

"If a department cannot resolve a disagreement without sending it to me, then the department has not been properly organized. If an administrator cannot approve a routine expense without waiting for my instruction, then the authority assigned to that administrator is meaningless. If a palace servant can ignore established rules because he believes I will personally correct the matter, then the rules are not functioning."

The Emperor's fingers rested against the edge of the desk.

"You are saying the palace has become dependent on you."

"I am saying that I have allowed it to become dependent on me."

He studied her face.

There was no anger in it. No wounded pride. No accusation.

Only exhaustion, carefully contained.

"Are you tired?" he asked.

"Yes."

The answer was so direct that Zhao Jingheng fell silent.

Xu Yuru lowered her eyes briefly.

"I am not asking to abandon my responsibilities without preparation. I am asking that the imperial household establish a structure capable of carrying them without requiring me to intervene in every matter."

"And if the structure fails?"

"Then the failure should be addressed by the officials responsible for it."

"What if they cannot manage it?"

"Then they must be trained, replaced, or given clearer authority."

The Emperor rubbed his brow.

"You make it sound simple."

"It is not simple. But it is necessary."

He picked up the petition again.

The wording was formal and deliberate. It requested a review of the Empress's position, the transfer of administrative authority, an inventory of outstanding duties, and the establishment of a transition arrangement.

She had not written a single line suggesting that the palace should simply manage without her from the following morning.

She had prepared for the possibility that the request would be refused.

That, more than the petition itself, unsettled him.

"How long have you been considering this?"

"Some time."

"How long?"

"Long enough to know that I will not withdraw the request merely because it is inconvenient."

Zhao Jingheng let out a slow breath.

"Yuru, the court will object."

"I expect it will."

"The Empress Dowager will object."

"I expect that too."

"The princes may be unsettled."

"Then they should be informed properly."

"And the ministers will say that the imperial household cannot change its structure simply because the Empress wishes to retire."

Xu Yuru's expression remained composed.

"Then the ministers may explain why they believe the current structure should remain dependent on one person."

For the first time that morning, the Emperor almost smiled.

Almost.

He looked down at the petition again.

"I will consider it."

Xu Yuru bowed her head.

"Thank you, Your Majesty."

"Do not submit another petition for the time being."

"I have submitted only one."

"I know. I would prefer that it remain the only one."

"That depends on the response."

Zhao Jingheng looked at her.

She looked back.

The Emperor was accustomed to dealing with ministers who disguised their demands in elaborate language. He was accustomed to officials who threatened resignation but expected promotion, and to noble families who described a desire for influence as concern for the empire.

Xu Yuru was doing none of those things.

She genuinely wanted to leave.

He found that considerably more difficult to negotiate.

After the Empress departed, Zhao Jingheng remained seated for some time.

The petition lay open on his desk.

He read it once more, paying closer attention to the attached list of administrative responsibilities.

There were entries concerning palace stores, household expenditure, ceremonial preparations, appointments, internal regulations, education arrangements for the younger imperial children, maintenance of residences, and the resolution of disputes between departments.

The list continued for several pages.

Zhao Jingheng turned to the final page.

He frowned.

"How many responsibilities are listed here?"

Chief Eunuch Gao Shun, who stood nearby, stepped forward.

"Your Majesty, the appendix contains eighty-seven separate administrative matters."

The Emperor looked up.

"Eighty-seven?"

"Yes, Your Majesty."

"How many are routine matters?"

Gao Shun hesitated.

"Many, Your Majesty."

"How many should require the Empress's personal attention?"

Gao Shun did not answer immediately.

That was an answer in itself.

Zhao Jingheng closed the petition.

"Summon the Grand Secretary. Also summon the Ministers of Rites and Personnel."

"At once, Your Majesty."

Gao Shun bowed and withdrew.

The Emperor looked again at the list.

He had believed the inner palace was running smoothly.

Now he was beginning to wonder whether it was running smoothly because it had a system—or because one woman had spent eighteen years preventing its weaknesses from becoming visible.

The Grand Secretary arrived first.

Minister Wei Zhong was a careful man in his late fifties, known for his precise memorials and his reluctance to make promises without reviewing the relevant documents. He bowed before the Emperor and waited.

Minister Lu of Rites arrived shortly afterward, followed by Minister Han of Personnel.

Zhao Jingheng did not waste time on pleasantries.

"The Empress has submitted a petition to relinquish her position and withdraw from the administration of the inner palace."

The three officials looked at him.

Minister Lu's expression changed first.

"Your Majesty, has Her Majesty the Empress been unwell?"

"No."

"Has there been a disturbance in the inner palace?"

"No."

"Then perhaps—"

"The petition is deliberate."

Minister Lu stopped.

Minister Han looked down at the document placed before them.

The Grand Secretary remained silent for a moment before asking, "Does Her Majesty seek temporary relief from administrative duties, or does she intend to relinquish the position itself?"

"Both."

The ministers exchanged glances.

Minister Lu spoke cautiously.

"Your Majesty, the position of Empress is of great importance to the ancestral institution. It cannot be treated as an ordinary appointment."

"I am aware."

"The matter would require careful consideration."

"That is why I have summoned you."

The Grand Secretary opened the petition.

His eyes moved through the document. When he reached the appendix, his brows drew together.

"Eighty-seven matters?"

"That is what the Empress has listed."

"Are all of these currently handled through her office?"

"According to the petition."

Minister Han looked at the list.

"Some of these matters fall under the Department of Palace Stores. Others concern the rites and ceremonies. There are also appointments that should be handled by the inner palace administrators."

"Then why are they listed here?" the Emperor asked.

No one answered.

The Grand Secretary slowly closed the document.

"Your Majesty, before any decision is made regarding the Empress's position, the responsibilities of her office should be reviewed. We must establish which duties are properly attached to the position, which are delegated to subordinate officials, and which have accumulated through custom."

Zhao Jingheng nodded.

"Prepare a complete review."

Minister Lu hesitated.

"Should Her Majesty be asked to assist?"

"No."

The answer came quickly.

The ministers looked up.

Zhao Jingheng tapped the petition.

"She has already told me that she is tired of carrying these matters. I will not ask her to prepare the investigation into the work she is asking to relinquish."

The Grand Secretary bowed.

"Your Majesty is considerate."

"I am practical. If we send the work back to her, we will have learned nothing."

Minister Han lowered his head to conceal his expression.

The Emperor continued.

"Review every responsibility. Identify the department that should manage it, the official who currently handles it, and the authority required to complete it. If the authority is unclear, report it. If the regulations are inadequate, identify them. Do not simply write that the Empress should continue doing it."

"Yes, Your Majesty."

"And do not announce the petition publicly until the review is complete."

The ministers bowed.

As they withdrew, Zhao Jingheng looked once more at the appendix.

Eighty-seven responsibilities.

He had not known there were so many.

He had also not known that the Empress had been handling them with such little complaint.

The Emperor picked up his tea.

It had gone cold.

At the same hour, several streets away in the Eastern Noble District, Shen Mingzhao was discovering that a household ledger could be more dangerous than a family argument.

She sat in the eastern accounting room of the Qingyuan Marquisate, where the morning light entered through a row of narrow windows.

A long table stood before her. On it were account books, warehouse records, receipts, loan documents, and several lists prepared by the estate stewards.

Shen Mingzhao was twenty-six years old.

For the previous six years, she had managed much of the Marquisate's household expenditure and estate administration. Her father had entrusted her with the work because she was capable. Her mother had tolerated it because it was useful. Her younger brother had benefited from it because someone had made certain that the household accounts did not collapse under his spending.

None of these arrangements had been accompanied by a formal title.

When the family praised her, they called her diligent.

When they needed something, they called her responsible.

When she asked why the younger members of the household were permitted to spend without submitting proper accounts, they called her difficult.

Mingzhao had grown tired of the distinction.

She turned a page.

"Steward Zhou."

The elderly steward standing opposite her stepped forward.

"Young Miss."

"Read this entry."

He leaned over the ledger.

"Three hundred taels charged to the Second Young Master's residence. Listed as grain transportation and warehouse expenses."

"Where is the receipt?"

Steward Zhou checked the papers beside him.

"There is no receipt attached."

"Who approved the expenditure?"

"The entry was recorded under the authority of the household account."

"That is not what I asked."

The steward hesitated.

"The Second Young Master's residence requested the funds. The accounting office entered the expenditure afterward."

"Who approved it?"

"I cannot confirm that."

Mingzhao placed a small marker beside the entry.

"Then mark it unverified."

"Young Miss, if we mark it unverified, the Second Young Master may object."

"He may object after producing the receipt."

Steward Zhou bowed.

"Yes, Young Miss."

"Do not write that the money was stolen. Do not write that it was improperly spent. Write only that the supporting documents have not been provided."

"Understood."

Mingzhao turned to the next page.

"Now this one."

The steward moved closer.

"Two hundred and forty taels for repairs to the northern grain warehouse."

"Has the warehouse been inspected?"

"Not yet."

"Then how do we know the repairs were completed?"

"The foreman reported that they were."

"Has he provided a statement?"

"No."

"Has the steward of the northern warehouse confirmed it?"

"Not yet."

Mingzhao placed another marker beside the entry.

"Unverified."

Steward Zhou glanced at the growing collection of markers.

"Young Miss, if we mark every incomplete entry, a large portion of the account book will require review."

"Then a large portion of the account book requires review."

He fell silent.

Mingzhao leaned back slightly.

She was not accusing the family of theft. She was not trying to punish her brother through the accounts. She was doing something far less dramatic.

She was refusing to certify figures she could not verify.

It was astonishing how quickly this ordinary principle became offensive when applied to relatives.

"Have the inventory of the southern warehouse been completed?" she asked.

"Yes. The final count is expected tomorrow."

"Expected?"

"The warehouse steward is still checking the figures."

"Then it is not complete."

"Yes, Young Miss."

"Do not include it in the final inventory until it is signed."

Steward Zhou bowed again.

Mingzhao closed the ledger.

"Bring me the documents concerning my personal property."

The steward's expression changed.

"Your personal property?"

"Yes."

"Young Miss, do you wish to review your dowry records?"

"My dowry records, my mother's documented gifts, the assets inherited from my maternal relatives, and the commercial investments registered in my name. Also the money I personally lent to the household."

Steward Zhou looked at her carefully.

"All of them?"

"All of them."

"May I ask why?"

Mingzhao regarded him.

"Because I intend to divide the family property."

The old steward did not move.

For a moment, the accounting room seemed unusually quiet.

Then he lowered his head.

"I understand."

Mingzhao's expression remained unchanged.

"Do you?"

"I believe so, Young Miss."

"Then bring the records. I want the original documents, not a summary prepared for the family meeting."

"Yes, Young Miss."

He hesitated before leaving.

"Will you inform the Marquis?"

"I will inform the entire family."

Steward Zhou bowed and withdrew.

Mingzhao watched the door close.

She had not made the decision that morning.

Nor had she made it yesterday.

The thought had been growing for years, each unpaid loan and unrecorded responsibility adding another line to an account she had never intended to keep.

She had managed the household because someone had to.

She had paid expenses because the family could not afford delays.

She had corrected the accounts because inaccurate figures created larger problems later.

And whenever the family prospered, the credit belonged to the family.

Whenever something went wrong, the blame belonged to her.

The arrangement had been convenient for everyone except the person doing the work.

Mingzhao rose from her chair.

The family would soon discover that convenience was not the same as obligation.

The family meeting took place in the main reception hall before noon.

Old Madam Shen sat in the highest seat, her hands resting on the arms of a carved chair. Madam Liang sat to her right. Marquis Shen Guowei occupied the opposite seat, with Shen Mingyuan and Shen Mingxiu seated nearby.

Mingzhao entered last.

She wore a pale blue robe with a simple embroidered border. Her hair was arranged neatly, and there was no visible sign of anger or nervousness.

She greeted the elders and took her seat.

Old Madam Shen looked at her.

"Your father says you have something important to discuss."

"Yes, Grandmother."

"Then speak."

Mingzhao placed a document case on the table.

"I wish to formally divide the family property and establish an independent household."

Silence followed.

Her father frowned.

"What did you say?"

"I wish to divide the family property."

Madam Liang's hand tightened around her teacup.

"Mingzhao, what sort of thing is this to say before the elders?"

"A matter concerning the family's property should be discussed before the elders."

"Do not twist my words."

"I have not."

Shen Mingyuan leaned forward.

"Eldest Sister, what has happened? Did someone offend you?"

Mingzhao turned to him.

"No."

"Then why are you suddenly making such a demand?"

"It is not sudden."

His expression stiffened.

Mingzhao opened the document case.

"I have managed the household accounts for six years. I have reviewed estate expenditure, supervised the warehouse inventories, negotiated with suppliers, arranged payments, and handled several outstanding loans. I now intend to settle my own property and separate my responsibilities from those of the Marquisate."

Shen Guowei's brows drew together.

"You are the eldest daughter of this family. Why are you speaking as though you are an outside creditor?"

"Because the family has treated my work as a responsibility without ever defining its limits."

Madam Liang put down her teacup.

"Your father entrusted you with the household affairs because he trusted you. Is that now something to resent?"

"I do not resent being trusted. I object to being held responsible for matters over which I have no permanent authority."

"Your brother is the heir," Madam Liang replied. "He has his own duties."

"Then he should learn to manage them."

Shen Mingyuan's face darkened.

"I have never asked you to do everything."

Mingzhao looked at him.

"You asked for three hundred taels from the household account last month."

"That was for necessary expenses."

"Then the receipt should be easy to provide."

His mouth opened, then closed.

Old Madam Shen tapped her cane lightly against the floor.

"Enough. This is not a merchant's dispute. You are speaking about your own family."

"That is why I am asking for a family settlement rather than taking my property and leaving without explanation."

Shen Guowei's voice became stern.

"What exactly do you want?"

Mingzhao had prepared the answer.

"I want my personal dowry and inherited property identified and returned to my control. I want my independently owned commercial assets recognized. I want the documented loans I made to the household acknowledged and settled according to their terms. I want any property legally divisible under the family arrangements to be valued and divided according to the applicable rules. I also want the family's liabilities recorded so that no one can later claim I took assets while leaving debts behind."

Her father stared at her.

"You intend to take a share of the family estate?"

"I intend to establish what I am legally entitled to receive. I will not claim property that belongs to the Marquisate, and I will not surrender property that belongs to me."

Madam Liang's expression hardened.

"Do you understand what people will say? The eldest daughter of the Qingyuan Marquisate has demanded a division of property while her parents are still alive."

"People will say what they wish."

"They will say you are unfilial."

"Then they may also ask why the family's accounts require my work but my property rights are treated as a source of embarrassment."

"Mingzhao!" her father snapped.

She fell silent.

For several moments, the only sound in the hall was the faint movement of a servant outside the door.

Shen Guowei looked at his eldest daughter.

He had always known she was stubborn. He had also known she was competent. In the past, he had considered those qualities useful, even admirable.

Now he was beginning to understand that she had been keeping a record of every occasion on which her competence had been used against her.

"You cannot simply walk away," he said.

"I have not walked away. I am asking for a formal settlement."

"Who will manage the household accounts?"

Mingzhao met his eyes.

"I believe that is precisely what the family should decide."

Her father did not answer.

Shen Mingyuan looked uncomfortable. He had grown accustomed to finding his sister in the accounting room whenever a supplier complained or a payment was delayed. He had never considered what would happen if she were no longer there.

Madam Liang, however, was more concerned with the consequences.

"Your younger sister's marriage arrangements are approaching. Your brother's position as heir requires careful preparation. This is not the time to create division."

Mingzhao turned slightly toward Shen Mingxiu.

"Then the accounts should be settled before those arrangements become more complicated."

Shen Mingxiu had remained quiet until then. She now raised her eyes.

"Eldest Sister, are you leaving because you dislike living with us?"

Mingzhao studied her younger sister's expression.

There was concern in it, but also calculation. Mingxiu was not foolish. She knew that the departure of the person managing the household would change the balance of responsibilities.

"I am leaving because I wish to manage my own life."

"Must it happen now?"

"It should have happened earlier."

Old Madam Shen frowned.

"You speak as though this family has mistreated you."

Mingzhao did not answer immediately.

There were many things she could have said.

She could have described the years she had spent correcting accounts after midnight. She could have listed the occasions when her younger brother's expenses had been excused while hers were questioned. She could have reminded her mother that she had personally arranged several family obligations without receiving even a formal acknowledgment.

But the purpose of the meeting was not to persuade them to feel guilty.

She wanted a settlement.

Guilt was an unreliable form of payment.

"I am not asking the family to agree with my feelings," she said at last. "I am asking the family to agree to an accounting."

Her father looked at the document case.

"And if we refuse?"

"Then I will seek advice on the lawful procedures available to me."

The words were quiet.

They were also unmistakable.

For the first time, Shen Guowei looked less like a father reprimanding his daughter and more like a marquis facing a formal dispute.

"Leave the documents here," he said.

"I will leave copies. The original records will remain with the estate until they are independently verified."

"You do not trust us?"

"I trust accurate records."

Madam Liang's face turned cold.

Mingzhao stood and bowed to the elders.

"I will provide a complete inventory of my personal property and documented claims. I ask that the family appoint someone to verify the estate accounts as well."

She paused.

"Until the settlement is completed, I will continue to perform the duties necessary to prevent disruption. But I will not approve new expenses or certify incomplete accounts without supporting documents."

Shen Mingyuan looked up sharply.

"What does that mean?"

"It means that if you request money, you must provide the relevant documents."

"Even for ordinary expenses?"

"Especially for ordinary expenses. Those are the easiest to document."

She bowed once more.

Then she left the hall.

No one called her back.

Mingzhao returned to her eastern courtyard with Qiao Yue following behind.

Her attendant had been waiting outside the reception hall. She had heard enough from the servants to understand that the meeting had not gone well.

"Young Miss."

"Prepare tea."

"Yes."

Qiao Yue followed her into the room.

"Did they agree?"

"No."

"Did they refuse?"

"Not formally."

"Then what happened?"

"They objected."

Qiao Yue looked disappointed.

"That sounds worse than refusing."

"It is less useful than refusing."

Mingzhao sat at her desk and removed the hairpin that had begun to press against her scalp.

Qiao Yue poured tea.

"Young Miss, may I ask something?"

"Ask."

"Are you truly leaving?"

Mingzhao looked at the cup.

"Yes."

"Even if the Marquis becomes angry?"

"His anger does not alter the account."

"Even if Madam becomes upset?"

"Her feelings do not alter the account either."

Qiao Yue hesitated.

"Then may I go with you?"

Mingzhao looked up.

The question had come more quickly than she expected.

"Do you understand what you are asking?"

"I do."

"You have worked in this residence for years. Leaving will mean giving up the arrangements you know. The new household will be smaller. There may be fewer servants, fewer comforts, and more work."

"I understand."

"You may have to manage purchases, supervise repairs, and deal with merchants who believe a woman living independently is an easy person to deceive."

"I have already dealt with merchants who believe that."

Mingzhao regarded her for a moment.

Qiao Yue met her eyes without flinching.

"Then we will discuss the arrangements after the settlement," Mingzhao said. "I will not ask you to leave your position without knowing what you are agreeing to."

Qiao Yue bowed.

"Thank you, Young Miss."

Mingzhao reached for the next document.

"Do not thank me yet. I have not even found a suitable residence."

"Then I hope the residence will have a good kitchen."

Mingzhao glanced at her.

"That is your first concern?"

"It is an important concern."

"Then we shall place it second on the list."

"What is first?"

"Whether the roof leaks."

Qiao Yue nodded solemnly.

"That is also important."

For the first time that day, Mingzhao's expression softened.

It did not last long.

A knock came at the door.

Steward Zhou entered carrying a wooden case and several bundles of documents.

"Young Miss, the records you requested."

"Place them on the table."

He did so carefully.

"The personal property records are mostly complete. There are some missing documents concerning the loans made to the household. I have listed the dates and amounts that can be verified."

"Good. What about the commercial investments?"

"The partnership records are available. However, the valuation of the shares will require an independent assessment."

"Arrange one."

"Yes, Young Miss."

"And find out which residences are available in the eastern district. I want the asking prices, ownership records, condition of the buildings, and any outstanding debts attached to the properties."

Steward Zhou nodded.

"Do you have a preferred size?"

"Large enough for a modest household. Not a mansion."

Qiao Yue looked faintly disappointed.

Mingzhao noticed.

"If you wish to clean a mansion, you may volunteer to clean the Marquisate."

Qiao Yue immediately looked serious.

"A modest residence will be sufficient."

Steward Zhou lowered his head, concealing a small smile.

Mingzhao opened the first bundle of records.

There was a great deal to do.

The division would not be completed in a day, nor would the family accept every claim without argument. Some assets would need valuation. Some debts would need confirmation. The legal position of each property would have to be established.

She would need independent witnesses, copies of the original records, and a clear account of what she intended to take.

She would also need to make certain that she did not leave herself responsible for obligations she had no intention of carrying.

For the first time in years, she was preparing accounts for a household that belonged to her alone.

The thought was both satisfying and unsettling.

She turned the page.

"Steward Zhou."

"Yes, Young Miss?"

"Do not rush the inventory. Accuracy is more important than speed."

"Understood."

"And do not make the figures look better than they are."

"Of course."

"If an asset is worth less than we expected, record the lower value. If a debt cannot be proven, mark it as uncertain. I want a settlement that can withstand scrutiny."

Steward Zhou bowed.

"I will see to it."

Mingzhao looked down at the documents.

The family might call her unfilial.

They might call her selfish, difficult, or greedy.

But she would not give them the satisfaction of finding an error in her accounts.

By evening, the imperial palace had begun to respond to the Empress's petition.

The Grand Secretary had returned to his office and summoned several senior clerks. Minister Lu of Rites had requested the relevant regulations governing the Empress's position. Minister Han of Personnel had begun preparing a list of offices that might assume some of the duties currently handled through Chengming Palace.

The Emperor had instructed them to review the matter discreetly.

Discreetly, however, did not mean quietly.

By the time the first administrative memorandum was drafted, three departments had already questioned whether certain responsibilities belonged to them.

The Department of Palace Stores believed procurement approval should remain under its authority.

The Ministry of Rites believed ceremonial arrangements required a clear chain of command.

The Ministry of Personnel questioned whether the Empress's household appointments could be transferred without affecting the existing hierarchy of palace service.

The Grand Secretary read the first memorandum and placed it aside.

"How many matters have been reviewed?"

The clerk bowed.

"Six, Your Excellency."

"And how many have been assigned to a responsible office?"

"Two."

"What happened to the other four?"

"Each department has submitted a statement explaining why it should not be responsible for them."

The Grand Secretary stared at the clerk.

"Have they explained who should be responsible?"

The clerk hesitated.

"No, Your Excellency."

The Grand Secretary closed his eyes.

He had served the dynasty for more than thirty years. He had survived wars, floods, tax disputes, and two changes of senior ministers.

He had not expected the Empress's resignation to produce a dispute over who should receive a document concerning palace laundry.

"Send the matter back," he said.

"To which department?"

"To the department that currently handles the laundry."

"They say the Empress's office has always approved the annual allocation."

"Then ask them to explain why an annual allocation requires the Empress's personal approval."

The clerk bowed.

"Yes, Your Excellency."

The Grand Secretary picked up his brush.

The review had only begun.

That night, in Chengming Palace, Xu Yuru sat alone in her study.

The last of the day's reports had been placed in neat piles. Madam Fang had already reminded her twice that the hour was late.

The Empress had finally dismissed the remaining attendants.

Outside, the palace lanterns cast warm pools of light across the courtyard. The spring wind moved softly through the branches of the trees.

Xu Yuru opened a small wooden box.

Inside were several old documents: household regulations, lists of appointments, records of past reforms, and notes she had made over the years.

She took out a blank sheet of paper.

For a long moment, she looked at it.

Then she began to write.

Not a list of complaints.

Not a record of every failure.

A transition plan.

She wrote down the responsibilities that could be delegated immediately, those that required revised regulations, and those that should remain under the Emperor's direct authority. She noted which matters required training, which required new appointments, and which could be handled by existing administrators if their authority were clearly defined.

She wrote until her hand began to ache.

Then she stopped.

The paper before her contained only the beginning.

Eighty-seven responsibilities had been listed in the petition. Some were large enough to require several departments. Others were so small that she wondered how they had ever reached her desk.

She looked at the list again.

The palace would have to learn to function without her constant intervention.

And she would have to learn not to intervene simply because she could.

It would not be easy.

She knew that much.

A soft knock came at the door.

"Your Majesty?" Madam Fang's voice sounded from outside. "It is late."

Xu Yuru put down her brush.

"Come in."

Madam Fang entered and noticed the papers.

"Your Majesty is preparing something?"

"A transition plan."

Madam Fang looked at the document.

"Has His Majesty accepted the petition?"

"Not yet."

"Then why prepare this now?"

"Because whether he accepts it or not, the current arrangement cannot continue."

Madam Fang did not immediately reply.

She had served the Empress for many years. She knew how much of the palace's order depended on Xu Yuru's attention. She also knew that the Empress had rarely asked anyone to notice the effort involved.

"Your Majesty," she said carefully, "if the responsibilities are transferred, the palace may become disorderly for a time."

"Then it will become disorderly."

Madam Fang looked surprised.

Xu Yuru closed the wooden box.

"An institution that can function only when one person is exhausted is already disorderly. It has merely learned to hide the problem."

Madam Fang lowered her eyes.

"Would Your Majesty like me to prepare tea?"

"No. I would like you to prepare breakfast for tomorrow."

Madam Fang blinked.

"Breakfast?"

"Yes. At the proper hour."

"Your Majesty intends to eat on time?"

"I intend to attempt it."

Madam Fang bowed.

"Then I shall inform the kitchen."

She turned to leave.

"Madam Fang."

"Your Majesty?"

"Do not tell the palace administrators about the transition plan yet. His Majesty has not made a decision."

"Understood."

"And do not let anyone send me another dispute about ceremonial cushions."

Madam Fang paused.

"Your Majesty, the Second Prince's attendant has submitted a third request."

Xu Yuru looked at her.

Madam Fang looked back.

For several seconds, neither woman spoke.

Then Xu Yuru quietly covered her face with one hand.

"Let him keep the cushion."

At the Qingyuan Marquisate, a different document was delivered before the evening meal.

It was a brief notice from Shen Guowei's steward.

The family would hold another meeting the following morning to discuss Mingzhao's request.

The wording was polite.

The implication was not.

Mingzhao read it once and placed it beside her account books.

Qiao Yue stood nearby.

"Will you attend?"

"Of course."

"Do you think they will agree tomorrow?"

"No."

"Then why are they holding another meeting?"

"Because they hope I will change my mind."

Qiao Yue looked at the growing stack of documents.

"Will you?"

Mingzhao dipped her brush into the ink.

"No."

She wrote the date on the top page.

**24th Day of the Third Month, 18th Year of Yonghe.**

The first day of her new accounts had begun.

The settlement would take time. The family would argue. Some claims would be disputed, and some would have to be abandoned. She would need to find a residence, establish a household, and determine how much of her income could be relied upon without the Marquisate's support.

She had not yet secured her independence.

But she had stopped pretending that the existing arrangement was acceptable.

Mingzhao set down her brush.

Across the capital, the Empress of Great Yong was making a similar calculation.

One woman wished to leave the imperial household without allowing it to collapse.

The other wished to leave her family household without allowing it to consume everything she owned.

Neither had been granted permission.

Neither intended to wait forever.

And somewhere in the imperial palace, the Emperor was beginning to understand that his wife had not submitted a petition for temporary rest.

She had submitted a resignation.

For the first time in eighteen years, Zhao Jingheng had a problem that could not be solved by asking the Empress to handle it.

Unfortunately, he had not yet discovered who else in the empire knew how to manage a household.

**End of Chapter 1**
