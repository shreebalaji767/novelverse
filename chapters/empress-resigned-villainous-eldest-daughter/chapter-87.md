# **Chapter 87: The Former Empress Has Decided to Return a Book and Borrow Two More, Although Her Father Has Begun to Suspect That His Library Is Becoming a Branch of Her Residence. The Marquis's Eldest Daughter Has Closed a Profitable Contract and Has Discovered That Success Does Not Require Accepting the Next Offer Immediately. The Marquisate's New Procedure Has Been Asked to Explain Why a Missing Voucher Cannot Be Replaced by a Convincing Memory.**

**21st Day of the Sixth Month, 18th Year of Yonghe.**

The former Empress prepared to visit her parents.

This required less preparation than a formal visit and more preparation than walking to the western pavilion.

The wrapped book was placed in a carrying case. Fang Ru checked that the pages had not been bent and that the paper wrapping was secure. A small note from Yuru's father, which had accompanied the book when he lent it to her, was tucked inside the cover.

The two books waiting in the eastern courtyard had not yet been examined. Yuru intended to look at them before deciding whether to borrow both.

Fang Ru stood beside the table.

"Would you like to take the basket of fruit as well?"

"No. It was sent to me."

"Then perhaps we should take something else."

"Why?"

"Because you are visiting your parents."

"They have fruit."

"They may have more."

Yuru considered this.

"Then we shall not compete with them."

Fang Ru accepted the decision.

A short message had already been sent to the eastern courtyard. Yuru would visit after the morning meal, without a formal reception. Her mother had replied that she would be pleased to see her.

The former Empress left the guest residence with Fang Ru and two attendants carrying the book.

There was no procession.

There were no ceremonial announcements.

No one had been asked to prepare a formal reception.

The eastern courtyard was familiar, and Yuru found the walk pleasant.

Her father was waiting in the small library.

The Duke of Xu stood beside a low table where two books had been placed. One was a study of household estates and the management of stewards. The other concerned provincial granaries and the administration of reserve stores.

He looked at the carrying case.

"You have finished the first book."

"Yes, Father."

"And brought it back."

"As promised."

He accepted the case and opened it briefly to check the book's condition.

"You have taken good care of it."

"It was an interesting book."

"Did you find anything worth marking?"

"Several passages."

"Anything that troubled you?"

Yuru smiled.

"A few."

Her father looked pleased.

"Then it was worth reading."

He set the returned volume aside.

Her mother entered shortly afterward.

"Have you come to borrow the two books?"

"That was my intention."

"Your father has been keeping them ready."

The Duke of Xu glanced at his wife.

"They are books. They do not require a guard."

"Then why have you told the library attendant not to lend them to anyone else?"

"Because I knew Yuru intended to borrow them."

Madam Xu looked at her daughter.

"He has been waiting for you to visit."

Yuru looked at her father.

The Duke of Xu cleared his throat.

"I had already selected them."

"Of course."

He gestured toward the table.

"The first concerns household estates, land revenues, repairs, and the duties of stewards. The second is a collection of provincial reports on grain storage and distribution."

Yuru examined the titles.

"May I look through them first?"

"Certainly."

She opened the first volume and read its introductory section. The author had divided household management into several areas: land, rents, maintenance, labour, and accounts. The writing was practical, though some of its assumptions belonged to an older generation.

The second volume contained reports from several provinces. Some described successful reserve arrangements. Others discussed failures caused by poor storage, inaccurate registers, and delayed transport.

Yuru turned several pages.

Her father watched her.

"Do you wish to borrow both?"

"Yes."

He nodded.

The library attendant brought a register.

Yuru looked at it.

Her father noticed.

"What is it?"

"Nothing. I was wondering whether the borrowing record requires a return date."

"It does."

"Then I shall ask for a reasonable period."

The Duke of Xu smiled.

"Three months."

Yuru looked at him.

"That is generous."

"You have always read carefully."

Madam Xu sat down.

"Do not let him lend you every book in the library."

"I have not."

"Not yet," her husband said.

Yuru laughed.

The attendant recorded the two books under her name and entered the date. The Duke of Xu signed as the lending authority.

The books were wrapped separately.

Yuru watched the attendant complete the register.

"That is sufficient," she said.

Her father looked at her.

"Do you think the record is unnecessary?"

"No. It is useful. It means that neither of us will have to remember which books were borrowed."

The Duke of Xu nodded.

"An excellent reason."

Madam Xu looked between them.

"Then the library has gained two records and lost two books."

"It has lent two books," Yuru corrected.

"That is what I said."

"It is not quite the same."

Her mother smiled.

The family remained together for a while. They discussed the weather, the garden, and a distant relative whose letter had arrived several days earlier. The conversation did not turn toward the palace's administration.

Yuru did not bring it up.

Her father asked whether she had found the guest residence comfortable.

"It is suitable for now."

"And your permanent residence?"

"I have not decided."

"The eastern courtyard remains available."

"I know."

"You need not decide immediately."

Yuru looked at him.

"Thank you."

He nodded.

There was no further discussion.

When she departed, she carried two new books and had returned one old one.

Her mother walked with her to the courtyard entrance.

"You should come again when you wish."

"I will."

"And do not wait until you have finished every book before visiting."

Yuru smiled.

"I shall try."

Madam Xu watched her daughter leave.

"She is becoming accustomed to having nothing scheduled."

The Duke of Xu looked toward the library.

"She has two books now."

"Those are not schedules."

"No," he said. "They are books."

Madam Xu glanced at him.

"Do not select another one yet."

He considered this.

"I had already thought of one."

"Then think of it quietly."

At the temporary residence near the eastern market, Mingzhao had completed the final account for the two-hundred-bolt silk order.

The final statement had been copied and sealed. One copy remained with the workshop, one was sent to Lin Shuyun, and one was kept with Sun Qiming's supporting papers.

The buyer's payment had been received in full.

The goods had been delivered.

The workers had been paid.

The account showed a positive margin.

Mingzhao read the final summary once more.

Qiao Yue stood beside her.

"Are you going to accept another order?"

"No."

Qiao Yue blinked.

"Not immediately?"

"Not immediately."

"Why?"

"Because we have just completed this one."

"Would that not be the best time to accept another?"

"It may be. It may also be the best time to examine what we learned before making another commitment."

Qiao Yue considered this.

"That sounds less exciting."

"It is less expensive."

Lin Shuyun arrived with a letter from a merchant who had previously expressed interest in the workshop's silk.

She placed it on the table.

"An inquiry."

Mingzhao opened it.

The merchant asked whether the workshop would accept an order of one hundred and fifty bolts, with a deposit and the balance payable after resale through his own trading house.

Mingzhao read the terms.

The deposit covered only a small part of the production cost. The remaining payment depended on the merchant's resale, and no fixed settlement date had been specified.

She put the letter down.

"Decline."

Lin nodded.

"That is what I expected."

Qiao Yue looked at the paper.

"Is this the same merchant who proposed deferred payment before?"

"Yes," Lin said. "He has not changed the important terms."

"Perhaps he thought we might have changed our minds."

Mingzhao shook her head.

"If he wants another discussion, he can propose a firm payment schedule."

Lin sat down.

"He may not."

"Then we have saved ourselves a discussion."

Qiao Yue collected the letter.

"Should I write that we are not accepting the order under the proposed terms?"

"Yes. Do not say we are unwilling to work with him in the future. His terms are unacceptable, not necessarily the man."

Lin smiled.

"That is a careful distinction."

"It is also true."

The reply was prepared.

Mingzhao then turned to the workshop's internal report.

The two trainees had continued working under ordinary supervision. Their folding work had improved, although one still required more time on the tighter edge sections. The senior finisher had suggested assigning them to different stages until their speed became more consistent.

Mingzhao read the recommendation.

"That seems reasonable."

Lin nodded.

"One has become more accurate. The other is faster, but still needs checking."

"Then do not judge them by the same measure."

The workshop supervisor's report had also proposed keeping the revised folding method for future work.

Mingzhao tapped the relevant paragraph.

"Only when the buyer's specifications permit it."

"Of course," Lin said.

"The method was approved for this order. It is not automatically approved for every order."

Lin nodded.

She had learned that a successful improvement could become a problem if someone treated it as a universal rule.

"Shall we hold a workshop meeting next week?" she asked.

"Yes. Review the order, the training, the inspection records, and the final cost. We should identify what can be repeated and what depended on this particular buyer's specifications."

Qiao Yue looked at the papers.

"Will there be another register?"

"There is already one."

"Then perhaps we should give it a rest."

Mingzhao looked at her.

"The register does not need rest."

"Neither does it need to grow every day."

Lin laughed.

"That may be the first sensible objection anyone has made to an account book."

Mingzhao put the report away.

The next order would be considered when there was one worth accepting.

Until then, the workshop could improve its methods without promising work it had not agreed to perform.

At the Marquisate, Shen Mingyuan received a written inquiry concerning the old payment entry attributed to Mingzhao.

The accounts office had found a reference to the payment in a general purchasing summary. The summary included a date, an amount, and the mark of the estate purchasing seal.

It did not include the original voucher.

It did not identify the recipient.

It did not state the goods or services purchased.

Mingyuan read the inquiry.

The clerk who had prepared it had written:

*The payment appears to have been made under the authority of the eldest young lady.*

Mingyuan drew a line through the sentence.

He wrote beneath it:

*The entry bears the estate purchasing seal. The seal register identifies Chen Rui as custodian on that date. The original voucher has not been found. The recipient and purpose remain unverified. No personal receipt or authorization by the eldest young lady has been established.*

He handed the paper back.

"Use this wording."

The clerk looked at the corrections.

"Would it not be simpler to say that the payment was made under her authority?"

"No."

"The entry is in the account."

"The entry proves that a payment was recorded."

"The seal was used."

"The seal register identifies its custodian."

"Then the payment was authorized."

"Possibly. But we do not yet know who authorized it, what was purchased, or who received the money."

The clerk lowered his eyes.

Mingyuan continued.

"If we write that my sister authorized it, we are making a claim that the documents do not establish."

"Then what should we do?"

"Find the voucher."

The clerk bowed.

Mingyuan watched him leave.

He had once assumed that the family accounts could be understood by gathering the books, comparing totals, and asking the old stewards what they remembered.

He had since learned that memories could be incomplete, records could be separated, and a mark on a page did not necessarily establish the full chain of responsibility.

He had also learned that a family member's name appearing beside an entry did not prove that the person had received the money.

The distinction mattered.

Especially when the person concerned had spent six years being expected to answer for every household expense.

He returned to the grain file.

Zhang's latest report had clarified the difference between the warehouse's total stock and its immediately available stock. The original figure of forty-seven sacks remained. The instruction for twenty remained. Eighteen were recorded as received. Two were unconfirmed. Twenty-seven were still unexplained.

The separate transport payment still had no established destination.

Mingyuan closed the folder.

There was no new evidence.

There was no reason to pretend otherwise.

Later that afternoon, Steward Zhou reported that the southern kitchen's water channel had been repaired and that the final payment had been recorded under the urgent-purchase provision.

The original estimate and the final quantity were both preserved.

The purchasing clerk had complained that the record was too long.

Liang Wen had replied that the repair was now finished and the register could remain long without requiring the clerk to read it aloud.

Steward Zhou had considered this a reasonable answer.

Mingyuan approved the record.

He then asked whether the new consolidated purchasing register was being maintained properly.

Liang Wen reported that the unit mismatch had been corrected, the urgent repair had been documented, and all original entries remained visible.

"Any further problems?"

"Several small corrections."

"Any missing authorizations?"

"None in the entries reviewed today."

Mingyuan nodded.

"Continue."

Liang Wen bowed.

He was beginning to understand the new procedure.

It was not designed to make the accounts beautiful.

It was designed to make it difficult for anyone to turn a missing record into a confident statement.

This was an unpleasant responsibility.

It was also becoming his responsibility.

In the evening, Yuru returned to the western guest residence with the two books.

Fang Ru helped place them on the low table.

"Two more."

"Yes."

"And the first one has been returned."

"Yes."

Fang Ru looked at the three empty spaces on the shelf where the books had once been arranged.

"Your father's library must be relieved."

"Why?"

"It has recovered one book and lost two."

Yuru smiled.

"That is how lending works."

"Then perhaps your father will need to acquire more books."

"He already has many."

"Then perhaps he will need another shelf."

Yuru removed the wrapping from the first new book and opened it.

The chapter headings were practical and clearly arranged. She read the first page, then turned to the section on estate maintenance.

Fang Ru watched.

"Will you read this one first?"

"Yes."

"And the book about provincial granaries?"

"Afterward."

"Will you finish both within three months?"

Yuru looked at the borrowing register's small copy, which Fang Ru had brought back with the books.

"That is the return period."

"Then you have a schedule."

Yuru closed the book.

"I have a return date. That is not the same as a schedule."

Fang Ru considered the distinction.

"Your father may disagree."

"He is welcome to."

Yuru opened the book again.

The former Empress read for the remainder of the afternoon.

She had returned a book, borrowed two, and visited her parents.

The palace had not asked her to do anything else.

For the day, that was enough.
