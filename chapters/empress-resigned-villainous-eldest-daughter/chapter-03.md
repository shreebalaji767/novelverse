# Chapter 3: The Empress's Resignation Has Reached the Palace. The Brooms Have Become a Government Matter.

**25th Day of the Third Month, 18th Year of Yonghe.**

The Emperor had slept poorly.

This was not unusual when the empire faced a serious problem. A border dispute, a failed harvest, a provincial rebellion, or a minister suspected of corruption could keep Zhao Jingheng awake until dawn.

The present difficulty was considerably more humiliating.

He had spent part of the previous evening reading the preliminary review of the Empress's eighty-seven responsibilities. By the time he reached the final page, he had learned that the Empress's office was involved in the approval of palace brooms, kitchen ladles, laundry schedules, seasonal clothing, and the repair of storage containers.

He had also learned that the Department of Palace Stores had been unable to explain why the purchase of new brooms required the Empress's approval.

The Emperor had put the report aside.

Then he had picked it up again.

Then he had asked Gao Shun whether the Empress personally inspected the brooms.

Gao Shun had replied that Her Majesty did not.

This had not improved his mood.

At the hour of Chen, Zhao Jingheng entered the Hall of Mental Cultivation and found three memorials waiting on his desk.

The first concerned river transport.

The second concerned the appointment of a provincial official.

The third concerned the inner palace.

He looked at the third memorial.

"Who sent this?"

"Minister Lu of Rites, Your Majesty," Gao Shun replied.

"Why?"

"Minister Lu requests clarification regarding the division of ceremonial responsibilities."

The Emperor opened the memorial.

He read the first paragraph.

Then he read it again.

Minister Lu had written that certain ceremonies required the Empress's participation, while others were traditionally administered through her office. He requested instructions on whether those duties would be transferred to the Ministry of Rites in the event of the Empress's resignation.

Zhao Jingheng set the memorial down.

"Has the Empress resigned?"

"No, Your Majesty. She has submitted a petition."

"Has the petition been approved?"

"No, Your Majesty."

"Then why is Minister Lu asking where to transfer duties?"

Gao Shun remained silent.

The Emperor looked at the memorial again.

"Summon the Grand Secretary."

"At once, Your Majesty."

"And tell Minister Lu that he is to review the responsibilities currently under his authority before requesting additional ones."

Gao Shun bowed.

"Yes, Your Majesty."

Zhao Jingheng reached for the river transport memorial.

At least the river did not require him to approve the purchase of a new broom.

The Grand Secretary arrived shortly afterward.

Wei Zhong was carrying a new bundle of documents.

The Emperor looked at it.

"Is that the review?"

"Yes, Your Majesty."

"How many responsibilities have been examined?"

"Twenty-six."

Zhao Jingheng glanced at the report.

"And how many have been assigned?"

"Eleven."

"How many require changes to the regulations?"

"Nine."

"And the remaining six?"

"They require further examination."

The Emperor nodded.

"Better."

The Grand Secretary placed the report on the desk.

"Your Majesty, there is another matter."

Zhao Jingheng looked up.

"What now?"

"The review has revealed that several duties are performed by the same administrators under different instructions."

"Explain."

Wei Zhong opened the report.

"For example, the Department of Palace Stores maintains the inventory of seasonal supplies. The inner palace administrators maintain the distribution records. The Empress's office receives a consolidated report and approves the annual replacement schedule."

"That sounds reasonable."

"It would be, Your Majesty, if the three records were consistent."

The Emperor's expression changed.

"They are not?"

"No."

"Why?"

"The Department of Palace Stores counts goods when they enter the warehouse. The inner palace administrators count goods when they are distributed. The Empress's office uses the final figures from both departments to determine the replacement requirement."

Zhao Jingheng frowned.

"Then the discrepancy is between the departments."

"Yes, Your Majesty."

"Why has the Empress been resolving it?"

The Grand Secretary lowered his head.

"Because the departments have not agreed on which inventory should be treated as authoritative."

The Emperor stared at him.

"How long has this been happening?"

"We have not yet established the exact period."

"Find out."

"Yes, Your Majesty."

Zhao Jingheng leaned back.

He was beginning to see a pattern.

The inner palace had not necessarily been asking the Empress to perform every task herself. Instead, it had created a system in which disagreements, incomplete records, and unclear responsibilities were sent upward until they reached her.

She had become the final authority not because every matter required her judgment, but because no one else had been made to settle the matter.

"Grand Secretary."

"Your Majesty?"

"Do not simply transfer these responsibilities to another person. If the records are inconsistent, correct the records. If the authority is unclear, clarify the authority. I do not want another official spending eighteen years approving the same unnecessary matters."

Wei Zhong bowed.

"Your Majesty is correct."

The Emperor picked up his tea.

"Is there anything else?"

The Grand Secretary hesitated.

"Minister Lu wishes to meet with Your Majesty regarding ceremonial responsibilities."

"Tell him to submit a written explanation after reviewing the existing regulations."

"Yes, Your Majesty."

"And tell him that the Empress's petition is not an invitation for the Ministry of Rites to collect every task it can find."

The Grand Secretary lowered his head.

"I will convey Your Majesty's instruction."

As he withdrew, Zhao Jingheng returned to the report.

Twenty-six responsibilities had been examined.

Sixty-one remained.

He looked at the number for a long time.

Then he called Gao Shun.

"Have breakfast brought."

Gao Shun bowed.

"Yes, Your Majesty."

The Emperor paused.

"And make certain the kitchen knows how much food is required."

"Yes, Your Majesty."

"Do not ask the Empress."

Gao Shun lowered his head.

"Of course, Your Majesty."

At the Qingyuan Marquisate, Shen Mingzhao was also beginning to understand the difficulty of transferring responsibility.

The difference was that she had expected the difficulty.

Her family had not.

At the hour of Mao, Steward Zhou arrived in her eastern courtyard with a preliminary list of the estate's outstanding accounts.

Mingzhao was already awake.

She had spent the previous evening reviewing the documents concerning her personal investments and had found two discrepancies between the partnership records and the household account.

Neither discrepancy was large.

Both needed to be investigated.

"Young Miss," Steward Zhou said, placing the list on the table, "the northern warehouse inventory has been completed."

"Has it been signed?"

"Yes."

"By whom?"

"The warehouse steward and two senior clerks."

"Have the figures been checked against the previous inventory?"

"Yes. There is a difference in the number of stored grain sacks."

"How large?"

"Forty-seven."

Mingzhao looked up.

"Forty-seven?"

"Yes, Young Miss."

"Is there a record of distribution?"

"There is a record of grain being sent to the household kitchens, but the figures do not fully account for the difference."

"Then the inventory is not complete."

Steward Zhou nodded.

"That is why I have marked the difference for review."

"Good. Have the warehouse steward provide the distribution records and delivery acknowledgments. Do not assume the grain is missing until the figures have been reconciled."

"Understood."

Mingzhao took the list.

There were several entries marked in red ink.

One concerned the grain warehouse.

Another concerned repairs to the northern storage building.

A third concerned payments made to a supplier of lamp oil.

"Who reviewed the lamp oil payments?"

"The account clerk."

"Who approved them?"

"The household steward."

"Did the supplier confirm the quantities?"

"Not yet."

Mingzhao put down the list.

"Send for the supplier's delivery records."

"Yes, Young Miss."

She looked at the steward.

"Has Father appointed a representative to review my property claims?"

"Not yet."

"Have they selected a candidate?"

"Your father has asked the family steward to suggest several names."

"That is not an appointment."

"No, Young Miss."

Mingzhao nodded.

"Then we continue preparing our own records."

Steward Zhou hesitated.

"Young Miss, may I offer a suggestion?"

"Speak."

"Perhaps you should separate the records into three groups. Your personal property, the claims you have against the household, and the family estate accounts. It may prevent confusion later."

Mingzhao looked at him.

"That is sensible."

She reached for a blank sheet.

"Prepare three separate inventories. Do not combine them."

Steward Zhou bowed.

"I will arrange it."

"Also, make a fourth list."

"A fourth?"

"Outstanding obligations that may affect the settlement. Loans, guarantees, unpaid wages, and any contracts for which my name appears as an approving party."

The steward's expression became serious.

"That may take some time."

"Then take the time."

Mingzhao looked down at the papers.

She had no intention of leaving behind a hidden liability that could later be used to drag her back into the household's affairs.

Her independence would not be built on incomplete accounts.

The first family response arrived before breakfast.

It came in the form of a message from Madam Liang.

Mingzhao was requested to attend her mother's private sitting room.

Qiao Yue, who had been arranging the documents, looked at the message.

"Does Madam wish to discuss the settlement?"

"Probably."

"Should I prepare the documents?"

"No."

Qiao Yue looked puzzled.

"Why not?"

"Because if Mother wishes to discuss the settlement, she can request the relevant documents. If she wishes to discuss my feelings, the account books will not help."

Qiao Yue considered this.

"Should I accompany you?"

"Wait here."

Mingzhao entered her mother's sitting room alone.

Madam Liang was seated near the window, with a cup of tea on the table. She was dressed elegantly, and her expression was controlled.

"Sit down," she said.

Mingzhao greeted her and took the offered seat.

Madam Liang studied her daughter.

"You have been very busy."

"The accounts require review."

"Must you spend every moment looking at account books?"

"At present, yes."

"You have become obsessed with this matter."

Mingzhao did not answer.

Madam Liang sighed.

"Your father has not yet selected a representative because he does not want the matter to become complicated."

"The matter is already complicated."

"It need not be."

"Then an independent review should make it simpler."

Madam Liang's expression tightened.

"You keep saying independent. Are you suggesting that your family cannot be trusted?"

"I am suggesting that the person who prepares an account should not be the only person who verifies it."

"Your father has managed the Marquisate for many years."

"Then he should have no difficulty providing the records."

Madam Liang looked away.

For a moment, she seemed to consider a different approach.

"Mingzhao, I want you to understand something. Your brother is the heir. The estate must remain stable. Your younger sister's future must also be considered. If you insist on taking your property away, the family will have to adjust several arrangements."

"I understand."

"Then perhaps you should wait until your brother has settled into his responsibilities."

Mingzhao looked at her mother.

"How long?"

Madam Liang did not answer.

"One year? Three years? Five?"

"Do not speak like that."

"Mother, I have already spent six years managing the household. If I wait until my brother is ready, who will decide when he is ready?"

"Your father will."

"Then Father should appoint someone to train him."

Madam Liang put down her cup.

"Your brother is not incompetent."

"I did not say he was."

"You speak as though he cannot do anything."

"I said he has not yet demonstrated that he can manage the accounts. Those are different statements."

Madam Liang's face grew colder.

"Your father expects you to reconsider."

Mingzhao folded her hands.

"Then Father will be disappointed."

Her mother stared at her.

"Do you truly intend to leave?"

"Yes."

"Even if your family does not support you?"

"I will settle what is mine. I will not demand that the family support my independent household."

"That is not what I meant."

Mingzhao waited.

Madam Liang looked at her for a long moment.

Then she said, "You have always been too proud."

Mingzhao lowered her eyes.

"Perhaps."

She did not argue.

Her mother seemed almost unsettled by the absence of a defensive response.

Mingzhao rose.

"Is there anything else?"

Madam Liang shook her head.

Mingzhao bowed and left.

Outside the room, Qiao Yue was waiting.

"Young Miss?"

"Nothing has changed."

Qiao Yue sighed.

"Then I suppose we continue with the accounts."

"Yes."

At the palace, the first serious disagreement over the Empress's responsibilities emerged before noon.

It concerned the appointment of senior palace servants.

Minister Han of Personnel believed that appointments should be handled through the existing palace administration, with the Emperor retaining final authority over the highest positions.

Minister Lu of Rites argued that certain positions were traditionally associated with ceremonial responsibilities and should remain under the Empress's supervision.

The Grand Secretary believed both ministers were trying to avoid a difficult administrative question.

The disagreement reached the Emperor shortly before midday.

Zhao Jingheng listened to their arguments.

Minister Han spoke first.

"Your Majesty, the appointment of palace officials should follow a clear hierarchy. Routine appointments may be made by the relevant department. Senior appointments should be submitted for imperial approval."

Minister Lu bowed.

"Your Majesty, certain positions have duties closely connected with the Empress's office. Removing the Empress's supervision without establishing an equivalent authority may cause confusion."

The Emperor looked at him.

"Which positions?"

Minister Lu produced a list.

"The senior ceremonial attendants, the supervisors responsible for formal household observances, and several officials who manage the arrangements for imperial family gatherings."

"Are these positions currently vacant?"

"No, Your Majesty."

"Are the officials performing their duties?"

"Yes."

"Have they failed to perform them?"

"Not to my knowledge."

"Then why must their authority change?"

Minister Lu paused.

"Because the Empress has submitted a petition."

Zhao Jingheng leaned back.

"Minister Lu, if a department has been functioning properly, I do not intend to disrupt it merely because another office is changing its responsibilities."

"Your Majesty is wise."

"But if a department has been relying on the Empress to resolve matters that fall under its own authority, it must be corrected."

Minister Lu bowed.

"Yes, Your Majesty."

The Emperor turned to Minister Han.

"Prepare a list of appointments that require imperial approval, appointments that can be made by the existing administrators, and appointments that require new regulations. Do not include ceremonial duties merely because they are connected to a ceremony."

Minister Han bowed.

"Understood."

Zhao Jingheng looked toward the Grand Secretary.

"Have the departments submit their actual procedures. I want to know what they do, not what they believe they ought to do."

Wei Zhong bowed.

"Yes, Your Majesty."

The meeting ended.

As the ministers withdrew, the Emperor looked at Gao Shun.

"Has the Empress been informed of these discussions?"

"No, Your Majesty."

"Good."

He paused.

"How is she?"

Gao Shun considered his answer.

"Her Majesty has continued her usual duties. She reviewed the palace stores inventory this morning and instructed the Department of Palace Stores to provide proper calculations before submitting its procurement request."

The Emperor looked at him.

"Did the department provide them?"

"No, Your Majesty."

"Why?"

"They have requested another day."

Zhao Jingheng closed his eyes.

"Of course they have."

Xu Yuru, meanwhile, had begun to organize her own records.

She was not waiting for the Emperor's decision.

In the private study of Chengming Palace, she had placed the eighty-seven responsibilities into separate categories.

Some belonged to the inner palace administration.

Some belonged to the Department of Palace Stores.

Some concerned ceremonies and should be coordinated with the Ministry of Rites.

Others involved the education and household arrangements of the imperial children.

There were also matters that did not properly belong to any single office.

She marked those separately.

Madam Fang stood beside the table.

"Your Majesty, the current list contains eighty-seven responsibilities. The revised list contains ninety-two."

Xu Yuru looked up.

"Why has it increased?"

"Five responsibilities were not included in the original appendix. They were being handled through informal arrangements."

Xu Yuru stared at the list.

"Which ones?"

Madam Fang read them out.

"Maintenance of the private garden paths. Distribution of medicine to the senior palace servants. Replacement of damaged household utensils. Records of gifts received by the imperial children. And the annual inspection of the kitchens."

Xu Yuru leaned back.

"Why was the kitchen inspection not included?"

"Because it was considered part of general household supervision."

"Who performs it?"

"Two senior administrators inspect the kitchens, then submit a report to your office."

"And what do I do with the report?"

"You approve the corrective measures."

"Do the administrators have authority to correct ordinary deficiencies?"

"They do."

"Then why does the report come to me?"

Madam Fang lowered her eyes.

"Because the procedure has always required it."

Xu Yuru looked at the list again.

There it was.

The phrase that had quietly ruled her life for years.

*Because the procedure has always required it.*

She took her brush and crossed out the final approval requirement.

"From now on, ordinary kitchen deficiencies should be corrected by the administrators. Serious matters should be reported through the appropriate office. There is no reason for every damaged utensil to reach Chengming Palace."

Madam Fang nodded.

"Yes, Your Majesty."

Xu Yuru moved to the next item.

"Medicine distribution to senior servants?"

"The palace physician prepares the schedule, and the administrators distribute the medicine."

"Then the physician should certify the schedule, and the administrators should record distribution."

"Yes, Your Majesty."

"Why does my office receive it?"

Madam Fang did not answer.

Xu Yuru sighed.

"Remove the routine approval."

She continued through the list.

By the time she finished, six additional duties had been reassigned to their existing administrators.

It was a small change.

But it was the first practical step toward the arrangement she wanted.

Madam Fang gathered the revised papers.

"Your Majesty, should these instructions be sent to the departments?"

"Not yet. The Emperor has ordered a review. I will not issue changes that conflict with his decision."

"Then should the documents remain here?"

"Prepare a copy for my records. The original can remain with the current office until His Majesty decides."

Madam Fang bowed.

Xu Yuru looked at the window.

The garden beyond it was bright with spring sunlight.

She had once enjoyed the work of organizing the palace. She liked order. She liked knowing that a difficult problem could be solved through careful planning.

What she no longer enjoyed was being expected to solve every problem simply because she had solved the previous one.

She wanted to spend time reading without a servant arriving with a dispute over kitchen utensils.

She wanted to have breakfast without remembering a warehouse inventory.

She wanted to speak with her children without a clerk waiting outside the door with a report that had already been checked by three people.

She did not want to abandon the imperial family.

She wanted the imperial family to stop treating her as the only person capable of maintaining it.

In the afternoon, Shen Mingzhao received the first independent response from one of her commercial partners.

The letter came from Lin Shuyun, a merchant who had worked with Mingzhao for several years.

Lin Shuyun was not a close friend. She was a practical businesswoman who respected clear agreements and disliked unnecessary complications. Their relationship had always been based on mutual benefit.

Mingzhao unfolded the letter.

Lin Shuyun confirmed the current balance of their silk warehouse partnership. She also provided a copy of the original agreement and a statement of the most recent distribution.

The figures were consistent.

Mingzhao marked the entry as verified.

At the end of the letter, Lin Shuyun had added a short note.

*I heard from the Eastern Commercial Street that the Qingyuan Marquisate is reviewing its household arrangements. If your personal investments are being separated, please inform me which partnership records should be transferred to your own account. I would prefer not to have the wrong person signing future instructions.*

Mingzhao read the note twice.

Then she smiled faintly.

At least one person in the capital understood that a change in household arrangements did not automatically alter a business agreement.

She wrote a reply.

She confirmed that the partnership remained valid and that she would provide any necessary documentation after the settlement.

Then she added a request for an updated valuation.

She did not intend to assume that the investment was worth the amount recorded in the old account.

A partnership could be profitable one season and troublesome the next.

Independence required accurate figures, not comforting ones.

Qiao Yue entered while Mingzhao was sealing the letter.

"Young Miss, Steward Zhou has returned."

"Send him in."

The steward entered with another bundle of documents.

"The Marquis has selected a representative."

Mingzhao put down the letter.

"Who?"

"Mr. Chen, the family's former legal adviser."

Mingzhao considered this.

Mr. Chen had handled several property matters for the Marquisate. He was experienced and knew the family's arrangements.

He was also a long-standing adviser to Shen Guowei.

"Will he act alone?"

"The Marquis says Mr. Chen will review the documents."

"Then we should ask whether he is acting as the family's adviser or as an independent reviewer."

Steward Zhou nodded.

"I can prepare the question."

"Do so. If he is reviewing the family estate, he may represent the Marquisate. If he is reviewing my personal claims, I require an independent assessment."

"Yes, Young Miss."

Mingzhao looked at the papers.

"Has Father appointed an independent accountant?"

"Not yet."

"Then the review has not been arranged."

Steward Zhou lowered his head.

"Understood."

She was not surprised.

Her family wished to settle the matter without admitting that it was a dispute.

But a settlement required both sides to know what was being settled.

Mingzhao turned to Qiao Yue.

"Have the residence inquiries begun?"

"Yes, Young Miss. Two properties are available in the eastern district. One is near Eastern Commercial Street, and the other is farther north, close to the canal."

"Have they provided ownership records?"

"The agents have promised to send them."

"Do not visit either property until the records arrive."

Qiao Yue nodded.

"Yes."

"And do not let them know we are desperate to purchase."

"We are not desperate."

"Good. Then do not allow them to charge us as though we are."

That evening, the Emperor summoned Xu Yuru to the Hall of Mental Cultivation.

The message arrived shortly before the evening meal.

Madam Fang helped the Empress change into a more formal robe. Xu Yuru selected a dark green garment with restrained embroidery and wore a simple hair ornament.

She entered the hall after the announcement.

Zhao Jingheng was seated near the window.

"Yuru."

"Your Majesty."

He gestured for her to sit.

"I have begun reviewing your petition."

"I understand."

"The Grand Secretary and the Ministers of Rites and Personnel have examined the first portion of the responsibilities listed in your appendix."

Xu Yuru waited.

"Some matters are already handled by the departments responsible for them. Others have become dependent on your personal approval through custom."

"That is correct."

The Emperor studied her.

"Did you know there were ninety-two responsibilities?"

Xu Yuru paused.

"The original list contained eighty-seven."

"Five additional matters were discovered."

She nodded.

"That is possible. I prepared the list based on the responsibilities formally routed through my office. Some informal arrangements may not have been included."

Zhao Jingheng looked at her.

"You knew there were informal arrangements?"

"I knew that some departments sent matters to me without a clear regulation requiring it."

"Why did you allow it?"

Xu Yuru considered the question.

"At first, because it was easier to resolve the matter than to return it. Later, because the departments became accustomed to receiving decisions from my office. Eventually, I was correcting the same problems repeatedly."

The Emperor was quiet.

"Then you acknowledge that some of this is your responsibility."

"Yes."

He seemed to have expected a defensive answer.

Instead, she had accepted the point without hesitation.

Xu Yuru continued.

"I should have required the departments to follow their own authority earlier. But the fact that I allowed the arrangement to continue does not mean it should continue indefinitely."

Zhao Jingheng nodded slowly.

"That is fair."

He picked up a sheet.

"The review has found disagreements between the Department of Palace Stores and the inner palace administrators concerning inventory records."

"I am aware."

"Have you been resolving those disagreements?"

"Yes."

"Why?"

"Because neither department has been willing to accept the other's figures."

"Then why not require them to reconcile the records?"

"I have. Several times."

The Emperor looked at the paper.

"Did they?"

"Eventually. Usually after the matter had been returned more than once."

Zhao Jingheng put down the document.

"Then the problem is not simply that the Empress's office receives too many matters. It is that the departments do not follow instructions."

"That is part of the problem."

He looked at her.

"What is the rest?"

Xu Yuru answered carefully.

"The palace has developed a habit of treating my approval as a substitute for responsibility. If a department submits an incomplete report, it expects my office to correct it. If two officials disagree, they expect me to decide. If an administrator is uncertain, he waits for my instruction instead of consulting the regulations."

"And you have allowed it."

"Yes."

The Emperor leaned back.

For a moment, he looked almost troubled.

"Yuru, I do not want the inner palace to fall into disorder."

"Neither do I."

"Your departure would create difficulties."

"Then we should prepare for them."

He looked at her.

"Would you accept a temporary reduction in your duties while the review proceeds?"

Xu Yuru considered the offer.

It was reasonable.

It was also not what she had requested.

"I will cooperate with a transition arrangement," she said. "But I do not wish for the review to become a permanent excuse to keep the current system."

"I understand."

"Your Majesty, I am not asking to abandon the palace tomorrow. I am asking for a clear decision and a practical plan. If the position of Empress is to remain occupied, then its responsibilities should be defined. If the position is to be relinquished, then the imperial family must decide how to proceed."

Zhao Jingheng looked at her for a long moment.

"Do you truly no longer wish to remain Empress?"

Xu Yuru did not answer immediately.

She had expected the question.

She had also known that it would be difficult to answer.

"I do not wish to continue in the position as it currently functions."

"That is not quite the same answer."

"No."

He waited.

Xu Yuru looked down at her hands.

"I have served the imperial family for eighteen years. I have no wish to erase those years or pretend they meant nothing. But I do not want the rest of my life to be decided by the assumption that I will always be available to manage everything."

The Emperor's expression softened.

"Have I treated you unfairly?"

Xu Yuru looked at him.

"You have trusted me."

"That is not an answer."

"It is part of the answer."

She continued quietly.

"Your Majesty has not ordered me to manage every matter personally. But when the palace sent its problems to me, I resolved them. When the departments failed, I corrected their work. When the family required arrangements, I made them. It was convenient for everyone, including me, to believe that this was simply how the palace should operate."

Zhao Jingheng lowered his eyes.

"And now?"

"Now I wish to stop."

The Emperor did not immediately respond.

The silence between them was not hostile.

It was simply unfamiliar.

After a while, he said, "I will continue the review. No final decision has been made."

Xu Yuru bowed her head.

"Thank you, Your Majesty."

He looked at her.

"Do you need anything?"

She considered the question.

There were many things she could have requested.

A residence outside the inner palace. A formal allowance. A change in her duties. A promise that the princes would be cared for.

Instead, she said, "I would like the departments to stop sending me matters they can resolve themselves."

Zhao Jingheng stared at her.

Then he laughed.

It was not a loud laugh, but it was genuine.

"That may be the most difficult request you have made."

Xu Yuru's expression remained calm.

"I am aware."

Later that night, the Emperor reviewed the remaining documents alone.

The Grand Secretary had submitted another list of unresolved responsibilities.

The number had increased again.

Ninety-four.

Zhao Jingheng read the new entries.

One concerned the approval of the palace's annual kitchen utensil replacement schedule.

Another concerned the inspection of the imperial laundry.

The final entry concerned the allocation of courtyard cleaning tools.

He put the report down.

Then he called Gao Shun.

"Bring me the Empress's original petition."

Gao Shun retrieved it.

The Emperor opened the document and read the final paragraph.

Xu Yuru had written that she was willing to assist with a reasonable transition, but that the transition must not become a means of preserving the existing arrangement under another name.

He read the sentence twice.

Then he looked at the growing pile of reports.

"How many responsibilities remain?"

Gao Shun answered carefully.

"The review is ongoing, Your Majesty."

"That is not a number."

"I believe the Grand Secretary is preparing the latest figure."

Zhao Jingheng rubbed his forehead.

"Find out."

Gao Shun bowed.

"Yes, Your Majesty."

After he left, Zhao Jingheng looked down at the petition.

He had once believed that the Empress's work was an extension of her natural ability.

Now he was beginning to see that ability had become an excuse for the rest of the palace to avoid doing its own work.

He set the petition aside.

Tomorrow, he would speak with the Grand Secretary again.

And he would issue a clear instruction to the departments.

The Empress's resignation was not to become a reason for them to request more authority, more money, or more staff without explaining their existing responsibilities.

The palace would have to learn to manage itself.

Whether it liked the lesson or not.

At the same hour, Mingzhao was sitting at her desk in the Qingyuan Marquisate.

The first property inquiry had arrived.

The residence was located in the eastern district, within reasonable distance of the commercial streets. It had a main hall, two courtyards, a small study, a kitchen, and several servant rooms.

The asking price was high.

The ownership record was incomplete.

Mingzhao put the document aside.

The second property was farther north, near the canal. It was smaller, but the roof required repairs and the rear courtyard had drainage problems.

She marked it for inspection.

Qiao Yue leaned over her shoulder.

"Which one do you prefer?"

"I have not seen either."

"The second one sounds less expensive."

"It also sounds as though the roof leaks."

"Perhaps only a little."

"Rain does not fall only a little because the owner asks for a lower price."

Qiao Yue nodded.

"Then we should inspect it."

"After the ownership documents arrive."

Mingzhao closed the property list.

There was another document beneath it.

The first draft of her personal property inventory.

She read through the entries.

Her dowry assets were listed separately. The inherited property from her maternal relatives was identified. Her commercial investments had been recorded according to the partnership agreements. The loans to the household were listed with dates and available supporting documents.

The figures were not final.

But they were beginning to form a complete picture.

Mingzhao picked up her brush.

At the bottom of the page, she wrote:

*All valuations subject to independent confirmation.*

She looked at the sentence.

Then she added:

*No family estate property included.*

Qiao Yue read it.

"Young Miss, why write that?"

"Because I want the settlement to be clear."

"Will they still accuse you of taking too much?"

"Probably."

"Then why be so careful?"

Mingzhao looked at her.

"Because I do not intend to become what they accuse me of being."

Qiao Yue fell silent.

Mingzhao returned to her papers.

She was not trying to prove that she was a good daughter.

She was trying to establish a life in which she did not have to ask permission to spend her own money, make her own decisions, or leave a task unfinished when it was not hers to complete.

That would require discipline.

It would also require money.

And, judging from the price of the eastern residence, rather more money than she had hoped.

She made a note to review her available funds again.

The independence she wanted would not be inexpensive.

But she preferred a costly freedom to a comfortable arrangement that belonged to someone else.

The following morning, the Grand Secretary would submit a revised report to the Emperor.

It would reveal that several departments had been using the Empress's office as a final approval authority without any written basis.

It would also reveal that some of those departments had never properly maintained their own records.

The Emperor would have to decide whether the palace needed a new administrative structure or merely the enforcement of existing rules.

At the Qingyuan Marquisate, Mingzhao's father would have to decide whether to accept an independent valuation or risk a more formal property dispute.

Neither decision would be easy.

And neither would be made in a single day.

For now, the Empress had a petition awaiting judgment.

The eldest daughter had an inventory awaiting verification.

The palace had ninety-four administrative responsibilities under review.

The Marquisate had forty-seven unexplained grain sacks.

And somewhere in the Department of Palace Stores, an official was still trying to determine whether the Empress had to approve the purchase of new cleaning tools.

The empire was vast.

Its problems were numerous.

But the most immediate question in the imperial household remained surprisingly small:

**Who, exactly, was responsible for buying the brooms?**

**End of Chapter 3**
