# **Chapter 124: The Former Empress Has Discovered That Preparing for a Family Visit Requires Fewer Decisions Than Preparing for a Palace Ceremony, Although Her Aunt's Chickens Have Introduced an Unexpected Question. The Marquis's Eldest Daughter Has Learned That a Merchant May Agree to Every Condition Except the One That Protects the Seller. The Marquis's Heir Has Finally Opened the Northern Depot's Receiving Register and Has Discovered That a Record of Arrival May Still Fail to Identify What Arrived.**

**28th Day of the Seventh Month, 18th Year of Yonghe.**

Xu Yuru had finished the branch.

It was not a particularly impressive branch.

The stem curved a little too sharply, the first leaf was narrower than the others, and the final leaf had a small knot where the thread had been pulled through twice. Nevertheless, the cloth lay flat, the stitches held, and the branch was recognisable without requiring an explanation.

Yuru held it at arm's length.

Fang Ru stood beside her.

"Finished?" she asked.

"Finished."

"Would you like me to inspect it?"

"No."

Fang Ru looked at the cloth.

"Then I shall not."

Yuru placed it on the table.

After a moment, she picked it up again.

"Actually, you may look."

Fang Ru examined the embroidery with appropriate seriousness.

"The leaves are much better."

"That is a generous assessment."

"The stem is a little uneven."

"I know."

"But it looks like a branch."

"That is the important part."

Fang Ru smiled.

Yuru folded the cloth and placed it inside the wooden box. She had not decided what to do with it. It might become a small cover for a cushion, or it might remain a piece of practice cloth. She did not feel compelled to assign it a purpose immediately.

The box was returned to the table.

"Your aunt's visit is in two days," Fang Ru said.

"Yes."

"Shall I prepare the carriage arrangements?"

"They are already confirmed."

"Would you like to take the tea?"

"Yes."

"Anything else?"

Yuru considered the question.

"Perhaps a little dried fruit."

Fang Ru nodded.

"Your aunt sent you dried plums."

"She did."

"Would you like to return some?"

"No. I would rather not begin an exchange of dried fruit that requires a register."

Fang Ru lowered her head to hide a smile.

Yuru opened the letter from her aunt once more. It had been placed beside her writing brush.

The final paragraph concerned the neighbour's chickens.

Her aunt had written that the neighbour had promised to repair the fence. She had also written that the chickens had continued entering the garden, apparently having taken the promise as a suggestion rather than an instruction.

Yuru read the paragraph aloud.

Fang Ru listened.

"Perhaps the fence has not yet been repaired."

"That is possible."

"Or perhaps the chickens have found another way in."

"That is also possible."

"Will your aunt ask you to speak to the neighbour?"

Yuru folded the letter.

"I hope not."

"Would you?"

"I would ask whether the fence had been repaired. If it had not, I would ask why. If it had, I would ask how the chickens were entering."

Fang Ru considered this.

"That sounds like a reasonable inquiry."

"It also sounds like the beginning of another afternoon."

Yuru placed the letter down.

"I shall be visiting as her niece, not as the keeper of her poultry."

Fang Ru smiled openly this time.

The morning passed without further preparations.

Yuru read several pages of her garden book, drank tea, and spent a little time looking through the correspondence she had received. There was a letter from a cousin who had recently begun managing a small household account. Yuru had already answered the first question in it and now found a second question about whether a servant's advance should be recorded as an expense or a debt.

She wrote a brief reply.

"It depends on whether the money is an advance against wages or a payment that does not need to be returned," she wrote. "The account should state which arrangement was agreed. Do not decide by the name someone gives the payment afterward."

She read the sentence again.

Then she added:

"If the arrangement was not made clear, record what is known and ask the person who authorized it."

She sealed the letter.

Fang Ru collected it with the outgoing correspondence.

"Would you like to read the next letter?" she asked.

"Later."

Yuru closed the writing box.

For the rest of the morning, she did not open it again.

At the Shen workshop, Guo Jian's reply arrived shortly after the midday meal.

The merchant had considered Mingzhao's final terms.

He agreed to retain a reference sample and compare the finished cloth against it. He also agreed that the sample would be kept by both parties until the order was completed.

He still refused to define the permitted difference in shade.

Instead, he wrote that the cloth would be accepted if it remained consistent with the ordinary quality of the sample.

Mingzhao read the letter twice.

Sun Qiao waited beside the table.

"Is he accepting?" he asked.

"He is accepting the arrangement that allows him to decide whether he has accepted."

Sun Qiao looked at the letter.

"He has agreed to keep the sample."

"Yes."

"And to compare the cloth against it."

"Yes."

"But he has not agreed to a measurable difference."

"Correct."

Sun Qiao hesitated.

"Could we make a second sample and have him approve it?"

"We could. But he has already said that the colour need not be exact."

"Perhaps he means a small difference."

"Perhaps."

Mingzhao set the letter down.

"How small?"

Sun Qiao did not answer.

"How would we measure it? Under what light? Against which part of the sample? Would he compare it in the warehouse or after it had been stored for several days?"

The foreman considered the questions.

"We would need to agree on those things."

"Exactly."

Mingzhao took up her brush.

She wrote a final response. The workshop appreciated the revised proposal but could not accept the order without a defined inspection standard. If Guo Jian wished to proceed, he could send a representative to examine the sample and agree to a written range of acceptable variation. Otherwise, the workshop would not undertake the work.

She signed the letter.

Sun Qiao took it.

"Should I remove the twenty bolts from the expected work list?"

"They were never on the production list."

"I meant the inquiry list."

"Keep it under pending correspondence until he answers. Do not enter it as expected income."

Sun Qiao nodded.

Mingzhao looked at the workshop accounts.

The twenty-bolt proposal had been promising. It had a named merchant, a sample, a price, a delivery point, and a deposit proposal.

It still lacked one condition that mattered.

She had no intention of allowing the number of favourable details to conceal the missing one.

"Has the supplier sent the revised material price for the six-bolt trial?" she asked.

"Not yet."

"And the measurable colour range?"

"Not yet."

"Then that file remains unchanged."

Sun Qiao nodded.

He had learned that Mingzhao's silence did not mean that a proposal had been approved.

Sometimes it meant she was waiting for someone else to finish writing.

In the northern records section, Mingyuan stood beside a long examination table.

The temporary receiving register had finally been brought out.

Its cover was worn, and one corner had been repaired with a strip of darker paper. The title identified it as a receiving book for the northern depot. The date range included the relevant month.

Liang Wen had placed the register beside the Northern Road Movement Supplement.

Steward Zhou De stood at the other end of the table.

A records clerk opened the receiving book to the appropriate section.

"These are the entries for the relevant week," he said.

Mingyuan leaned forward.

The pages contained several deliveries: timber, iron tools, oil, household goods, and sacks of grain. Each entry had a date, a description, a quantity, and a space for the receiving clerk's acknowledgment.

Some entries were complete.

Others contained corrections.

Mingyuan asked the clerk to turn back two pages.

"Why?"

"To see how the register was used before the dispatch date."

The clerk turned the pages.

Mingyuan examined the entries.

The receiving clerk had generally recorded the cargo, the number of carts, and the name of the person delivering the goods. When the cargo was weighed separately, the quantity was entered in a later column. Several deliveries had a second signature from a storehouse official.

"Now the following pages," Mingyuan said.

The clerk turned them.

The same pattern continued.

Mingyuan pointed to an entry.

"This delivery has no cart number."

"The carts were recorded in the transport register," Liang Wen said. "The receiving book records the quantity and the delivering party."

"Then the two books were not always intended to repeat the same details."

"That appears to be the case."

Mingyuan looked at the relevant date.

There were several entries.

One concerned a delivery of timber.

Another recorded iron fittings.

A third listed household supplies.

None contained the four cart numbers from the movement register.

He checked the dates immediately before and after.

There was no obvious matching entry.

"Could the four carts have been received on the following day?" he asked.

"Possibly," Liang Wen replied.

"Then check the next day."

The clerk turned the page.

The following day's entries included grain, timber, and two deliveries of oil.

Again, there was no matching cart number.

Mingyuan examined the grain entry.

The quantity was recorded separately, with the name of the receiving storehouse clerk and a second acknowledgment.

It did not match the four-cart dispatch in any identifiable way.

He set the page down.

"Do not connect this grain entry to the missing sacks."

Liang Wen nodded.

"The quantity is different, and there is no reference to the four carts."

"Good."

Mingyuan looked at the register again.

"Could the receiving book have been incomplete?"

The records clerk answered carefully.

"It is possible. Some entries were recorded in separate sheets when the depot was busy. Those sheets were sometimes attached later."

"Are any such sheets listed?"

"Not in this register. There are references to attached lists on several pages, but the lists are not all present."

Mingyuan's attention sharpened.

"Which pages?"

The clerk indicated three entries.

One concerned a large timber delivery.

Another concerned several loads of iron fittings.

The third was dated close to the four-cart dispatch.

Mingyuan pointed to it.

"What does this entry say?"

The clerk read the faded characters.

"It records a delivery awaiting confirmation of its contents. The receiving clerk wrote that a separate list was expected."

"Is there a quantity?"

"No."

"A cart number?"

"No."

"A name?"

"The name is partly obscured."

Mingyuan studied the entry.

It was not the four-cart dispatch.

The date was close, but not identical. The description was incomplete. There was no cart number to connect it with the movement register.

"Record it as a separate incomplete entry," he said.

The clerk bowed.

Mingyuan continued examining the pages.

After several minutes, he reached the end of the section.

There was no matching receipt.

The northern depot's receiving book had been found, but it did not confirm the arrival of the four carts.

He looked at Liang Wen.

"Does the register show who was responsible for receiving goods when the regular clerk was absent?"

Liang Wen turned to the preceding pages.

"There are two names. The register does not state whether they were both present on the relevant day."

"Can we find their duty roster?"

"We can ask the collection clerks to search the depot's personnel records."

"Do so."

Zhou De spoke.

"Young master, there is another possibility. The carts may have been received at a temporary unloading point rather than the main depot."

Mingyuan looked at him.

"Is there a record of such a place?"

"Not in the documents we have examined. But the northern road had several unloading yards."

"Then we need evidence that this particular dispatch was directed to one of them."

"Yes."

Mingyuan looked back at the movement register.

The destination was incomplete.

The cargo column was blank.

The receiving book had no matching entry.

The inquiry had not established where the carts went.

He closed the register carefully.

"Make a copy of the relevant receiving pages. Record that no matching entry was found in this book. Do not write that the carts failed to arrive."

Liang Wen repeated the instruction.

Mingyuan looked at the records clerk.

"Have the remaining temporary receiving bundles examined. There may be another book for a separate unloading yard."

The clerk bowed.

Mingyuan gathered the reports.

He had found the receiving register he had asked for.

It had not answered the question.

Still, he now knew something important: the main northern depot's receiving book did not contain a clear acknowledgment of the four carts.

That was not proof of what had happened.

It was proof of what the book did not record.

He had begun to appreciate the difference.

At the Marquisate, Shen Mingxiu brought the revised maintenance estimate to her mother.

The second carpenter had returned with a fuller price.

The cost was higher than the original estimate but still lower than the first carpenter's proposal.

Mingxiu had also asked whether the damaged timber could be inspected before replacement.

The carpenter agreed.

Madam Liang examined the revised document.

"This is acceptable."

"Then I will arrange the inspection."

"Can the work be completed before the next rain?"

"The carpenter says the temporary covering will protect the roof while the replacement tiles are prepared."

Madam Liang nodded.

"Then proceed."

Mingxiu took the document.

She hesitated before leaving.

"Mother."

"What is it?"

"Should I ask Father to approve the expense?"

Madam Liang considered the matter.

"Is it within the maintenance allowance?"

"Yes."

"Then follow the household procedure. The steward must confirm the work, and the accounts office must record the payment."

Mingxiu nodded.

She had once thought that the most difficult part of managing the estate was knowing what needed to be done.

Now she was discovering that knowing was only the beginning.

The work required an estimate, approval, a responsible person, inspection, and a record of payment.

Her elder sister had carried all of those things in her head for years.

Mingxiu returned to the accounts room and placed the estimate in the proper tray.

She did not ask anyone to praise her for doing so.

She was beginning to understand that the task itself was sufficient.

Yuru received a letter from her father in the late afternoon.

He asked whether she had finished the first of the books he had lent her and whether she wanted another.

She smiled at the second question.

Fang Ru brought the letter to her writing table.

"Would you like me to prepare a reply?"

"No. I shall write it."

Yuru took up her brush.

She told her father that she had finished the first book, was still reading the second, and had not yet completed the garden book. She thanked him for offering another, but said she would prefer to finish what she already had.

She paused, then added that she would visit her aunt in two days and expected to return before evening.

When she had finished, she read the letter.

It was short.

She sealed it.

Fang Ru placed it with the outgoing correspondence.

"Your father may still send another book," she said.

"He may."

"Would you accept it?"

"If he sends it, I shall thank him."

"Even if you do not want it?"

"I can thank him without borrowing it."

Fang Ru nodded.

Yuru glanced at the embroidery box.

"Would you like to practise the leaves again?" she asked.

Fang Ru looked surprised.

"Me?"

"You have been practising on the spare cloth."

"Only a little."

"Then you may continue."

Fang Ru brought out her practice piece. Her leaves were smaller than Yuru's, and the stitches were more regular.

Yuru examined them.

"You have improved."

Fang Ru looked pleased.

"I practised yesterday."

"Did you enjoy it?"

"Yes."

"Then keep practising."

Fang Ru sat down with her own cloth.

They worked quietly for a while.

After some time, Fang Ru said, "I think I would like to keep the tea jar you gave me."

"The one with the fitted lid?"

"Yes. It is useful for my tea."

"Then keep it."

"I shall not need another."

Yuru smiled.

"That is fortunate. I have no intention of starting a collection of tea jars."

Fang Ru laughed softly.

The light outside began to fade.

Yuru put away her needle.

She had finished the branch. Fang Ru had made several new leaves. Her father's letter had been answered, and her aunt's tea had been prepared.

The visit was two days away.

For once, the preparations were almost complete before the evening.

At the workshop, Mingzhao reviewed the day's accounts.

The correspondence clerk had recorded Guo Jian's latest offer as pending a final inspection arrangement. No production entry had been made.

The six-bolt trial remained on hold.

The workshop's cash balance was unchanged.

Sun Qiao brought her a note concerning the workers.

"Two of the younger workers have been asked to join another workshop for a short period," he said.

"Have they accepted?"

"Not yet."

"Do they wish to?"

"One does. The other is uncertain."

"Then they may decide."

Sun Qiao looked at her.

"Should I tell them that work may become available here?"

"You may tell them that we are considering proposals. Do not promise a date or a quantity."

"Yes."

"Have their wages been paid?"

"All wages due have been paid."

"Then there is no reason to hold them."

Sun Qiao nodded.

After he left, Mingzhao examined the workshop's reserve ledger.

The completed orders had produced a modest surplus. The money was not enough to expand carelessly, but it was enough to maintain the workshop while she considered the next step.

She had once been responsible for an estate where every idle room, empty storehouse, and unused piece of land was treated as a failure that needed immediate correction.

The workshop was smaller.

Its problems were also smaller.

She preferred that.

She closed the ledger and placed it in the cabinet.

That evening, Mingyuan received another report from the records collection.

The clerks had found a second temporary receiving book.

It belonged to a smaller northern unloading yard.

Its date range was incomplete, and several pages were missing. The surviving section covered part of the month in which the four carts had been dispatched.

The report did not say whether the cart numbers appeared in it.

Mingyuan read the message.

"Have they examined the relevant pages?" he asked.

"Not yet," Zhou replied. "They have only confirmed the book's title and date range."

"Then have them examine it tomorrow."

Zhou nodded.

Mingyuan looked at the files spread across his table.

The main depot register had not recorded the four carts.

The general freight register had not recorded them.

The loose sheets had not identified them.

Now another receiving book had appeared, one that might have been used by a smaller unloading yard.

He felt the familiar temptation to assume that this must be the answer.

He resisted it.

"Tell the clerks to copy the preceding and following pages as well," he said. "And if any entry appears to match, they must record the basis for the comparison."

Zhou wrote it down.

Mingyuan leaned back.

"Also, ask whether the second yard's duty roster survives."

"Yes."

"And whether the yard had its own transport office or received instructions from the main depot."

"That may require a separate inquiry."

"Then make one."

Zhou bowed.

Mingyuan looked toward the closed cabinet where the property schedule was kept.

The family division remained unfinished.

His sister's claims had not been settled. The disputed payments remained unresolved. The four-cart inquiry had produced more records but no final explanation.

He could not force any of these matters to end simply because the household wished to have them finished.

He took up his brush and wrote the next day's instructions.

For the moment, the only useful conclusion was that the search had reached another receiving book.

Whether that book would prove helpful remained to be seen.

The records collection had now supplied two registers, several loose sheets, and a number of references to papers that might once have been attached to other papers.

Mingyuan was beginning to suspect that the old transport office had been very fond of attachments.

It had apparently been less fond of keeping them together.
