# **Chapter 112: The Former Empress Has Discovered That a Letter May Be Enjoyable Even When It Contains No Request for Assistance. The Marquis's Eldest Daughter Has Authorized Six Bolts of Trial Production and Has Discovered That an Authorization Is Not the Same as an Order from a Buyer. The Marquis's Heir Has Found Another Record of an Old Book and Has Learned That Knowing Where a Book Was Sent Is Not the Same as Knowing Where It Went.**

**16th Day of the Seventh Month, 18th Year of Yonghe.**

The former Empress received three letters that morning.

None contained a request for assistance.

This was unusual enough that Xu Yuru read all three before breakfast.

The first came from her elder cousin, who lived in the eastern part of Yongjing. It concerned a household matter so ordinary that Yuru had to read the final paragraph twice to determine whether there was any hidden request concealed beneath the polite greetings.

There was not.

Her cousin had written to say that a niece had begun learning calligraphy, that the household's old inkstone had been repaired, and that the repairman had charged more than expected.

The second letter came from an aunt in the Xu family. It contained a short account of a neighbour's new residence, a complaint about the price of good tea, and an invitation to visit whenever Yuru wished.

The third was from a woman she had known before entering the palace.

It was the shortest.

*I heard that you have been reading about gardens. I hope the book is useful. I have recently discovered that the small courtyard behind my study receives more sunlight than I remembered. I am considering planting something there, although I have not yet decided what.*

Yuru placed the three letters beside one another.

Fang Ru, who had been arranging the morning tea, glanced at them.

"Are they important?"

"Not particularly."

"Then why are you smiling?"

Yuru looked at her.

"Must a letter be important before one may enjoy reading it?"

Fang Ru considered this.

"No, Madam."

"Then I have answered your question."

Fang Ru poured the tea.

The letters remained on the table. Yuru had no reason to send immediate replies. Her cousin had not asked her to judge the repairman's charge. Her aunt had not asked her to arrange a visit with the neighbour. Her old acquaintance had not asked her to select a plant.

She could answer each in her own time.

This simple freedom still required occasional practice.

For many years, a letter arriving at the palace had usually carried something that needed a decision. Even private correspondence had often become entangled with family obligations, ceremonial arrangements, requests from relatives, or matters that someone believed could not be handled without her.

Now she could read a letter and leave it on the table.

She could also answer it without first determining what other people expected her to do.

Yuru took another sip of tea.

"Fang Ru, prepare writing paper for me after breakfast."

"Which letter shall I answer first?"

"The one about the courtyard."

Fang Ru nodded.

"Shall I have the garden book brought here?"

"No. I know how to write a letter without consulting a book."

Fang Ru lowered her eyes, but the corners of her mouth moved.

Yuru noticed.

"Do you find that amusing?"

"Only that you have said it before I could ask."

"Then you are becoming more efficient."

Fang Ru accepted the remark without replying.

Outside, the gardener was speaking with one of the attendants about a delivery of fresh soil. Yuru heard the conversation through the open window, but did not turn toward it.

She had a letter to write.

That was enough for the morning.

In the Marquisate of Qingyuan, Shen Mingyuan had discovered that an old record could be more troublesome than a missing one.

The previous afternoon, Steward Zhou had brought him the transfer list from the records reorganization.

The list confirmed that the northern dispatch book and the transport movement records had been moved into the records collection.

It did not state the final storage location of the northern dispatch book.

The movement records had later been transferred to the old accounts storehouse, but the transfer list did not establish that every northern dispatch page had remained with them.

The distinction had appeared simple when Mingyuan first read it.

It had become less simple after he asked the clerks to explain what they had actually received.

"Bring the transfer list again," he instructed Liang Wen.

Liang Wen placed it on the desk.

Mingyuan read the relevant entry.

"Here. Northern dispatch book and movement records."

"Yes, Young Master."

"Does it say they were placed in the old accounts storehouse?"

"It says the movement records were transferred there during the later reorganization. It does not separately identify the northern dispatch book."

"Then why did we begin searching the old accounts storehouse for both?"

"Because the two categories were collected together before the reorganization."

Mingyuan looked at him.

"Collected together is not the same as stored together."

"No, Young Master."

"Then write that distinction in the inquiry record."

Liang Wen took out his brush.

Mingyuan continued reading.

"How many bundles were listed in the transfer?"

"Three bundles of movement records and one bundle of northern dispatch records."

"Was the bundle number copied into the later storage register?"

"Only the movement-record bundle numbers."

"Then we have a missing link between the original transfer and the later storage entry."

Liang Wen nodded.

"Yes."

Mingyuan set down the paper.

He had once thought that investigating an old account meant finding a person who remembered what had happened. He had since learned that a person might remember a conversation, a book, a payment, or an instruction without remembering the detail that mattered.

The records were no better.

A register could confirm that a book had existed. A transfer list could confirm that it had been moved. Neither could prove that the particular page they needed had survived the journey.

"Ask the old accounts storehouse keeper to check the bundle numbers already recorded," Mingyuan said. "Do not ask him to search every shelf again. We have already searched the shelves."

"Should I also ask the records office whether the northern dispatch book was separated?"

"Yes. Ask whether there is a later receipt, a shelf entry, or a record of its transfer. Do not ask whether anyone remembers seeing it unless the written inquiry has been answered first."

Liang Wen wrote this down.

Mingyuan leaned back.

"And do not tell them we are looking for the four-cart entry."

Liang Wen paused.

"Why not?"

"Because if they know what we want to find, they may begin telling us what they think we wish to hear."

Liang Wen considered the answer and nodded.

Mingyuan did not look especially pleased with himself. He had learned the principle from the trouble caused by earlier inquiries, and he did not intend to pretend that learning it made him an expert.

"Send the inquiry today."

"Yes, Young Master."

When Liang Wen had finished, Mingyuan asked for the revised grain discrepancy schedule.

The figures remained unchanged.

Forty-seven original.

Twenty instructed.

Eighteen received.

Two unconfirmed.

Twenty-seven unexplained.

The four-cart dispatch remained a separate matter. The old purchasing voucher remained missing. The possible merchants in the purchasing summary remained possible references rather than established recipients.

Mingyuan read the headings once more.

He had begun to understand that a neat page could contain several unanswered questions without becoming a useless page.

It was less satisfying than finding an answer.

It was also more honest.

At the eastern market, Shen Mingzhao was considering six bolts of cloth.

The six bolts did not yet exist.

They were only an authorized trial, dependent upon confirmation of the material price and the approved sample.

The distinction had been written twice in the workshop's production book, partly because Mingzhao wanted the foreman to understand it and partly because she wanted herself to remember that she had not yet committed money to materials.

The foreman, Luo Shun, arrived shortly after the morning accounts were checked.

He carried a small paper packet.

"Miss Shen, the supplier has sent the material quotation."

Mingzhao opened it.

The quoted price was close to the amount discussed the previous day. The supplier had also specified the minimum quantity he would sell at that rate.

"Is this price guaranteed?"

"For five days, according to the note."

"And the material quality?"

"The same grade as the sample we examined. He has written the source and the thread count."

"Has he supplied a sample from the actual batch?"

"No. He says he can send one this afternoon."

"Then we do not buy the material yet."

Luo Shun nodded.

"I expected that."

Mingzhao looked up.

"You expected me to refuse?"

"I expected you to ask whether the sample came from the actual batch."

"That is not a refusal."

"No, Miss."

"Then do not describe it as one."

The foreman bowed his head slightly.

Mingzhao returned to the quotation.

The merchant estimates had suggested a possible demand of four to six bolts for one familiar specification, and six to eight for another specification if the colour matched an earlier sample.

Neither estimate was a purchase order.

The six-bolt trial was therefore intended to test production quality and provide something a prospective buyer could inspect. It was not an excuse to manufacture a larger quantity in the hope that someone would eventually pay for it.

"How many workers would be needed?" she asked.

"Two experienced workers can supervise the trial. We would need one additional worker for preparation and another for finishing."

"Will that interfere with the other workshop's existing work?"

"No. The work can be arranged separately."

"Can it be completed without borrowing workers from the maintenance group?"

"Yes."

Mingzhao put down the quotation.

"Then prepare the proposed work schedule. Keep the trial separate from the ordinary production accounts. No materials are to be issued until the batch sample and price are confirmed."

"Understood."

"And if the supplier's actual sample differs from the one we examined?"

"We return it."

"If the price changes?"

"I bring the revised quotation to you."

"If the merchant who expressed interest decides not to inspect the finished bolts?"

Luo Shun hesitated.

"Then we will have six bolts without a confirmed buyer."

"Correct."

He looked at her.

"Would you still authorize the trial?"

"That is why it is limited to six."

The foreman nodded.

Mingzhao took up her brush and wrote the conditions in the production book.

She had no intention of turning a small experiment into an obligation to maintain a larger workshop than the orders justified.

The workers had been told that no further order was promised. Their existing agreements would be honoured, but she would not invent work merely to make the workshop appear busy.

When she finished writing, she handed the page to Luo Shun.

"Read it."

He read the entire entry.

"Six bolts only. Materials after batch sample and price confirmation. Separate production account. No additional quantity without a new authorization."

"Good."

He returned the paper.

"Miss Shen, should I tell the workers to prepare the looms?"

"Tell them the proposed schedule is being prepared. Do not tell them production has begun."

"Yes."

He left with the quotation and the written conditions.

Mingzhao remained at the table.

On another sheet lay the latest workshop account. The eighty-bolt order from Gao Wen had been completed, inspected, accepted, and paid. The remaining balance had been received. Wages, suppliers, and the carrier had all been paid. The final account showed a modest positive profit.

There was no reason to reopen it.

The previous two hundred-bolt order was also closed. The buyer had received the full quantity, and the final account had been settled.

Those completed orders gave Mingzhao confidence in the workshop's ability to fulfil an agreed specification.

They did not give her confidence in a buyer who had not yet agreed to buy.

She folded the supplier's quotation and placed it in the pending file.

Then she turned to the household property schedule.

There were still too many unresolved entries.

The family had improved the classifications. The furnishing expenditure had been correctly restored to the estate's expenditure account. The confirmed advances, uncertain advances, and disputed entries remained separate.

The seventy-two taels for study repairs still awaited a decision about reimbursement.

The two hundred and twenty-eight taels remained unverified.

The four-cart dispatch had not been connected to the unexplained grain.

The old purchasing voucher had not been found.

Mingzhao closed the file.

She had two kinds of work before her.

One could be completed by making a good product and selling it under clear terms.

The other depended on people who had spent years believing that a vague memory was sufficient evidence.

She preferred the first.

Unfortunately, preference did not settle the second.

At the palace, Xu Yuru had finished writing her reply.

She had answered her acquaintance's letter without suggesting a plant, arranging a gardener, or recommending a particular courtyard design.

Instead, she had written that sunlight was a useful beginning, that a small space did not require an elaborate plan, and that the pleasure of choosing what to plant might be greater than the pleasure of receiving a finished garden.

She had then added that she hoped the courtyard would become a pleasant place to sit.

Fang Ru read the address before sealing the letter.

"Shall I send it with the afternoon post?"

"Yes."

Fang Ru placed it in the dispatch tray.

Yuru turned to the other two letters.

Her aunt's invitation could be answered later. Her cousin's account of the repaired inkstone required no immediate reply.

She took up the garden book.

The chapter she had reached concerned the placement of small seats and the use of covered spaces during rain. It was not a subject she had previously considered important.

She read a few pages, then paused at a description of a simple wooden bench placed beneath a roofed passage.

The author recommended that it be positioned where a person could sit without obstructing the movement of servants carrying water or garden tools.

Yuru smiled faintly.

It was practical advice.

She turned the page.

A knock came at the door.

Fang Ru went to answer it and returned with a palace attendant carrying a small parcel.

"Madam, this was delivered from the Dowager's residence."

Yuru set down the book.

The parcel was wrapped in plain paper and tied with a narrow cord. There was no elaborate seal.

She opened it.

Inside lay a small embroidered purse containing several pieces of fragrant wood.

A note accompanied it.

The Dowager had written that the scent was mild and suitable for keeping near a writing table. She had received more than she needed and thought Yuru might enjoy it.

There was no request to visit.

There was no reference to the imperial household.

Yuru read the note twice.

"Did the attendant say anything?"

"He said only that Her Majesty the Dowager sent it."

Yuru looked at the purse.

"Then send my thanks."

Fang Ru waited.

"Shall I prepare a return gift?"

"No. I will send a note first."

"Yes, Madam."

Yuru placed the fragrant wood beside her writing materials.

For a moment, she considered whether accepting a gift might create an obligation. It was an old habit to examine every gesture for its possible consequences.

But the Dowager had sent something pleasant, not a duty.

Yuru did not need to turn it into a complicated matter.

She wrote a short note of thanks.

When she finished, she returned to the book.

The fragrant wood rested beside the inkstone, and its scent gradually spread through the room.

Fang Ru opened the window a little wider.

"Is the fragrance too strong?"

"No."

"Would you like it moved?"

Yuru glanced at the purse.

"Leave it there."

Fang Ru returned to her work.

The former Empress continued reading.

In the Marquisate, Mingyuan's inquiry to the old accounts storehouse produced an answer before noon.

The storehouse keeper reported that the movement-record bundles were still present under their later register numbers.

The northern dispatch book was not among them.

He had checked the three recorded bundles and compared their contents with the transfer list. None contained the separate book.

He also reported that the old storehouse had received several boxes of loose papers during the same reorganization, but those boxes had not been entered under the northern dispatch category.

Mingyuan read the reply.

"Did he list the box numbers?"

Liang Wen checked the attached sheet.

"Yes. There are six."

"Are the boxes accessible?"

"The keeper says they are in the rear records room. They have not been opened since the reorganization."

"Why?"

"The contents were described as miscellaneous papers. No one requested them."

Mingyuan looked at the sheet again.

Six boxes.

It was not an answer, but it was a place to look.

"Ask Steward Zhou to arrange a supervised examination. Two clerks should record the contents as each bundle is opened. Nothing is to be removed without an entry."

Liang Wen nodded.

"Should the former transport supervisors attend?"

"No. We do not need their memories to identify paper bundles. First establish what is there."

"Understood."

Mingyuan tapped the transfer list.

"Add that the northern dispatch book was not found among the three recorded movement-record bundles."

Liang Wen wrote it down.

"Then the book may be in one of the six boxes?"

"It may be."

"Or it may have been lost."

"Yes."

Liang Wen waited for a further instruction.

Mingyuan gave none.

He was learning to let an unanswered possibility remain unanswered until evidence changed it.

The habit did not make the investigation faster.

It did make the records more difficult to misuse.

When Liang Wen had left, Mingyuan went to the outer room, where his younger sister Shen Mingxiu was examining a small group of maintenance estimates.

She had asked Steward Zhou to provide the estimates because she wanted to understand how ordinary repairs were priced. The request had been hers, not something Mingyuan had assigned to her.

She looked up when he entered.

"Did you find the dispatch book?"

"No."

"Then why do you look less annoyed than yesterday?"

"We found six boxes that have not been examined."

"That sounds like more work."

"It is."

"Then I do not understand why you are pleased."

Mingyuan considered.

"We now know where to search next."

Mingxiu looked down at her estimates.

"That is a very modest reason to be pleased."

"It is still a reason."

She gave him a thoughtful look.

"Does Father know?"

"He will receive the written report."

"Will he ask whether the four carts carried grain?"

"He may."

"And what will you tell him?"

"That we do not know."

Mingxiu nodded.

"That answer will disappoint him."

"It is not my duty to make an answer more pleasing than the evidence allows."

She looked at him for another moment.

"You sound like Steward Zhou."

Mingyuan frowned.

"Is that a criticism?"

"Not necessarily."

She returned to the estimates.

Mingyuan glanced at the paper.

"What are you checking?"

"The difference between the cost of repairing a door and replacing it."

"And?"

"The replacement estimate is higher, but the carpenter says the existing frame is warped. If we repair the old door now, we may have to replace it before winter."

"Have you asked whether the carpenter is the only one who can do the work?"

"No. I asked for a second estimate."

Mingyuan looked at her more carefully.

"Good."

Mingxiu raised her eyes.

"I know."

He almost smiled.

She returned to her work, and he went back to the accounts room.

The household was not becoming efficient all at once.

It was beginning to ask more questions before spending money.

For the moment, that was progress enough.

At the temporary residence near the eastern market, Mingzhao received a short message from the workshop before the afternoon meal.

The supplier would deliver the actual batch sample the following day.

The price remained valid for five days.

The merchant who had expressed interest in inspecting the six-bolt trial had not yet confirmed a visit.

Mingzhao read the message and placed it in the pending file.

Qiao Yue entered with a small tray.

"Miss, the household accounts have been checked."

"Anything unusual?"

"No. The kitchen expenditure is within the amount set aside. The lower room has remained dry since the last rain."

"Good."

"There is also a letter from the Marquisate."

Mingzhao looked up.

"Who sent it?"

"It bears the steward's seal. The message says that the records office has found six boxes of miscellaneous papers from the reorganization. They intend to examine them tomorrow."

"Does it say whether the northern dispatch book is among them?"

"No."

"Then there is nothing for me to answer."

Qiao Yue placed the letter on the table.

"Would you like me to keep it with the other property papers?"

"Yes."

Qiao Yue hesitated.

"The Marquisate may ask you to attend once they have examined the boxes."

"They may."

"Will you go?"

"If they send a clear agenda and the relevant documents, I will consider it."

"And if they do not?"

"Then I will not."

Qiao Yue accepted this answer.

Mingzhao returned to her own papers.

After a moment, Qiao Yue asked, "Would you like the carriage prepared for tomorrow?"

"For what?"

"The workshop. You may wish to inspect the supplier's sample."

"I will go after it arrives. There is no reason to prepare the carriage before we know when it will be delivered."

Qiao Yue nodded.

Mingzhao looked at the pending file.

The next day's work depended on a supplier sending a sample, a merchant deciding whether to inspect a small trial, and a records clerk opening old boxes.

None of these events could be made certain by her impatience.

She had learned, at considerable expense, that being responsible for a result did not mean that every part of the result could be controlled.

She placed the workshop papers in one stack and the Marquisate's letter in another.

The two stacks remained separate.

That, at least, was entirely within her power.

And for once, she intended to keep it that way.
