# **Chapter 90: The Former Empress Has Discovered That a Provincial Granary Can Be Mismanaged by People Who Never Visit It, While a Household Roof Can Be Perfectly Inspected by Someone Who Has No Intention of Climbing It. The Marquis's Eldest Daughter Has Received a Workshop Capacity Report and Has Discovered That Eighty Bolts Are Not Difficult Until Someone Promises to Deliver Them Too Soon. The Marquis's Heir Has Found Another Old Record and Has Learned That Finding a Document Is Not the Same as Finding an Answer.**

**24th Day of the Sixth Month, 18th Year of Yonghe.**

Xu Yuru began the morning with the book on provincial granaries.

She had expected it to be a dry account of storage procedures, and in some respects it was. The author discussed the construction of storehouses, the thickness of walls, the placement of ventilation openings, the prevention of dampness, and the inspection of grain before it was accepted.

Yet the most useful passages concerned the people responsible for those tasks.

The author distinguished between the official who authorized a grain purchase, the clerk who recorded it, the steward who received it, and the officer who inspected the storehouse.

He complained that in poorly managed districts, one person sometimes performed several of these duties because the local administration considered separate appointments an unnecessary expense.

Yuru read the passage with interest.

The author did not argue that every small office required a large staff. He argued that when one person performed several functions, the records should still show which action had been taken in which capacity.

That was a sensible distinction.

She turned the page.

A later passage described a county where the same official had approved the purchase, certified the delivery, and authorized payment. When a shortage was discovered, no one could establish whether the grain had never arrived, had deteriorated in storage, or had been distributed without a proper entry.

Yuru put down her tea.

"That is a familiar difficulty."

Fang Ru, who was arranging the morning's correspondence, glanced at the page.

"Have you encountered such a case before?"

"More than one."

"Did the officials resolve it?"

"Some did. Others produced three additional registers."

Fang Ru smiled.

Yuru continued reading.

The author recommended that a small office preserve distinct signatures even when it could not afford separate personnel. A person might perform two tasks, but the records should not pretend that the tasks were one.

Yuru marked the paragraph.

She thought of the years when she had reviewed palace expenditure. In the beginning, she had corrected individual mistakes. Later, people had begun bringing her problems before they had attempted to resolve them. Eventually, her attention had become part of the process itself.

She had not ordered that arrangement.

She had helped create it by repeatedly accepting the work.

That did not make her solely responsible for the palace's habits. Nor did it mean that every correction she had made was wrong.

But she could now see the difference between helping a system function and becoming the part of the system that everyone expected to function.

Fang Ru placed a small paper slip beside her.

"Your borrowing record."

Yuru glanced at it.

The two books had been entered with their titles, lending household, date of receipt, and return period. The record showed three months.

"Good."

"Your father also sent a short note."

Yuru opened it.

The note contained no new invitation and no administrative question. Duke Xu had written that he hoped the books were useful and that he had selected another volume but would wait before sending it.

Yuru read the last line twice.

"He has been restrained."

"Madam Xu may have reminded him."

"That is likely."

She folded the note.

"Write to my mother. Tell her the granary book is interesting and that I have not yet finished the household management book."

Fang Ru prepared the writing materials.

"Shall I mention the passage about separate responsibilities?"

"Yes. My father may enjoy knowing that one of his books contains a useful argument."

"And the other?"

"Tell him I still disagree with the author about personally inspecting every roof."

Fang Ru lowered her eyes.

"Would you like that included?"

"Certainly. He selected the book."

The letter was written and sent before noon.

Yuru returned to her reading.

The roof inspector could wait.

Shen Mingzhao received the workshop report shortly after the morning meal.

It was more detailed than the first estimate she had requested.

Lin Shuyun had listed the remaining work, the workers available, the current commitments, and the time required for preparing the new sample.

The report did not claim that all eighty bolts could be produced immediately.

Instead, it proposed a sequence.

First, the dyer would prepare a small trial quantity using the sample as the reference. The senior weaver would compare the result against the merchant's material. If the colour and weave were consistent, production could begin.

The first group would be inspected before the rest of the order proceeded.

The two trainees could assist with preparation and simpler stages, but the report did not count them as independent finishers. The experienced worker expected to be absent for several days had been excluded from the initial schedule.

Mingzhao read the delivery estimate.

Then she read it again.

"How long does Lin Shuyun believe the work will take?"

Qiao Yue looked at the report.

"Twenty-four days after the sample is approved and the deposit is received."

"Does that include the buyer's inspection?"

"It says the inspection period is additional."

"Then the merchant's proposed delivery period is impossible."

"Unless we ask for a shorter inspection period."

"We may ask. We cannot assume he will agree."

Mingzhao drew a line beneath the proposed schedule.

The workshop had completed its previous order on time, but the last weeks had required careful coordination. The workers had learned a revised folding method, several corrections had been necessary, and the additional packing requirements had increased the transport cost.

The final account had shown a modest profit.

That profit had not come from pretending that every stage was effortless.

Mingzhao took out a fresh sheet.

"Prepare a reply. We can offer twenty-four days after approval of the trial sample and receipt of the deposit, with inspection completed within three days of the buyer receiving the goods."

Qiao Yue wrote it down.

"Should the delivery address be included?"

"Yes. The named warehouse must be confirmed before the contract is signed."

"And the correction period?"

"Five days after inspection for defects against the written specifications. No open-ended correction obligation."

Qiao Yue paused.

"Would the merchant accept that?"

"If he wants the goods, he may."

Mingzhao continued dictating.

"The colour standard must refer to the approved sample. Any permitted variation must be written down. If the buyer changes the requested colour after production begins, the additional cost and time must be agreed separately."

Qiao Yue finished the draft.

Mingzhao reviewed it.

"Send this to the agent. Make clear that these are proposed terms, not an accepted order."

"Understood."

Qiao Yue folded the letter.

"Do you think the workshop can manage eighty bolts?"

"Yes, if the trial succeeds and the schedule remains as reported."

"Then why not accept now?"

"Because the sample has not been tested, the warehouse has not been named, and the merchant has not accepted the terms."

Qiao Yue nodded.

Mingzhao returned the capacity report to its folder.

"An order is not a collection of favourable sentences. It is an agreement between people who must perform specific obligations."

Qiao Yue smiled.

"You have been saying similar things for several days."

"Then perhaps someone will eventually listen."

He Zhen received the proposed terms that afternoon.

He did not return immediately with an answer.

Mingzhao considered this reasonable. A merchant who agreed to every clause without consulting anyone would be either unusually careless or unusually dishonest.

She had no interest in discovering which.

Instead, she spent the afternoon reviewing the accounts from the completed two-hundred-bolt order with Sun Qiming.

The final account had been closed, but Sun had prepared a separate summary comparing the original production estimate with the actual expenditure.

"The revised folding method saved some working time," he explained. "However, the training expenses were higher than estimated. The inspection process also took longer because the new workers required more supervision."

"Did the additional packing affect the final margin?"

"Yes, though not enough to remove the profit."

"And transport?"

"The agreed transport cost was paid. The additional containers were recorded separately."

Mingzhao examined the summary.

"Would you use the same training arrangement again?"

"With adjustments."

"What adjustments?"

Sun pointed to a section of the report.

"The two trainees should not be assigned identical work merely because they began training together. One is more careful but slower. The other works quickly but requires more correction. Their tasks should reflect those differences until their performance becomes more consistent."

Mingzhao nodded.

"That is Lin Shuyun's conclusion as well."

"The workshop's observations agree with the account."

"Good."

Sun hesitated.

"Do you intend to accept Gao Wen's order?"

"I am considering it."

"The proposed deposit is substantial."

"It is."

"That reduces the financial risk."

"It reduces one kind of risk."

Sun looked at her.

Mingzhao tapped the specification sheet.

"It does not make an unclear delivery address clear. It does not make an impossible schedule possible. It does not tell us what the buyer will consider a defect."

Sun nodded.

"Then the contract should settle those matters before production begins."

"Exactly."

He gathered the completed accounts.

"At least this order has no remaining payment."

"That is because the buyer received the goods, signed the warehouse receipt, and paid the balance."

Sun gave a faint smile.

"An unusually satisfactory ending."

"Do not become accustomed to it."

He looked at her.

"Why?"

"Because merchants are capable of finding new ways to make a simple transaction difficult."

Sun laughed.

Mingzhao returned the summary to its folder.

The previous order was complete. Its profit was modest, its accounts were closed, and no worker was waiting for unpaid wages.

That was enough.

She did not need to accept another order merely to prove that the workshop could continue operating.

At the Marquisate, Shen Mingyuan received a report from the accounts office concerning an old transport register.

It was not the missing voucher.

The clerk had found a book recording the movement of several carts during the same season as the grain discrepancy. The entries contained dates, the number of carts, and the names of the drivers.

One page included a movement of four carts from the northern warehouse.

Mingyuan examined the copy.

"Does this identify the grain?"

"No, Young Master. The entry says only that four carts were dispatched. The cargo column is blank."

"Is there a destination?"

"The destination column is also blank."

"Who signed the entry?"

"The warehouse clerk. The driver's name is written below, but the handwriting is unclear."

Mingyuan leaned closer.

The name might have been Zhao Fusheng.

It might also have been Zhao Fushun.

He did not allow the clerk to choose between them.

"Make a certified copy. Preserve the original. Ask Zhang whether he recognizes the handwriting, but do not tell him what name we suspect."

The clerk bowed.

Mingyuan turned to Steward Zhou.

"This may be relevant."

"It may."

"But it does not establish that the carts carried the missing twenty-seven sacks."

"No."

"It does not establish that the driver was Zhao Fusheng."

"No."

"And it does not connect the transport payment to this movement."

Steward Zhou looked at him.

"You have learned to make three separate statements before breakfast."

Mingyuan set down the copy.

"I have learned that people become very confident when a record resembles the answer they want."

The steward did not disagree.

Mingyuan placed the document in the inquiry folder.

It was a useful lead, but not a conclusion.

For the first time, the grain inquiry had produced a new document rather than another recollection.

That was progress.

It was not proof.

The old purchasing payment remained equally difficult.

The accounts office had still not located the original voucher.

Mingyuan asked Liang Wen to prepare a list of every bundle examined so far, including the categories that had been searched and the dates of the search.

He did not want the clerks to search the same papers repeatedly while assuming that a missing document must be somewhere in the next bundle.

Liang Wen completed the list.

"Would you like me to send it to your sister?"

"No. She has already reviewed the surviving entry and answered what she can."

"Then she will not receive another request?"

"Not unless we find something that gives her a specific question to answer."

Liang Wen nodded.

Mingyuan reviewed the list.

The surviving entry still showed the estate purchasing seal.

The seal register still identified Chen Rui as its custodian on the date.

The original voucher remained missing.

The recipient and purpose remained unknown.

Mingyuan wrote a short instruction:

*The entry is to remain unresolved pending supporting evidence. No personal authorization or receipt is to be attributed to Shen Mingzhao solely from the surviving seal impression.*

He signed it.

Steward Zhou read the sentence.

"Your father may ask why you have written this so carefully."

"Then I will explain."

"He may say that the household cannot leave every old question unresolved."

"Then the household should find the evidence."

The steward looked at him for a long moment.

"You sound more like the accounts office than the heir."

Mingyuan returned the pen to its holder.

"Perhaps the accounts office has finally become useful."

At the western guest residence, Yuru received a short reply from her mother.

Madam Xu had written that she was pleased the granary book was useful. She also reported that Duke Xu had found another volume about provincial transport, but she had instructed him not to send it until Yuru had finished the two books already borrowed.

Yuru read the letter with amusement.

Her father had apparently accepted the restriction.

He had not accepted it enthusiastically.

Fang Ru looked at the letter.

"Would you like to reply?"

"Tomorrow."

"Shall I put it with the other correspondence?"

"Yes."

Yuru returned to the granary book.

The passage she had been reading concerned transport losses. The author warned that a record of dispatch should not be treated as proof of receipt at the destination.

She marked the paragraph.

Then she sat back.

It was a simple principle, but it had to be repeated because people repeatedly forgot it.

A cart leaving a warehouse did not prove that it reached the intended storehouse.

A signature on a purchase authorization did not prove that the goods had been received.

A person responsible for keeping a register was not automatically responsible for every action recorded in it.

Yuru closed the book.

She had no intention of sending these observations to the palace.

They belonged to the book, to her thoughts, and perhaps to a future conversation with her father.

They did not need to become instructions.

She placed the paper slip between the pages and stood.

"Would you like to walk before the evening meal?" Fang Ru asked.

"Yes."

"Along the western path?"

"That will do."

They left the room without taking the books.

The afternoon air was warm, and the path was quiet. Yuru walked at an unhurried pace, stopping once to look at a flowering shrub near the wall.

Fang Ru remained beside her.

After a while, Yuru said, "I used to think that if I stopped checking something, it would be neglected."

Fang Ru did not answer immediately.

"Some things were neglected," she said at last.

"Yes."

"And some were managed perfectly well without you."

"Yes."

Yuru looked ahead.

"It took me a long time to distinguish between them."

Fang Ru adjusted her pace to match hers.

They continued walking.

There was no report to prepare at the end of the path, no official waiting for a decision, and no need to turn the walk into an inspection.

When they returned, Yuru drank tea and resumed her reading.

The two books remained on the table.

The return period remained three months.

And the roof inspector, for another day, remained entirely imaginary.
