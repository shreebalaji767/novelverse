# **Chapter 67: The Former Empress Has Entered a New Month Without Receiving a Single Document. The Marquis's Eldest Daughter Has Entered a New Month Without Receiving a Single Settlement. The Workshop Has Entered a New Month with More Work Than Anyone Can Count Comfortably.**

**1st Day of the Sixth Month, 18th Year of Yonghe.**



The first day of a new month was generally considered an appropriate time to review accounts.



In the imperial palace, this meant that several officials arrived early with fresh registers, clean sheets of paper, and the determined expressions of people who believed that a new month should begin with everything properly arranged.



In the temporary guest residence in the western residential section, Xu Yuru began the day by opening her window.



The morning air was warm. A servant had swept the courtyard before sunrise, and the potted plants along the covered walkway had been watered.



Fang Ru placed breakfast on the table.



“Madam, the kitchen has sent porridge, steamed buns, and two small dishes.”



Yuru glanced at the tray.



“Three things?”



“Three.”



“Then the kitchen has successfully counted.”



Fang Ru smiled. “Would you like me to ask whether they intended to send anything else?”



“No. If they did, they can keep it.”



Fang Ru served the porridge.



Yuru sat down and began eating.



The guest residence had become more comfortable over the preceding weeks. The servants no longer appeared startled whenever she entered a room. The household accounts were no longer sent to her for informal approval. Madam Zhao had stopped asking whether she wished to inspect every minor arrangement.



The change was not complete.



Some people still paused before making decisions, as though waiting for an invisible instruction.



But they were beginning to proceed without it.



That morning, a young attendant approached Fang Ru with a small question about the courtyard's water jars.



Fang Ru listened, considered the matter, and answered.



The attendant bowed and left.



Yuru continued eating.



Fang Ru noticed her looking toward the doorway.



“Madam?”



“Nothing.”



“You were watching the attendant.”



“I was watching you answer.”



Fang Ru blinked.



“Was my answer incorrect?”



“No. You answered without asking me.”



Fang Ru looked briefly uncertain.



Yuru set down her spoon.



“That was praise.”



“Oh.”



Fang Ru's expression relaxed.



Yuru returned to breakfast.



For years, she had been surrounded by people who waited for her judgment. It had taken considerable effort to teach herself that not every question required her attention.



It was taking other people just as much effort to learn the same lesson.



After breakfast, she opened a letter from her mother.



It contained a few lines about the weather, a complaint that the fruit seller had raised his prices, and a note that the preserved vegetables sent to the guest residence had been prepared in two different jars.



The second jar, her mother explained, contained a milder variety.



Yuru read the letter twice.



Then she put it away.



There was no request attached.



No hidden question about residence arrangements.



No suggestion that she should return to the palace and take charge of something.



It was an ordinary letter.



She found herself appreciating that.

At the Imperial Household Administration, the new month's registers were being opened.



Madam Zhao stood beside the main table while two clerks compared the closing figures from the previous month with the opening figures for the sixth month.



One clerk read aloud.



“Remaining lamp oil: according to the final issue register, the amount should be—”



“Stop,” Madam Zhao said.



The clerk stopped.



“Which register?”



“The final issue register.”



“And does it record the remaining quantity or the quantity issued?”



The clerk looked down.



“The quantity issued.”



“Then it cannot establish the remaining quantity by itself.”



The clerk hesitated.



“The opening stock is in the warehouse register.”



“Then compare the two.”



A second clerk opened the warehouse book.



The figures did not match immediately.



He began turning pages.



Madam Zhao waited.



A few months earlier, someone would probably have carried the discrepancy to the former Empress, asking her to decide which record should be treated as authoritative.



Now the clerks were expected to identify the source of the difference.



The first clerk eventually found an additional issue entry.



It explained part of the discrepancy, but not all of it.



“Should we enter the remaining amount as the difference after that issue?” he asked.



Madam Zhao looked at the page.



“Only if the opening stock and every intervening issue are confirmed.”



“They are not all confirmed.”



“Then do not calculate a confirmed balance from unconfirmed entries.”



The clerk looked disappointed.



Madam Zhao did not soften her expression.



“An incomplete figure does not become complete because you write it neatly.”



The clerk lowered his head.



“Yes, Madam.”



Across the room, another official was preparing the monthly expenditure summary.



He had separated purchases, issues, and remaining stock into different columns.



The arrangement was more cumbersome than the old format.



It was also more useful.



Madam Zhao examined the headings.



“Keep this format.”



“Yes.”



“And retain the original descriptions in the attached sheets.”



“Yes.”



She returned the papers.



The former Empress had not instructed them to do this that morning.



That was precisely the point.

In the Eastern Palace, Crown Prince Zhao Cheng'an was facing a similar problem.



His new monthly register had been prepared with considerable care.



The clerk responsible for it stood beside the desk, waiting for approval.



The Crown Prince examined the first page.



“Why is this quantity listed as a total?”



“Because the previous month's remaining amount and this month's purchases have been added together.”



“Were the previous month's remaining amount confirmed?”



The clerk paused.



“Not entirely.”



“Then why is it included in the confirmed total?”



The clerk's expression became uncomfortable.



“I thought it would be useful to have a working figure.”



“A working figure may be useful. It must not be mistaken for an established figure.”



Cheng'an placed the register down.



“Put the confirmed purchases in one column. Put the unconfirmed opening balance in another. If you need a working estimate, show the calculation separately and label it as an estimate.”



“Yes, Your Highness.”



The clerk took the register back.



Cheng'an looked at the papers remaining on his desk.



The new format required more effort.



It also made it harder to conceal uncertainty beneath a convenient total.



He had begun to understand why his former stepmother had insisted on preserving unresolved entries.



It was not because she enjoyed making paperwork longer.



It was because the wrong answer could be more troublesome than no answer.



He called for the next file.



This time, he read the heading before opening it.

The Second Prince, Zhao Chengyu, had also received his monthly household allocation.



He examined the amount and frowned.



“Why is this lower than last month?”



The attendant standing beside him answered carefully.



“The previous month's request included additional provisions for the gathering.”



“The gathering has ended.”



“Yes, Your Highness.”



“Then the amount should be lower.”



“It is.”



Chengyu looked at him.



“Why do you sound surprised?”



The attendant lowered his head.



“I am not surprised, Your Highness.”



Chengyu looked back at the register.



There were twelve regular guests listed for the previous gathering and twenty attendants included in the original estimate.



The revised account had recorded the actual attendance separately.



The extra expense had not been approved merely because someone had written a larger number.



Chengyu tapped the paper.



“Who prepared this?”



“The office clerk, under the supervision of the household steward.”



“Did the steward check it?”



“Yes.”



“Then keep it.”



He pushed the register aside.



The attendant waited.



Chengyu glanced at him.



“What?”



“Nothing, Your Highness.”



“You look as though you expected me to object.”



The attendant lowered his head further.



Chengyu sighed.



“I am allowed to complain about a small allowance. I am not required to invent a reason for a larger one.”



The attendant bowed.



“Yes, Your Highness.”



Chengyu picked up another document.



He was beginning to suspect that administrative responsibility involved a great deal of reading and very little opportunity to accuse anyone of incompetence.



It was an unfortunate discovery.

In the southern part of Yongjing, Shen Mingzhao began the new month by reviewing a letter from her accountant.



Sun Qiming had sent a brief report concerning the family settlement records.



The Shen family had begun checking the cash books against the entries relating to her personal advances.



The first group of records had been examined.



No confirmed repayment to Mingzhao had yet been identified.



However, Sun had not treated that as proof that no repayment had ever occurred.



He had written that several entries required further examination because the books recorded money leaving the estate accounts without consistently identifying the final recipient.



Mingzhao read the report while Qiao Yue arranged the morning tea.



“Any confirmed repayment?” Qiao Yue asked.



“Not yet.”



“Then the family has found nothing.”



“The family has found entries.”



“That sounds like nothing.”



“It is not nothing.”



Mingzhao placed the report on the table.



“If money left an estate account, we need to know where it went. If it went to me, there should be a receipt, an acknowledgment, or some other evidence. If it went to a steward, that does not establish repayment to me.”



Qiao Yue poured tea.



“And if they cannot identify the recipient?”



“Then the entry remains unexplained.”



“Your father may dislike that.”



“My father dislikes many things.”



Qiao Yue considered this.



“That is true.”



Mingzhao picked up the second sheet.



Sun had also noted that the revised property schedule contained a new column distinguishing the person who authorized an expenditure from the person who ultimately received its benefit.



Mingzhao read that line again.



The distinction was important.



Her name appeared in many old records because she had managed the estate.



That did not mean every payment authorized under her supervision had been her personal property or her personal debt.



The furnishing expenditure had already demonstrated the danger of confusing an account manager with an owner.



She took a writing brush.



“Send Sun a reply.”



Qiao Yue brought the inkstone closer.



Mingzhao dictated.



“Ask him to continue the examination using the original cash books. For each suspected repayment, record the date, amount, account from which it was paid, named recipient, and supporting evidence. Do not classify an entry as repayment merely because it appears near one of my advances.”



Qiao Yue wrote.



“Anything else?”



“Yes. The four payment-confirmed advances must remain separate from the nine confirmed advances until the repayment arrangements are established.”



Qiao Yue finished writing.



Mingzhao reviewed the letter and sealed it.



The settlement was not moving quickly.



But at least the categories were becoming more precise.



That was preferable to a fast conclusion built from incorrect assumptions.

At the Marquisate of Qingyuan, Shen Mingyuan was discovering that reviewing old accounts required more patience than he had expected.



The cash books had been brought to a side room.



Several bundles of papers lay across the table, each tied with faded string.



Steward Zhou De stood nearby.



Mingyuan opened the first register.



“Here is a payment of thirty-two taels.”



“Yes, Young Master.”



“It says it was for household transport.”



“Yes.”



“Who received it?”



“The cart contractor, according to the entry.”



“Which cart contractor?”



Steward Zhou looked at the page.



“The name is not written.”



“Is there a receipt?”



“There is a receipt number.”



“Where is the receipt?”



“We have not found it.”



Mingyuan looked at him.



“Then we do not know whether this payment was made to the contractor?”



“We know the account records a payment. We do not yet have the supporting receipt.”



Mingyuan leaned back.



He was beginning to understand why Mingzhao had refused to accept broad statements.



A line in a register could establish that someone had written a payment into an account.



It could not necessarily establish who received the money.



He turned to the next page.



“Continue.”



Steward Zhou nodded.



The work proceeded slowly.



They found one payment with a matching receipt, another with a named recipient but no acknowledgment, and a third with a reference to a separate account.



None could yet be connected to the advances claimed by Mingzhao.



Mingyuan rubbed his forehead.



“How many books are there?”



“Those concerning the relevant period?”



“Yes.”



“Seven.”



Mingyuan stared at the bundles.



“Seven?”



“Seven principal books. There are also supporting sheets.”



Mingyuan closed his eyes briefly.



Steward Zhou did not comment.



He had managed estate accounts for decades. He knew that the number of records was rarely the most troublesome part.



The troublesome part was discovering what they did not say.



Mingyuan reopened the book.



“Bring the supporting sheets tomorrow.”



Steward Zhou bowed.



“Yes, Young Master.”



Mingyuan looked at the empty space beside the register.



He had once believed that managing an estate meant making decisions.



He was beginning to learn that many decisions were only as reliable as the records beneath them.

In another part of the Marquisate, Shen Mingxiu was waiting for the carpenter's revised work schedule.



The southern building's roof repairs had been approved in principle.



The approval did not mean the work could begin immediately.



The carpenter still needed to confirm the order of work, identify the damaged supports, and distinguish between pieces requiring repair and those requiring replacement.



Mingxiu sat at a small table with the previous estimate.



She had marked several lines.



Her mother, Madam Liang, entered the room.



“Has the carpenter replied?”



“Not yet.”



“Then send someone to remind him.”



“I already sent someone yesterday.”



Madam Liang glanced at the papers.



“Why is this taking so long? The roof has been waiting for repairs.”



“Because the carpenter has not confirmed the damaged supports.”



“Surely he can simply replace them.”



Mingxiu looked up.



“Why replace sound timber?”



Madam Liang frowned.



“To make certain the roof is secure.”



“That is not the same as replacing everything.”



Mingxiu tapped the estimate.



“If a support can be repaired safely, the cost should be recorded as repair. If it cannot, the carpenter must explain why replacement is necessary.”



Madam Liang looked at her daughter for a moment.



“You have become very particular.”



Mingxiu did not answer immediately.



She had heard that criticism before.



It was usually delivered by someone who wanted her to approve a figure without asking how it had been calculated.



“Someone must be particular,” she said.



Madam Liang's expression tightened.



But she did not argue further.



Mingxiu returned to the estimate.



The carpenter would be paid for work properly completed, not for materials that had never needed replacing.



She was beginning to understand that a household's money could disappear through perfectly respectable-looking decisions.

At the Marquisate's old warehouse, former steward Zhang had begun examining the certified copies of the grain records.



The sheets had been arranged chronologically, as Mingyuan requested.



He placed the first copy beside the second.



Then the third.



He worked slowly, comparing the recorded quantities, the dates, and the names of the officials who had signed each entry.



The old records were not entirely consistent.



Some listed the total stock.



Others listed the amount immediately available for issue.



That distinction explained why two books had shown different figures without necessarily contradicting each other.



It did not explain the missing twenty-seven sacks.



Zhang returned to the earliest sheet.



He remembered the warehouse.



He remembered the separate storage areas.



He remembered the pressure to make sure grain intended for transport was ready before the carts arrived.



But memory did not supply a recipient.



He wrote a note in the margin of his own working copy.



*Document confirms the recorded quantity. Document does not identify the final destination.*



He continued to the next sheet.



There was no point writing a name simply because he could imagine who might have been responsible.



If he made an accusation, it had to be supported.



He finished the first group of comparisons before noon.



Then he requested another set of copies.



The inquiry would take longer than Mingyuan had hoped.



It was already taking longer than Zhang had expected.



Neither fact made the records clearer.

At Lin Shuyun's workshop, the new month began with a different kind of problem.



A delivery tally had been placed beside the worktable.



The first forty-eight bolts of the new order had been inspected and accepted by Zhou Jian on behalf of buyer Zhou Wenqi.



Another twenty-four had passed the workshop's internal inspection.



They had not yet been inspected by the buyer.



The remaining quantity to be produced was now one hundred and twenty-eight bolts.



Lin Shuyun read the tally aloud.



“Forty-eight accepted. Twenty-four internally inspected. One hundred and twenty-eight remaining.”



Mingzhao checked the figures.



“Correct.”



Lin looked at the workshop floor.



The additional workers were becoming more familiar with the revised folding method. The two trainees had improved, although neither had reached the speed of the experienced finishers.



One was careful and slow.



The other was quick and still needed reminders not to rush the edges.



The senior worker supervising them approached.



“The next group is ready for inspection.”



Mingzhao stood.



“Have the measurements been checked?”



“Yes.”



“Against the recorded specifications?”



“Yes.”



“And the sample?”



“Beside the inspection table.”



Mingzhao followed him.



The first few bolts were examined.



The folds were even.



The edges were consistent.



One bolt had a slight difference in tension.



Mingzhao examined it, then looked at the worker responsible.



“Why is this one tighter?”



The trainee hesitated.



“I thought the fold should be held more firmly.”



“Did the specification require a firmer fold?”



“No.”



“Then why did you change it?”



The trainee lowered his eyes.



“I wanted it to be neat.”



Mingzhao looked at the bolt again.



It was not badly made.



But it was not identical to the approved sample.



“Neatness is not permission to change the specification.”



“Yes, Manager Shen.”



“Correct it, and record the adjustment.”



The trainee took the bolt back.



The senior worker watched him go.



“He is improving.”



“He is.”



“He made the same mistake twice last week.”



“And this time he corrected it without damaging the material.”



The senior worker nodded.



Mingzhao returned to the table.



She did not expect the trainees to become experienced workers in a few days.



She expected them to learn the agreed method and follow it consistently.



The difference mattered.



A worker who could produce one excellent bolt by improvising might produce twenty inconsistent ones the following day.



The buyer was paying for a uniform order, not a demonstration of individual creativity.



Lin Shuyun looked at the inspection notes.



“The revised method is saving time.”



“It is.”



“More than we expected.”



“Time saved is not the same as profit earned.”



Lin gave her a sidelong glance.



“You have become very cautious.”



“I have become acquainted with wages.”



Lin laughed.



“That is a difficult education.”



“It is an expensive one.”



They continued checking the group.



The corrected bolt passed the second inspection.



The remaining bolts were accepted internally.



The production record was updated.



No buyer inspection had yet been scheduled for this group.



Mingzhao made sure the distinction was written clearly.

After the inspection, Lin brought out the new wage ledger.



The four additional workers had now completed their first weeks of employment.



Their wages were recorded separately from the original workshop staff.



The trainees' supervised hours were also listed.



Mingzhao examined the entries.



“Why is this training time included in the ordinary production total?”



Lin looked at the page.



“Because they were working during those hours.”



“They were working, but not at the same rate as experienced finishers.”



“They still received wages.”



“I am not disputing their wages. I am disputing the classification.”



Lin considered the point.



The trainees' hours were legitimate labour costs, but they were also part of the additional expense required to bring new workers into the workshop.



If the figures were mixed together without explanation, it would be difficult to assess whether the revised method had actually reduced production costs.



Lin took the ledger.



“We can separate training hours, supervised production hours, and ordinary production.”



“Do that.”



“Then the report will be longer.”



“It is already longer.”



Lin looked at her.



Mingzhao looked back.



After a moment, Lin laughed again.



“Very well. We shall have a ledger large enough to frighten the next buyer.”



“The next buyer will not be paying for the ledger.”



“No. Only for the silk.”



“That is fortunate.”



They returned to work.



The workshop was becoming more efficient.



It was also becoming more aware of the cost of efficiency.



Neither development could be measured by looking at the number of finished bolts alone.

That afternoon, Mingzhao received a short letter from her father.



It was not an invitation to another family meeting.



Instead, Shen Guowei informed her that the cash-book examination was underway and that the family would send her a written summary once the relevant entries had been reviewed.



He also stated that the family would not classify any unverified payment as repayment without supporting evidence.



Mingzhao read the letter.



Qiao Yue stood beside her.



“Does that mean they agree?”



“It means they have written that they will examine the evidence.”



“Is that different?”



“Very.”



Qiao Yue sighed.



“You make every sentence sound like a legal dispute.”



“Because every sentence can become one.”



Mingzhao folded the letter.



She was not satisfied.



But she was not going to reject a reasonable procedure simply because it came from her family.



“Send a reply thanking Father for confirming the process.”



Qiao Yue blinked.



“Thanking him?”



“Yes.”



“Should I write that you are grateful?”



“No.”



Qiao Yue looked down at the writing paper.



“Then what should I write?”



“Thank him for confirming the process.”



“That sounds less warm.”



“It is also more accurate.”



Qiao Yue began writing.



Mingzhao returned to the workshop accounts.



The family settlement remained incomplete.



The nine confirmed advances had not disappeared.



The four payment-confirmed advances still required clarification.



The ten uncertain entries remained uncertain.



The seventy-two taels in study repairs still required a decision on reimbursement.



The two hundred and twenty-eight taels remained disputed.



And the grain discrepancy had not been resolved.



The new month had not made any of those matters disappear.



It had, however, given them another date under which to be recorded.

In the western guest residence, Xu Yuru received a small message from Fang Ru shortly before evening.



The attendant had been asked by Madam Zhao whether the former Empress wished to receive a visitor from the inner palace.



Yuru looked at the message.



“Who?”



“An attendant from the Third Princess's residence.”



“Did she say why?”



“She said the princess wished to ask about a book.”



Yuru considered it.



“Which book?”



“She did not specify.”



“Then the princess should send the title.”



Fang Ru nodded.



“Would you like me to tell her that you are unavailable?”



“No. Tell her I will be happy to answer once I know which book she means.”



Fang Ru prepared the reply.



Yuru returned to her seat.



The question was probably about the library catalogue. The Third Princess had been trying to identify an old volume whose title was missing from the surviving record.



Yuru was willing to help.



She was not willing to turn every question into an invitation to resume the entire administration.



There was a difference.



A personal answer did not make her responsible for the whole library.



A conversation did not make her the head of the Imperial Household Administration.



She had spent too many years allowing those distinctions to blur.



Now she intended to keep them separate.

The next morning, the Third Princess's message arrived.



It identified the book.



The first page contained a partial title, but the old catalogue listed two works with similar names.



Yuru read the description and sent a brief reply suggesting that the princess compare the remaining chapter headings with the two catalogue entries.



She did not choose one for her.



She did not ask for the entire library register.



She did not summon a clerk.



The answer was enough to guide the next step.



Fang Ru placed the message on the writing table.



“Will the princess send another question?”



“Perhaps.”



“Should I prepare the reply paper?”



Yuru smiled faintly.



“Prepare it when she asks.”



Fang Ru bowed.



Outside, the courtyard remained quiet.



The sixth month had begun.



In the palace, officials were learning to record what they knew and preserve what they did not.



In the Marquisate, the family was discovering that an account entry did not become a repayment merely because it was convenient.



In the workshop, twenty-four internally inspected bolts waited for the buyer's next inspection, while another one hundred and twenty-eight still needed to be made.



And in the western guest residence, the former Empress was beginning to discover that a day could pass without anyone asking her to resolve a problem.



She did not yet know what she would do with all the time that might eventually become hers.



For the moment, she opened her mother's letter again and read the complaint about fruit prices.



Then she took up her brush.



There were several things she wished to tell her mother about the market.



None of them required an imperial seal.



And that, she thought, was a rather pleasant beginning to the sixth month.
