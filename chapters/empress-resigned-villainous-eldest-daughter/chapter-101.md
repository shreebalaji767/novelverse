# **Chapter 101: The Former Empress Has Discovered That a Household Manual Can Recommend a Monthly Account Review Without Explaining Why the Head of the Household Must Also Count Every Spoon. The Marquis's Eldest Daughter Has Discovered That Thirty Bolts Can Be Nearly Finished Without Being Nearly Delivered. The Marquis's Heir Has Discovered That a Missing Notebook May Have Been Issued Properly, Returned Improperly, or Never Returned at All, and That Each Possibility Requires a Different Question.**

**5th Day of the Seventh Month, 18th Year of Yonghe.**

Xu Yuru began the morning with the household manual.

The author had moved from servants' duties to accounts, and the first recommendation was sensible enough.

A household should review its expenditure regularly.

Yuru read the paragraph twice.

The author then recommended that the household head personally examine every account at the end of each month, compare it with every receipt, and interview every servant responsible for an unusual payment.

Yuru placed the book on the table.

"Fang Ru."

"Yes, Madam?"

"If this household has twenty servants, and each servant has made three purchases in a month, how long will the head of the household spend examining them?"

Fang Ru considered the question.

"Perhaps several days."

"And if the household has two hundred servants?"

"Then the head of the household will need a larger table."

Yuru looked at her.

Fang Ru lowered her eyes, though her smile remained.

Yuru opened her notebook.

The author had made the same mistake in several chapters. He had treated personal attention as the highest form of responsibility, as though a household became reliable merely because its head was tired enough to remember every detail.

Yuru wrote:

*Review the account at intervals. Examine exceptions. Require the responsible clerk to explain discrepancies. Do not confuse a review with repeating the clerk's work.*

She paused.

The sentence was not entirely new.

She had once reviewed palace accounts in precisely that manner. Later, when a clerk made an error, she had corrected it herself. When another clerk failed to notice the correction, she had checked the next entry. When a third office asked whether the first correction could be treated as a precedent, she had written a rule.

The rule had been useful.

The habit of personally following every rule had been less so.

Yuru closed the notebook.

She did not intend to become the household manual's second author.

"Fang Ru, I shall read the next section after breakfast."

Fang Ru set down the tea.

"Would Madam prefer something from the kitchen?"

"Something that does not require a monthly account review."

Fang Ru went to arrange it.

Yuru looked at the book again.

The author had written four pages about the importance of keeping accurate household records.

He had written nothing about the importance of allowing the person who kept them to finish breakfast.

At the eastern-market workshop, Shen Mingzhao received the production report for the Gao Wen order.

The final thirty bolts had been divided into three working groups.

Ten had completed weaving and were awaiting the finishing examination.

The remaining twenty were still being worked.

The report included the names of the workers responsible for each group, the materials issued, the quantity returned, and the inspection stage.

Mingzhao read it carefully.

The first ten were not yet complete.

The next twenty were not yet complete.

The final thirty were not yet complete.

She put down the report.

Qiao Yue, who had brought it from the workshop, glanced at the marked page.

"Is there a problem?"

"No."

"Then why has Madam crossed out the word 'finished'?"

"Because the report called the ten bolts finished."

"They have completed weaving."

"Then the report should say they have completed weaving."

Qiao Yue nodded.

Mingzhao returned the paper with a correction.

She did not object to the workshop reporting progress. She objected to the habit of allowing one stage of work to stand for the entire order.

The distinction mattered.

A bolt could be woven but not finished.

It could be finished but not inspected.

It could pass internal inspection but fail the buyer's examination.

It could be accepted but remain undelivered.

It could be delivered but remain unpaid.

The workshop had learned to record each stage separately.

Now it had to learn to describe each stage accurately.

Mingzhao looked at the contract.

The delivery period was still sufficient.

The deposit had been received.

The sample had been approved.

There was no need to hurry by sacrificing inspection.

She wrote one sentence at the bottom of the report:

*Continue according to the approved schedule. Do not shorten the inspection period to make the progress report appear complete.*

Qiao Yue took the paper.

Mingzhao returned to her desk.

The order was moving.

That was enough for the day.

At the Marquisate, Shen Mingyuan received a second reply from the transport office.

The office had examined the surviving issue records for the missing movement notebook.

The notebook had been issued to the northern warehouse transport supervisor.

The record did not identify the individual who received it.

The transport office had also found a later entry concerning the return of several notebooks from the same warehouse.

The relevant notebook was not listed.

Mingyuan read the reply.

It did not establish that the notebook had been lost during the dispatch.

It did not establish that the supervisor had kept it.

It did not establish that the notebook had ever been returned.

The transport office had merely confirmed that its own surviving register was incomplete.

Mingyuan called Liang Wen.

"Please prepare a separate list of the questions that remain."

Liang Wen took out a sheet.

"The four-cart dispatch?"

"Yes."

"The missing notebook?"

"Yes."

"The grain discrepancy?"

"Separate."

"The old purchasing voucher?"

"Also separate."

Liang Wen looked at the page.

"That will be four lists."

"Four questions."

"Some of them may be connected."

"They may be. But we do not know that yet."

Mingyuan dictated the first question.

Who held the transport supervisor's duty on the date of the four-cart dispatch?

The second:

Was the missing movement notebook assigned to that supervisor, and is there any surviving record of its transfer or return?

The third:

What evidence connects the four-cart dispatch to the twenty-seven unexplained sacks?

The fourth:

Has the original purchasing voucher attributed to Mingzhao been located, and if not, what evidence remains concerning its recipient and purpose?

Liang Wen wrote each question separately.

Mingyuan examined the list.

He had once thought that a complicated investigation became easier when all the entries were placed on one large table.

Now he understood that a large table could also make unrelated uncertainties appear connected.

A missing notebook was not proof of missing grain.

A purchasing seal was not proof of personal receipt.

A payment entry was not proof of repayment.

The questions had to remain separate until the evidence brought them together.

Mingyuan signed the request.

"Send it to the appropriate offices."

Liang Wen gathered the papers.

"Will the family council ask why we have not reached a conclusion?"

"Probably."

"And what shall we tell them?"

Mingyuan looked at the records.

"That the inquiry is still in progress."

The family council met in the afternoon.

The Marquis of Qingyuan sat at the head of the table.

The revised property schedule lay before him.

Several members had already marked the document with questions.

One asked whether the four confirmed payment entries could be treated as repayments.

Another asked whether the seventy-two taels for study repairs should be deducted from the estate's maintenance expenditure.

A third asked why the northern canal property remained outside the division.

Mingyuan answered each question in turn.

The four payment-confirmed entries were not yet classified as repayments because the records did not establish the arrangement.

The seventy-two taels had been paid from Mingzhao's personal funds for estate maintenance. Whether reimbursement was due remained a separate question.

The northern canal property had been acquired independently and was not part of the family estate division.

The council member who had asked about the canal property frowned.

"Then the family cannot claim it?"

"Not merely because it is adjacent to family land."

The Marquis looked at the schedule.

He had hoped the revised document would bring the matter closer to settlement.

Instead, it had revealed more questions.

But the questions were now written down.

That was better than allowing them to remain hidden beneath a total that looked convenient.

The council agreed to continue examining the supporting documents.

No final division was declared.

Mingyuan returned to the accounts office with the schedule.

He did not feel victorious.

He felt tired.

Yet the tiredness was different from the one he had known when his sister managed the estate.

Then, every unresolved matter seemed to belong to her.

Now, the questions belonged to the offices and people responsible for answering them.

At her residence, Mingzhao received a brief note from the workshop supervisor.

The ten bolts that had completed weaving were now in finishing.

The next twenty remained in production.

The report had been corrected.

Mingzhao read it and set it aside.

Qiao Yue brought another paper.

"This is from the Marquisate."

Mingzhao opened it.

The family council had acknowledged receipt of her questions concerning the property schedule.

It had not yet supplied the missing supporting documents.

The letter also stated that the council intended to continue the transport inquiry.

Mingzhao read the final paragraph.

It asked whether she would be willing to attend a meeting once the records were assembled.

She took up her pen.

She was willing to attend a meeting concerning the property division and her own claims.

She was willing to answer written questions about past authorizations when the relevant records were provided.

She was not willing to resume estate administration.

She wrote that clearly.

Then she folded the letter.

Qiao Yue watched her.

"Will they accept it?"

"They may."

"And if they do not?"

"Then they will have to explain which part they cannot accept."

Mingzhao placed the letter beside the property schedule.

The family had begun to understand that she would not return merely because a room had been prepared, a chair had been placed at the table, or a document had been left beside her name.

She was not refusing every discussion.

She was refusing to become the answer to every unfinished question.

That distinction had taken the family a long time to learn.

It would probably take longer to remember.

Toward evening, Xu Yuru finished the chapter on household accounts.

The author had recommended that the head of the household personally inspect the accounts every month.

Yuru had written three questions in the margin.

Who prepares the account?

Who checks it?

Who is responsible for correcting an error?

She closed the book.

The questions were more useful than the author's conclusion.

Fang Ru brought a small plate of fruit.

Yuru took one piece and looked toward the courtyard.

The guest residence was quiet.

No official had arrived with a report.

No clerk had asked her to confirm a quantity.

No servant had brought a register that needed her signature merely because she had once held the position responsible for it.

She ate the fruit.

Then she returned to her notebook and added one final sentence beneath the morning's observations:

*The head of a household should know whether the work is being done. That does not mean the head must do it again.*

She read the sentence once.

It was plain.

It was sufficient.

Yuru put away the notebook.

Tomorrow, she would continue the manual.

Perhaps the author would finally reach a chapter in which the household head was permitted to sit down.
