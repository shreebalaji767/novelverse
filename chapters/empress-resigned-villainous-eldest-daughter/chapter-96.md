# **Chapter 96: The Former Empress Has Discovered That a Household Manual Can Recommend Three Supervisors for One Delivery and Still Fail to Explain Who Should Be Allowed to Make a Decision. The Marquis's Eldest Daughter Has Received a Production Report Without Being Summoned to the Workshop. The Marquis's Heir Has Requested a Transport Roster and Has Learned That an Archive Can Be Missing Even When Every Official Insists That It Must Exist Somewhere.**

**30th Day of the Sixth Month, 18th Year of Yonghe.**

Xu Yuru began the morning by reading the last two pages of her father's book.

She had intended to finish the chapter the previous evening, but the final section had contained a list of household rules so long that she had decided it deserved fresh attention.

The author recommended that a household establish separate responsibility for receiving goods, counting goods, recording goods, storing goods, and inspecting the accounts.

He then advised the household head to examine every record personally.

Yuru stared at the final sentence.

"Fang Ru."

Her attendant, who was arranging the writing materials, turned.

"Madam?"

"How many people does this author believe a household head should become?"

Fang Ru glanced at the page.

"Perhaps he believes a capable person can manage several duties."

"Then he should have written a book about several people sharing one body."

Fang Ru lowered her head.

Yuru put the book down.

The problem was not that the author wanted supervision. A household without oversight could be exploited by careless servants or dishonest suppliers. A person responsible for the household's property had to know whether the accounts were reliable.

But the author had failed to distinguish between establishing a system and personally performing every part of it.

Yuru had made that mistake herself.

She remembered a palace storekeeper who had once brought her a discrepancy between the number of jars purchased and the number entered into the receiving register. She had found the error, corrected the record, and instructed the office to reconcile future deliveries.

The next month, the same storekeeper had brought her another discrepancy.

The month after that, he had brought her a third.

It had taken her an embarrassingly long time to realize that the storekeeper had learned to bring the problem to her rather than correct the procedure that produced it.

She had been praised for efficiency.

The office had become dependent.

Both things had been true.

Yuru turned the book over and examined its cover.

"Perhaps Father chose this book because he knows I will disagree with it."

Fang Ru smiled.

"Or perhaps he chose it because it is the only book in his library that has so many opportunities to irritate you."

"That would be a very particular form of hospitality."

She marked the final paragraph.

Then she wrote a short note to her father.

She thanked him for lending the book and mentioned that she had found its discussion of divided duties useful, although its repeated insistence on personal supervision had left her wondering whether the author expected the household head to inspect the roof, the kitchen, the accounts, and the servants before going to bed.

She added that she hoped her father would not attempt to follow the recommendation literally.

After reading the note, she considered adding another sentence.

She did not.

Her father would understand the joke.

If he did not, he could read the book again.

The former Empress's letter reached the Xu residence before noon.

Duke Xu read it in his study.

He was pleased that his daughter had found the book useful. He was less pleased that she had found its author ridiculous.

He had selected it because it contained practical material about estate management, not because he intended to provoke her.

He read the final sentence again.

Then he laughed.

Madam Xu, who had entered to ask whether he intended to send Yuru another book, heard him.

"What is amusing?"

"Yuru has accused the author of expecting the household head to inspect the roof, the kitchen, the accounts, and the servants before sleeping."

Madam Xu looked at the letter.

"That sounds like the author."

"Do you think so?"

"I think the author has never had to manage a household with more than six people."

Duke Xu folded the letter.

"I chose it because I thought she might enjoy the discussion of estate responsibilities."

"Then you should let her finish reading it before choosing another."

"I was not planning to send another immediately."

Madam Xu looked at him.

He cleared his throat.

"I was only considering what might be suitable."

"That is how you ended up with two books ready for her when she had not yet returned the first."

Duke Xu had no reply.

He placed Yuru's letter in the drawer where he kept family correspondence.

He did not prepare another book.

For the moment.

At the eastern-market workshop, Shen Mingzhao received the second production report for the eighty-bolt order.

The first ten bolts had passed internal inspection.

The next twenty had been completed in their weaving stage, but finishing and inspection remained.

The two bolts previously separated for edge checks had both passed after one minor correction.

The new report included the names of the workers responsible for the correction and the inspector who had confirmed the result.

Mingzhao read it carefully.

Then she looked at the production schedule.

The workshop had not lost time.

The correction had been completed within the planned work period.

No additional material had been written off.

She put the report aside.

Qiao Yue, who had been arranging the correspondence, looked toward the paper.

"Will you go to the workshop today?"

"No."

"Do you not need to see the next group?"

"I have the report."

"But the twenty bolts are not yet ready."

"That is why the report says they are not ready."

Qiao Yue accepted this.

Mingzhao continued reading a separate note from the workshop supervisor.

The workers had asked whether the finishing inspection could be moved to the afternoon rather than performed immediately after the morning weaving shift. The reason was practical: the fabric needed time to settle after the loom work, and the edges were easier to assess after the material had rested.

The supervisor had proposed a trial.

Mingzhao read the proposal twice.

It was not unreasonable.

The approved sample had been assessed after the fabric had settled. The workshop's current schedule had been designed around the convenience of the workers, not necessarily the most reliable inspection conditions.

She wrote a brief response.

The workshop could test the afternoon inspection arrangement on the current group, provided the production record stated the time between weaving and inspection. The change was not to alter the approved specifications or the correction process. If the results differed, the supervisor was to record the difference before changing the regular schedule.

She signed the instruction.

Qiao Yue watched her.

"You have approved a change without visiting."

"I have approved a trial, not a permanent change."

"That is still a decision."

"Yes."

"Then you have not stopped making decisions."

Mingzhao raised an eyebrow.

"I have stopped making every decision personally."

Qiao Yue considered this.

"That may be harder."

"It is certainly less convenient."

Mingzhao returned the letter to its envelope.

She had spent years believing that a good manager should know every detail.

She still believed a good manager should know the important details.

But knowing that a task existed was not the same as performing it. Knowing that a worker had made a correction was not the same as standing beside the worker. And approving a reasonable trial did not mean she had to supervise every minute of it.

The distinction was becoming clearer.

She hoped it would also become profitable.

At the Marquisate, Shen Mingyuan received the first response from the central transport office.

It was not the response he wanted.

The office had found a reference to the northern warehouse's transport supervisor in an old duty assignment register. However, the register identified the position rather than the individual who had held it on the date of the four-cart dispatch.

The office had also located a note stating that a movement notebook had been issued to the northern warehouse supervisor during the relevant season.

There was no record of its return.

Mingyuan read the copy.

"Does the office have the original?"

Liang Wen shook his head.

"They sent a certified copy from their surviving register. The original volume is in the central archive."

"Then why did they not examine it?"

"They say the volume is undergoing reorganization."

Mingyuan looked at him.

"How long has it been undergoing reorganization?"

Liang Wen consulted the accompanying note.

"Since the beginning of the previous year."

Mingyuan stared at him.

Steward Zhou, standing nearby, looked away.

"That is a long time to reorganize a register."

"The office says several damaged volumes were found in the same storage section," Liang Wen explained. "They are being repaired and indexed."

"Can they confirm whether the movement notebook itself was returned?"

"No."

"Can they identify the supervisor?"

"Not from the copy they sent."

Mingyuan placed the paper on the table.

The new information established that a separate movement notebook had indeed been issued to the northern warehouse supervisor.

It did not establish who had carried it.

It did not establish whether the notebook contained the missing destination.

It did not establish whether the four carts had carried grain.

But it was more than they had known yesterday.

"Send a request for the original duty register to be examined when the archive work permits," he said. "Ask whether the issue record contains the name of the person who received the notebook."

Liang Wen wrote it down.

"Should we also ask the northern warehouse for any local copy?"

"Yes. And ask the former transport supervisor if he can be located. Do not assume he still lives in the same district."

Steward Zhou nodded.

Mingyuan turned to the grain figures.

Forty-seven sacks in the original stock record.

Twenty covered by the instruction.

Eighteen confirmed as received.

Two unconfirmed.

Twenty-seven unexplained.

He placed the transport record beside the grain schedule but did not combine them.

"Keep these separate," he said.

Liang Wen looked at the papers.

"Even though the dates overlap?"

"Especially because the dates overlap. If we write them together, someone will eventually decide that the four carts carried the twenty-seven sacks. We have no evidence of that."

Steward Zhou gave a slow nod.

Mingyuan was beginning to understand that an investigation could become less reliable when its records were arranged too neatly.

In the afternoon, Mingyuan reviewed the property-division papers.

His father's letter had instructed the accounts office to prepare a separate schedule for Mingzhao's claims.

The first draft was not yet ready.

Several entries had supporting documents, but the family still needed to distinguish between money paid directly by Mingzhao and money merely authorized under her name as estate manager.

The nine confirmed advances were supported by records showing funds transferred from her personal accounts or receipts issued to her.

The four payment-confirmed entries had evidence of payment, but the family had not established whether the amounts had later been repaid through another account or offset against an estate obligation.

The ten uncertain entries lacked enough supporting evidence to classify them.

The seventy-two taels spent on the study repairs had been confirmed as estate maintenance paid from Mingzhao's personal funds.

The two hundred and twenty-eight taels remained disputed.

Mingyuan read the schedule and found that the clerk had written "Mingzhao's expenses" beside the seventy-two-tael entry.

He crossed it out.

"Write 'estate maintenance paid from personal funds.'"

The clerk looked uncertain.

"Would that not mean the same thing?"

"No. The first description makes it sound as though she spent the money for herself. The second says what the money was used for."

The clerk corrected the line.

Mingyuan examined the next page.

The four payment-confirmed entries had been grouped beneath the heading "possible repayment."

He crossed that out too.

"Write 'payment confirmed; repayment arrangement unverified.'"

The clerk nodded.

Mingyuan had once thought that learning the accounts meant learning how to make the figures balance.

Now he was discovering that it also meant refusing to make them balance when the evidence did not support it.

He looked at the unfinished schedule.

"Send this back for another review. I do not want the family to receive a document that makes uncertain matters look settled."

Steward Zhou nodded.

"Should the Marquis set a meeting date?"

"Not until the copies are ready."

"Madam Mingzhao may be waiting."

"Then we should give her something worth reviewing."

Mingyuan returned the papers.

He did not know whether his father would be pleased with the delay.

He suspected that his father would prefer a meeting to a completed schedule, because meetings could create the appearance of progress.

But Mingzhao had already made clear that she would not attend a gathering merely to hear the same questions repeated.

Mingyuan was beginning to think she was right about that.

At the temporary residence, Mingzhao received a short message from the workshop supervisor.

The afternoon inspection trial had begun.

The first group of six bolts had been allowed to rest after weaving. The inspector had compared them against the approved sample later in the day.

Five had passed.

One had been held for a closer edge examination.

The report did not yet recommend a permanent change.

Mingzhao read it.

She was pleased that the supervisor had not declared success after six pieces.

She was more pleased that the held bolt had been identified before the group was treated as complete.

She wrote back that the trial should continue for the remainder of the group and that the final result should compare both inspection timing and correction frequency.

She did not ask for the bolt to be sent to her.

She did not ask for the inspector to come to her residence.

She did not demand that the supervisor explain himself in person.

The workshop had been given a specific question to test.

It could return with the answer.

Qiao Yue brought in the evening correspondence.

Among the papers was a note from Sun Qiming, the independent accountant.

He had reviewed the closed account for the previous two-hundred-bolt order and confirmed that all payments, wages, packing charges, and transport expenses had been entered.

The final margin remained modest but positive.

Sun also noted that the new eighty-bolt order had been priced with the training and inspection costs of the earlier work in mind. The deposit covered the initial production requirements, but the final profit would depend on the actual finishing costs, transport, and any corrections.

Mingzhao read the note and set it aside.

The earlier order had been completed.

The new one had begun.

She was not going to confuse a successful account with a guarantee that every future order would succeed.

"Prepare the papers for tomorrow," she told Qiao Yue. "I want the new order's production record kept separately from the old account."

"Yes, Madam."

"And make sure the deposit is recorded as a deposit, not as profit."

Qiao Yue nodded.

Mingzhao looked at her.

"Do not let anyone write that the order has earned money merely because the buyer has paid part of it."

"I will tell the accounts clerk."

"Good."

The evening passed without another workshop message.

Mingzhao found that she did not need one.

At the western guest residence, Xu Yuru finished her letter to her father and placed the household manual beside the two borrowed books.

She had returned to the chapter on servants' authority, this time with a different question.

The author had described several household positions and assigned each a list of duties. Yet he had said little about what should happen when one servant discovered a problem outside his assigned responsibilities.

Should he report it?

Should he correct it?

Should he wait for the person whose duty it was?

The author had not answered.

Yuru considered the omission.

A servant who ignored an obvious problem because it was not his responsibility could cause harm. A servant who took over every task he noticed could create confusion.

The solution was not to forbid initiative.

It was to establish what a person could do immediately, what required approval, and what had to be reported.

She marked the passage.

Then she smiled.

"Fang Ru."

"Yes, Madam?"

"I think I have found something useful."

"Which passage?"

"The author has failed to answer his own question, so I shall answer it for him."

Fang Ru looked at the book.

"Will you write in the margin?"

"No. It belongs to Father."

"Then where will you write it?"

Yuru took a separate sheet of paper.

"Here."

She wrote a short note about responsibility, authority, and the duty to report an exception.

When she finished, she did not send it to anyone.

It was simply a thought she wanted to keep.

The former Empress folded the paper and placed it between the pages of her own notebook.

The day had passed without an imperial document, a palace dispute, or a request for her intervention.

At the Marquisate, a transport notebook remained missing.

At the workshop, one bolt still required inspection.

The family accounts were not settled.

None of these matters had been resolved.

And yet, for once, Yuru did not feel compelled to take any of them into her hands.

She closed the book.

Tomorrow could bring its own questions.

There was no need to answer them tonight.
