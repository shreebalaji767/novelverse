# **Chapter 95: The Former Empress Has Discovered That Delegating a Task Requires More Than Naming the Person Responsible. The Marquis's Eldest Daughter Has Discovered That a Workshop Can Complete Ten Bolts Without Her Watching Every Fold. The Marquis's Heir Has Found a Record of Four Carts and Has Learned That Knowing Who Recorded a Journey Is Not the Same as Knowing Where It Went.**

**29th Day of the Sixth Month, 18th Year of Yonghe.**

Xu Yuru had reached the section of her father's book in which the author explained the proper division of household servants.

She had expected a dull discussion of wages, meals, sleeping quarters, and the number of attendants required for a sufficiently respectable residence.

Instead, the author had devoted three pages to the dangers of assigning the same duty to several people.

Xu Yuru read the passage twice.

The first reading made her thoughtful.

The second made her put down the book.

Fang Ru, who was arranging the morning tea, noticed the movement.

"Is there something wrong with the passage, Madam?"

"The author believes that if three servants are responsible for receiving deliveries, each will assume that one of the others has counted the goods."

Fang Ru glanced at the page.

"That can happen."

"He recommends appointing one servant to receive the goods, another to count them, and a third to enter the quantity into the register."

"That sounds sensible."

"It does." Yuru turned the page back. "Then he recommends that the household head personally inspect the register every evening."

Fang Ru waited.

Yuru looked at her.

"Apparently, the author has spent several pages explaining how to distribute responsibility, only to conclude that the household head must collect all of it again before going to bed."

Fang Ru lowered her eyes.

Her shoulders moved once.

"Perhaps he is afraid the servants will become careless."

"Perhaps he has never tried to inspect a household register every evening."

Yuru returned to the book.

The author's intentions were not entirely unreasonable. A household with too many servants could become difficult to supervise. A household with too few could leave important duties unattended. If responsibilities were divided without clear boundaries, servants might dispute who had made an error, and if no one checked the records, an error could remain unnoticed until it became expensive.

Yet the author had treated the household head's personal attention as the final remedy for every weakness.

Yuru had once done precisely that.

When the palace stores produced inconsistent figures, she had examined them. When the ceremony office submitted an incomplete request, she had returned it. When two departments disagreed about who had authorized a purchase, she had demanded the original documents.

At first, each intervention had been reasonable.

Later, the officials had begun bringing every disagreement to her.

It had become easier to ask the former Empress than to settle a matter through the proper office.

Yuru had not created every weakness in the system, but she had sometimes made those weaknesses comfortable.

She had corrected errors so quickly that others had never needed to learn how to prevent them.

The realization was not pleasant.

It was also not a reason to return.

She closed the book and placed a narrow paper marker beside the passage.

"Fang Ru."

"Yes, Madam?"

"If we eventually have a household of our own, I would prefer to know who is responsible for receiving goods, who checks them, and who records them."

"That seems reasonable."

"And I would prefer not to inspect every register before sleeping."

Fang Ru considered this.

"Then perhaps the person responsible for checking the register should be responsible for checking it."

Yuru looked at her.

"That is an unusually direct interpretation of the author's three pages."

"It is the only interpretation that would allow you to sleep."

Yuru laughed.

She reopened the book.

For the first time that morning, she read the next paragraph without feeling that she ought to send someone a memorandum.

The palace had also received a quiet report that morning.

It concerned the replacement of two damaged storage shelves in a section of the inner household.

The repair had been completed. The receiving clerk had signed for the materials, the carpenter had recorded the work, and the accounts office had entered the payment against the appropriate maintenance allocation.

There was no request for Yuru's opinion.

There was no document waiting in the guest residence.

Gao Shun had seen the report and passed it to the responsible office without hesitation.

The Crown Prince had separately reviewed a household expenditure statement. He found that one entry had been recorded under the wrong month and ordered the correction to be made with the original entry preserved.

Neither matter required the former Empress.

This was becoming ordinary.

Yuru found that she was pleased by it.

She had once thought a successful day was one in which she had resolved every outstanding difficulty.

Now she was beginning to appreciate a day in which the difficulties had been resolved by the people responsible for them.

She returned to the book.

The author had reached the subject of servants who were given authority to make small purchases.

His advice was to establish a limit, require receipts, and prohibit the purchasing servant from approving his own account.

Yuru nodded.

"That part is good."

Fang Ru poured tea.

"Will you mark it?"

"Yes."

Yuru marked the passage.

She did not write a note to the palace.

At the temporary residence near the eastern market, Shen Mingzhao was examining the workshop's production record.

The new order for eighty bolts had begun only three days earlier.

The deposit from Gao Wen had been received on the twenty-sixth. The agreed sample and written specifications were attached to the production authorization. The delivery period had been set at twenty-four days from the approval of the sample and receipt of the deposit.

The first ten bolts had passed internal inspection the previous day.

The next twenty were now being worked.

Mingzhao did not begin by asking whether the workshop had completed them.

She asked what had been completed, what remained, and whether the workers had followed the approved sample.

The workshop supervisor, Liu Cheng, placed the production sheet on the table.

"The first ten are finished. The next twenty have been woven, but the finishing is not complete."

"Any variation?"

"Nothing outside the written specifications so far. Two bolts need another check after finishing."

"Why?"

"The colour is consistent, but the edges on those two appear slightly tighter."

"Compared with the approved sample?"

"Not yet. The finishing worker noticed the difference before sending them to inspection."

Mingzhao looked up.

"Who noticed it?"

"Zhou Lian."

"Then write that down."

Liu Cheng hesitated.

"She is not the inspector."

"She does not need to be the inspector to notice something."

He nodded and added the name to the record.

Mingzhao examined the sample again.

The original difficulty had been the colour and width. The first trial had been slightly too warm and narrow. The workshop had adjusted the dye and loom tension separately, rather than changing both at once and pretending to know which adjustment had worked.

The second trial had passed.

The approved sample now sat beside the contract, and every production group was supposed to follow it.

Mingzhao had no intention of allowing the workshop to replace an agreed standard with an informal understanding.

But she also did not want workers to hide small problems until the end of a production run.

"Have the two bolts been separated from the others?"

"Yes."

"Good. Let the finishing worker explain what was done differently. Then compare the edges with the sample. If they are within the agreed range, pass them. If not, correct them."

Liu Cheng wrote down the instruction.

"Should I wait for you to inspect them?"

"No."

He looked up.

Mingzhao met his eyes.

"You have the sample. You have the written specifications. You have the inspection method. Why would you wait for me to look at something you are already qualified to examine?"

Liu Cheng seemed to consider whether this was a test.

It was not.

"Then I will have Zhou Lian and the inspector compare them."

"Have them record the result."

"Yes."

Mingzhao returned the sheet.

She had been tempted, at first, to inspect every bolt herself.

It was not difficult to understand why. She knew the contract, remembered the sample, understood the workshop's weaknesses, and could identify a careless fold more quickly than someone who had only recently been assigned to the work.

But the purpose of taking independent orders had not been to create another household in which every decision depended on her.

If the workshop could not complete ten bolts without her standing beside the table, it was not a business. It was merely her personal labour spread across several people.

She had already spent six years being indispensable to a family that had treated her work as an obligation.

She did not intend to reproduce the arrangement with paid workers.

"Send me the inspection record this evening," she said. "Not the bolts themselves unless there is a disagreement about the sample."

Liu Cheng gathered the papers.

"Understood."

After he left, Qiao Yue placed a cup of tea beside Mingzhao.

"Madam, you did not inspect the two bolts."

"I was not asked to."

"You were told they needed checking."

"They do."

Qiao Yue waited.

Mingzhao took the tea.

"That is why the inspector has a job."

Qiao Yue lowered her eyes, concealing a small smile.

Mingzhao noticed it.

"Do you disagree?"

"No, Madam."

"Then why are you smiling?"

"Because you have said something that would have made you angry if someone had said it to you three months ago."

Mingzhao looked at her.

Qiao Yue immediately became occupied with the tea tray.

Mingzhao could have denied it.

Instead, she drank her tea.

"That may be true."

At the Marquisate of Qingyuan, Shen Mingyuan had spent much of the morning looking at a record that did not tell him where four carts had gone.

The loose dispatch pages had been found among the northern warehouse papers.

They recorded a movement of four carts, but the cargo column was blank. The destination was incomplete. A short note stated that the destination would be confirmed upon arrival.

No arrival confirmation had been found.

The handwriting might have belonged to Li Shun, a clerk who had worked at the northern warehouse and had been assigned dispatch-record duties. The old warehouse steward Zhang had recognized a resemblance in the handwriting, but he had not been willing to identify it with certainty.

Mingyuan had decided not to turn a resemblance into a fact.

The personnel record established that Li Shun had worked there.

It did not establish that he had written this particular entry.

The distinction was becoming familiar.

He sat with Liang Wen, the accounts clerk now responsible for maintaining the consolidated purchasing register. Steward Zhou stood beside the table, holding a second bundle of documents.

"We found another reference to the transport supervisor," Liang Wen said.

Mingyuan looked up.

"Where?"

"In the duty assignment book. The northern warehouse transport supervisor was responsible for carrying a separate movement notebook when carts were sent outside the regular delivery route."

"Was that notebook returned?"

"There is no return entry."

"Does the record say who held it?"

"Not on the page we found. The duty assignment identifies the position, not the person who carried the book on that particular day."

Mingyuan leaned back.

"So we have a four-cart dispatch entry, an incomplete destination, and a separate notebook that may have contained the missing information."

"Yes."

"And we do not know whether the notebook was lost, retained, or filed elsewhere."

"That is correct."

Steward Zhou placed the second bundle on the table.

"We have requested the transport duty roster for the relevant period. It may identify the supervisor."

"May?"

"The roster was kept separately from the warehouse dispatch pages. The copy in the northern office is incomplete. The central transport office may have a duplicate."

Mingyuan examined the loose sheets.

"Then request the duplicate. Do not write that Li Shun was responsible until we have a record identifying him."

Liang Wen nodded.

Mingyuan tapped the blank cargo column.

"And do not assume the four carts carried grain simply because we are examining the grain discrepancy."

Steward Zhou looked at him.

"The timing is similar."

"Similar timing is a reason to investigate, not a reason to combine the records."

He turned to the table where the grain figures had been copied.

Forty-seven sacks in the original stock record.

Twenty sacks covered by the instruction.

Eighteen sacks confirmed as received.

Two sacks still unconfirmed.

Twenty-seven sacks unexplained.

The four-cart dispatch record might eventually help.

It might also concern another movement entirely.

Until the cargo and destination were established, it remained a separate question.

The blank-destination transport payment was another separate question.

The old payment entry bearing Mingzhao's name was yet another.

Mingyuan had learned that placing three unresolved matters on one table did not make them one matter.

He had also learned that the more closely he examined the records, the less comfortable it became to offer a quick explanation.

"Have the transport office send the roster and any surviving notebook references," he said. "If there is no notebook, I want the record to say that no notebook was located. I do not want a blank space quietly replaced with a guess."

Liang Wen recorded the instruction.

Steward Zhou looked at the pages again.

"Should we ask Zhang to identify Li Shun's handwriting once more?"

"No. He has already told us what he can identify. We should not ask him the same question until his answer becomes the one we want."

The steward's expression shifted.

Mingyuan continued.

"If the transport roster identifies Li Shun, we can establish that he was assigned to the duty. If another record identifies the writer, we can establish that. Neither fact alone will tell us what the carts carried."

"Understood."

Mingyuan gathered the copies.

The investigation was moving, but it was not approaching a conclusion as quickly as he would have liked.

He disliked that.

He disliked even more the possibility that the estate had become accustomed to treating incomplete records as sufficient because nobody had been willing to ask what the missing lines meant.

In the afternoon, Mingzhao received a letter from the Marquisate.

It was not a summons.

That alone made her suspicious.

She read the first page carefully.

Her father had acknowledged her conditions concerning the next property-division discussion. The family would prepare a separate schedule of her personal claims, the confirmed advances, the disputed entries, and the estate property still requiring classification.

The proposed discussion would not include the routine administration of the estate.

It would not ask her to resume purchasing, supervise the servants, or take responsibility for current accounts.

The letter also stated that the family intended to provide copies of the supporting documents before proposing a meeting date.

Mingzhao read it again.

Then she placed it beside the latest property schedule.

The figures had not changed.

Nine advances were confirmed.

Four additional payments were confirmed in the books, but their repayment arrangements remained unclear.

Ten entries were still uncertain.

The seventy-two taels spent on repairs to the study remained classified as estate maintenance paid from her personal funds, with reimbursement still undecided.

The additional two hundred and twenty-eight taels remained unverified and disputed.

The furnishing payment had been restored to estate expenditure because it had been paid from the estate furnishing account. Her name as account manager did not make the expenditure her personal property.

The northern canal property remained outside the family division. She had not purchased it, and the boundary question had not been resolved.

Mingzhao put the letter down.

Her father had finally written something that resembled an answer to the question she had asked.

That did not mean the property division was finished.

It meant the family might be preparing to discuss it properly.

Qiao Yue entered with a small tray.

"Did the Marquis send another request?"

"He sent a letter acknowledging the previous one."

"Is that good?"

"It is better than receiving an invitation without an agenda."

Qiao Yue placed the tray down.

"Will you attend the discussion?"

"When there are documents to review and a date to discuss."

"And if they send the documents?"

"I will read them."

"Even if there are many?"

Mingzhao looked at her.

"Especially if there are many."

Qiao Yue nodded.

Mingzhao took a sheet of paper and began drafting a reply.

She thanked her father for confirming the proposed scope of the discussion. She would review the documents once copies were prepared. She also repeated that questions concerning past authorizations should be submitted separately with the relevant records, rather than added to the property-division discussion without notice.

She did not repeat that she would not resume estate management.

She had already said it.

Repeating a boundary was sometimes necessary, but she did not intend to turn every letter into a contest over the same sentence.

When she finished, she read the reply once, sealed it, and handed it to the correspondence servant.

"Send this tomorrow morning."

"Yes, Madam."

Mingzhao returned to the property schedule.

She had not won the division.

She had not received the money.

She had not even been given a meeting date.

But for the first time, the family had begun preparing a discussion according to the limits she had set.

It was a small change.

Small changes, she had discovered, were often the only kind that could be trusted.

Near sunset, the workshop sent its inspection report.

The two bolts with tighter edges had been compared against the approved sample.

One was within the permitted variation.

The other required a minor correction.

The correction had been completed, and both bolts had passed the second inspection.

The record included the names of the finishing worker and inspector, the time of correction, and the final result.

Mingzhao read it carefully.

She checked the quantities.

Ten bolts completed and passed.

Twenty bolts in progress.

No material loss recorded.

No buyer inspection yet.

No change to the delivery schedule.

She placed the report with the contract papers.

Qiao Yue, who had been sorting correspondence, glanced at the page.

"Everything is in order?"

"Everything recorded is in order."

"That sounds less reassuring than saying everything is finished."

"Because it is not finished."

Mingzhao folded the report.

"The buyer has not inspected anything. We have not delivered anything. We have received the deposit, not the balance. There is still work to do."

Qiao Yue smiled faintly.

"Then I will not congratulate you yet."

"Good."

Mingzhao returned to her desk.

Outside, the eastern market was gradually settling. Shopkeepers were drawing in their signs, porters were returning empty baskets, and the evening carts were moving more slowly through the crowded lanes.

Mingzhao looked at the workshop schedule once more.

Twenty-four days.

The first ten bolts were complete.

The next twenty were underway.

She had a written contract, an approved sample, a received deposit, and a workshop that was beginning to understand the difference between finishing work and proving that the work had been finished correctly.

It was not a large enterprise.

It was not yet a secure one.

But it was hers.

And, more importantly, it did not require her to stand beside every worker for the entire day.

She put the schedule away.

That evening, Xu Yuru reached the end of the chapter on servants' duties.

The author had concluded with a warning that servants should never be allowed to decide the household's arrangements without supervision.

Yuru considered the sentence.

Then she turned back several pages and looked at the earlier recommendation that responsibilities be divided clearly.

"Fang Ru."

"Yes, Madam?"

"Do you think the author has contradicted himself?"

Fang Ru came closer.

"Which part?"

"He says the servants should understand their duties and be responsible for them. Then he says the household head must personally decide everything."

Fang Ru looked at the page.

"Perhaps he believes servants can carry out instructions but should not make decisions."

"Then he has not delegated responsibility. He has delegated movement."

Fang Ru was silent for a moment.

Then she said, "That would explain why the household head never gets to rest."

Yuru smiled.

She placed a marker at the end of the chapter.

Her father had chosen an interesting book.

He had also chosen a book that was occasionally wrong.

That was perhaps why she enjoyed reading it.

She did not have to agree with every page simply because it had been written by someone who sounded authoritative.

Yuru closed the book and looked toward the window.

The evening was quiet.

There were still matters she missed. She missed the certainty of a completed task, the satisfaction of a resolved dispute, and the knowledge that a difficult arrangement had finally been put in order.

But she no longer missed being the person who had to put every arrangement in order.

For tonight, the book could remain on the table.

The palace could manage its own shelves.

The Marquisate could manage its own accounts.

And the workshop could finish its next twenty bolts.

It was, by the standards of the people who had once depended on her, an alarming amount of independence.

Xu Yuru thought it sounded quite pleasant.
