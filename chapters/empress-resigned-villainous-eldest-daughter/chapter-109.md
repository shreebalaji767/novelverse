# **Chapter 109: The Former Empress Has Discovered That Pruning Is Best Left to Someone Who Knows the Tree, While Her Father Has Discovered That Lending Books Does Not Require Sending the Entire Shelf. The Marquis's Eldest Daughter Has Received an Offer That Contains More Conditions Than Promises and Has Decided That a Busy Workshop Is Not Necessarily a Successful One. The Marquis's Heir Has Found Two Possible Drivers, Neither of Whom Has Yet Been Proven to Have Driven Anything.**

**13th Day of the Seventh Month, 18th Year of Yonghe.**

Xu Yuru read the section on pruning over breakfast.

The author was less unreasonable than she had feared.

He did not recommend cutting branches simply because they were untidy. Instead, he explained that a gardener should first identify which branches were dead, which were damaged, and which were competing for space. He warned against removing too much at once, particularly from a young tree.

Yuru approved.

She turned the page.

The next illustration showed a tree with several branches crossing one another. The author had marked the branches that should be removed and explained why.

Fang Ru placed the breakfast tray beside her.

"Is the book still interesting?"

"Yes."

"Does it say how often the trees should be examined?"

"It says that the gardener should observe them regularly."

Fang Ru waited.

Yuru looked up.

"That is not an invitation for me to begin a schedule."

"I did not say it was."

"You were thinking it."

"I was thinking that the author has finally found a way to recommend regular attention without requiring the reader to become the gardener."

Yuru considered the remark.

"That is an improvement."

She took a piece of fruit.

The garden outside was already active. A worker was carrying water toward the flower beds, while another was clearing fallen leaves from the path. The older gardener stood near the wall, speaking to a young man who held a pruning knife.

Yuru watched briefly.

The older man demonstrated how to hold the tool and pointed toward a branch.

The younger worker listened carefully.

Yuru returned to her breakfast.

There was something satisfying about seeing knowledge passed from one person to another without a report being prepared for her.

She had spent years ensuring that people understood their duties. She had also spent years stepping in when they failed to perform them.

The two habits had become difficult to separate.

Now she could observe a lesson being taught without deciding whether the teacher had chosen the correct words.

She finished breakfast and closed the book.

Fang Ru collected the tray.

"Would you like to walk in the courtyard?"

"Yes. After I finish this chapter."

Fang Ru glanced at the open pages.

"That may take some time."

"Then the courtyard can wait."

At the eastern market, Mingzhao received a second message from Luo Cheng.

The trader had not supplied the buyer's name.

He had, however, proposed a revised arrangement.

The raw silk would be delivered to the workshop and weighed in the presence of both parties. Its value would be recorded according to the market price on the day of delivery. The buyer would pay for processing after inspection, but only once the goods had been accepted by the final customer.

Mingzhao read the proposal.

Qiao Yue stood beside the desk.

"That is more specific than the first message."

"Yes."

"Does it make the offer acceptable?"

"No."

Qiao Yue did not look surprised.

Mingzhao placed the letter beside the earlier one.

The trader had improved the material valuation. That was useful. But the buyer remained unnamed, the final inspection was outside the workshop's control, and the payment date depended on a transaction in which Mingzhao had no part.

The workshop would be taking responsibility for material it did not own, labour it had to pay immediately, and goods whose payment might be delayed indefinitely.

The trader had improved one part of the proposal.

He had not improved the part that mattered most.

"Write that we can consider the processing fee separately from the value of the silk," Mingzhao said. "But payment for the processing must be due upon our buyer's inspection, not upon the buyer's resale."

Qiao Yue wrote it down.

"Should we ask for a deposit?"

"Yes. A processing deposit sufficient to cover the initial labour and dye expenses."

"And if he refuses?"

"Then there is no agreement."

Qiao Yue folded the reply.

Mingzhao turned to the workshop estimates.

The foreman's first estimate showed the cost of maintaining the present workforce. The second showed the limited production possible with existing materials. The third included replenishment.

She had not yet decided whether to use the second estimate to produce a small quantity for ordinary market sale.

That would require finding a buyer before committing too much stock.

It might be possible to produce a few bolts in a commonly requested colour and width, but the market price fluctuated. A finished bolt that could not be sold quickly would tie up money.

Mingzhao drew a line beneath the second estimate.

"Ask the foreman to identify which specifications have sold most consistently during the last six months."

Qiao Yue looked at the instruction.

"Not which ones brought the highest price?"

"Not yet. A high price is of little use if the goods remain in storage."

"Should he include the merchants who purchased them?"

"Yes. We need to know whether the demand came from several buyers or one unusually large order."

Qiao Yue nodded.

Mingzhao set the papers aside.

This was a more useful task than simply telling the workshop to prepare for another large order.

The workshop needed to know what it could sell, not merely what it could produce.

At the Marquisate, Mingyuan received a message from the transport office.

The office had located a wage list for the relevant season.

It contained a driver named Sun Shou, who had been employed during the month of the four-cart movement. A second entry listed Wei Zhen, whose employment had ended shortly before the recorded dispatch date.

The wage list did not identify the carts assigned to either man.

There was no surviving daily assignment slip attached.

Mingyuan read the names carefully.

"Sun Shou was employed during the period," he said.

Liang Wen nodded.

"Yes."

"Wei Zhen had already left?"

"According to the wage list, his final payment was made several days before the dispatch entry."

"Then he may not have been available."

"That is possible."

Mingyuan looked at the paper.

It would have been easy to strike Wei Zhen's name from the inquiry and write that Sun Shou was the driver.

But the wage list only showed that one man was employed and the other had apparently left. It did not prove who drove the carts.

"Ask whether Sun Shou was assigned to northern-road work during that week," Mingyuan said. "Also ask whether Wei Zhen was retained temporarily or paid for any later work."

Liang Wen wrote it down.

"Should we ask the office whether Sun Shou remembers the dispatch?"

"Yes, if he can be located. But first confirm that this is the correct man."

Liang Wen nodded.

Mingyuan returned the wage list.

"Do we have the original?"

"No. The office sent a copied extract."

"Record that."

"Yes."

Mingyuan leaned back.

The inquiry was narrowing, but it had not yet reached the carts.

He wondered how many people had handled the movement entry. Li Shun had prepared it, or at least remembered preparing it. Someone had instructed the dispatch through the transport supervisor's office. Two possible drivers had been named. The notebook's custody remained uncertain.

A record that should have taken a few lines to complete had become a collection of questions.

He opened the folder and checked the heading.

**Four-Cart Movement: Dispatch Entry and Custody of Notebook.**

Beneath it, he added:

**Possible driver identified in wage record: Sun Shou. Assignment not confirmed.**

He stopped there.

The words were not satisfying.

They were accurate.

Mingzhao's reply to Luo Cheng returned before midday.

The trader had accepted the idea of a processing deposit but wanted the payment to be made after the final buyer had inspected the finished goods.

Mingzhao read the message and put it down.

Qiao Yue waited.

"Is he refusing?"

"He is repeating the same condition in different words."

"Should we reply again?"

"No."

"Then the matter is finished?"

"Unless he sends a different proposal."

Qiao Yue gathered the papers.

Mingzhao looked at her.

"Do not mark it as a rejected order."

"What should I write?"

"Unaccepted inquiry. Terms not agreed."

Qiao Yue paused.

"Is there a difference?"

"Yes. We never accepted the order."

She wrote the words.

Mingzhao watched her finish.

It was a small distinction, but one that mattered in a business record. A rejected order implied that an agreement had been offered and refused. An unaccepted inquiry meant that the parties had not reached the point of agreement.

The workshop had not failed to complete anything.

It had not promised anything.

That was enough.

Qiao Yue placed the paper in the correspondence file.

"Miss, the foreman has asked whether the workers should be told that there may be no new order this month."

"Tell them the completed order has been paid and that the workshop is reviewing its next work. Do not promise a date."

"Will they be concerned?"

"Possibly."

"Some may seek work elsewhere."

"That is their choice. We will not ask them to remain by promising work we do not have."

Qiao Yue nodded.

Mingzhao returned to the market estimates.

She understood the cost of idle capacity.

She also understood the cost of keeping people by making promises that could not be honoured.

For six years, the Marquisate had expected her to absorb uncertainty on behalf of the household. She had arranged payments, found substitutes, negotiated delays, and made difficult matters appear orderly.

The workshop was not going to operate on the same principle.

In the palace, a small administrative matter was brought to the Emperor's attention.

A supply office had submitted a request concerning the replacement of several storage shelves. The request had been properly reviewed by the relevant palace administrator, but one of the attached estimates was incomplete.

Gao Shun presented the papers.

"The administrator has asked whether the request should be returned for a revised estimate or whether the work may proceed using the lower figure."

Zhao Jingheng examined the documents.

"Why is the estimate incomplete?"

"The carpenter's office has included the cost of timber but not the cost of transport."

"Then the lower figure is not a complete estimate."

"No, Your Majesty."

"Return it."

Gao Shun accepted the papers.

The Emperor looked at the next document.

"Has the administrator recommended which shelves should be replaced first?"

"Yes. The damaged shelves in the western storehouse have been listed as urgent. The others may wait."

"Then let the urgent repairs proceed after the transport cost is confirmed. The remaining shelves can be included in the next maintenance schedule."

Gao Shun bowed.

There was no mention of Xu Yuru.

The Emperor signed the instruction and returned the papers.

Gao Shun gathered them.

"Anything else?" Zhao Jingheng asked.

"No, Your Majesty."

The Emperor glanced toward the side table.

There was a small space where the former Empress's usual daily summaries had once been placed.

It had remained empty for several months.

At first, the absence had made the work feel less orderly. Later, the administrators had learned to prepare their own summaries and identify which decisions required imperial approval.

The Emperor had not become less informed.

He had simply stopped receiving every matter through one person.

He returned to the next document.

At the Duke Xu residence, Madam Xu received a message from her daughter.

Yuru had thanked her for the meal and said that she had returned safely.

The note was brief.

Madam Xu read it twice.

Duke Xu, seated nearby, glanced up.

"Has she written?"

"Yes."

"What does she say?"

"That she has returned safely."

"Good."

Madam Xu placed the note beside the tea tray.

Duke Xu returned to his book.

After a moment, he asked, "Did she say whether she finished the garden chapter?"

"No."

"Perhaps she has not."

"Perhaps."

"She may need another book."

Madam Xu looked at him.

"She has two borrowed books."

"One is nearly finished."

"You do not know that."

"She has been reading regularly."

"That does not mean she requires another."

Duke Xu considered the matter.

"Then I shall wait."

Madam Xu nodded.

This was the correct decision.

It was also the first time in several weeks that the Duke had accepted a restriction on lending books without proposing an alternative.

He looked down at the page.

The book was about provincial granaries.

He had intended to lend it to Yuru.

He decided to wait until she asked.

In the western guest residence, Yuru took a walk through the courtyard after the midday meal.

The gardener was trimming a damaged branch from a shrub near the path.

He noticed her and bowed.

"Madam."

Yuru returned the greeting.

"Is that branch diseased?"

"Not diseased, Madam. It was damaged during the rain."

"Will removing it help the rest of the shrub?"

"Yes. The break has begun to split further."

Yuru examined the branch.

It was cracked near the base.

"Then it is sensible to remove it."

The gardener bowed again and returned to his work.

Yuru continued along the path.

Fang Ru walked beside her.

"You did not ask him to explain the rest of the pruning."

"No."

"Are you not curious?"

"I am curious."

Fang Ru waited.

Yuru smiled.

"Curiosity does not require an interrogation."

They passed beneath the shade of a tree.

The courtyard was not especially large. It had a few shrubs, a small tree near the wall, and a narrow bed of flowers. It was not the sort of garden that would have impressed visiting officials or required a formal description in a household inventory.

Yuru liked it.

She liked that it had no ceremonial purpose.

She liked that the gardener could decide what needed attention without first receiving a written order from the former mistress of the inner palace.

She liked that she could walk through it without being asked whether the flowers had been arranged to suit a visitor.

At the far end of the path, a fallen leaf lay across the stones.

Yuru stepped over it.

The gardener would collect it later.

At the Marquisate, Mingyuan received another note from the transport office.

The office confirmed that Sun Shou had been employed during the relevant month. It could not establish whether he had been assigned to the four-cart movement. The daily assignment slips for that week had not been found.

There was also no record confirming that Wei Zhen had returned for temporary work.

Mingyuan read the note.

Liang Wen stood nearby.

"Does this identify the driver?"

"No."

"Does it rule out Sun Shou?"

"No. It confirms only that he was employed."

"Then what should be entered?"

Mingyuan took the pen.

He wrote:

**Sun Shou was employed during the relevant month. No surviving assignment record links him to the four-cart dispatch. Wei Zhen's service ended before the recorded date; no later temporary employment has been confirmed.**

He handed the paper to Liang Wen.

"Attach the copied wage extract and the transport office's reply."

"Yes."

"Then we will have to look for the dispatch order itself."

Liang Wen nodded.

"Should we ask the former supervisor again?"

"Yes. Ask whether the office retained a separate order book or whether dispatch instructions were recorded only in the movement notebook."

"Which supervisor?"

"Both, if they are willing to answer."

Liang Wen collected the papers.

Mingyuan looked at the folder.

He was beginning to see that the four carts might never be fully explained.

That possibility troubled him.

But it troubled him less than the thought of declaring the matter settled without knowing what had happened.

He placed the folder beside the grain discrepancy file.

The two remained separate.

That evening, Mingzhao reviewed the workshop's market summary.

The foreman had identified three specifications that had sold consistently over the previous six months. Two had been purchased by several merchants. The third had been bought mainly by one large trader.

The highest-priced specification was not the most reliable seller.

Mingzhao marked the first two.

"These may be suitable for a small production trial," she said.

Qiao Yue looked at the figures.

"How many bolts?"

"Not yet."

"Should we ask the merchants?"

"Yes. Ask whether they expect to purchase again and what quantity they would consider. Do not offer a price until we know the current cost of materials."

Qiao Yue nodded.

"Would you like the foreman to prepare samples?"

"Only after the material estimates are confirmed."

"That may take several days."

"Then it will take several days."

Qiao Yue gathered the papers.

Mingzhao closed the workshop account book.

The completed order remained profitable.

The next transaction remained uncertain.

She was not troubled by the distinction.

A business that could refuse a poor arrangement and wait for a better one was in a stronger position than a business that had to accept every offer merely to remain occupied.

She looked at the closed account once more.

The final figure was modest.

The account was clear.

For now, that was enough.

Before retiring, Xu Yuru returned to her garden book.

She read the last paragraph of the pruning chapter.

The author advised that a gardener should remove only what was necessary and should allow the remaining branches time to recover.

Yuru placed the book on the table.

She did not write a note.

She did not prepare a list of questions for the gardener.

She simply closed the cover.

In another part of Yongjing, Mingzhao had finished reviewing the workshop's sales information. She had identified two possible products for a small trial but had not authorized production.

At the Marquisate, Mingyuan had recorded that the possible driver Sun Shou remained unconfirmed and that the daily assignment slips were missing.

The former Empress had decided that a damaged branch could be removed by the gardener.

The workshop owner had decided that a possible order could wait until its terms were sound.

The Marquis's heir had decided that a possible driver could not be entered as a confirmed one.

Three decisions had been made.

None had required anyone to pretend that an unfinished matter was complete.
