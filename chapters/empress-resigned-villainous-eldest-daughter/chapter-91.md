# **Chapter 91: The Former Empress Has Discovered That a Book on Provincial Transport Contains More Sensible Advice About Responsibility Than a Book on Household Management. The Marquis's Eldest Daughter Has Received a Merchant's Revised Contract and Has Discovered That a Better Delivery Schedule Cannot Repair a Poorly Defined Inspection Clause. The Marquis's Heir Has Found Four Carts in an Old Register and Has Been Warned That Four Carts Are Not Necessarily Twenty-Seven Sacks of Grain.**

**25th Day of the Sixth Month, 18th Year of Yonghe.**

Xu Yuru had not yet received the book about provincial transport.

She was beginning to suspect that her father had been waiting for her to finish the two books already in her possession before selecting another.

This was probably the result of her mother's intervention.

It was also probably necessary.

The book on provincial granaries had proved more interesting than she expected. Its author was particularly concerned with the difference between the movement of goods and the completion of a transaction.

Yuru had read the passage about transport losses the previous afternoon. That morning, she returned to it and examined the examples more closely.

One described a district that recorded grain as dispatched when the carts left the warehouse. Another recorded it as received only after the destination warehouse had counted and inspected the sacks.

The author argued that both entries were necessary.

If the first was omitted, the sending office could not establish what it had released. If the second was omitted, the receiving office could not establish what had arrived.

Yuru turned the page.

The author then complained that some officials considered the receiving record unnecessary because the destination warehouse was managed by a trusted subordinate.

Yuru stopped reading.

"Fang Ru."

"Yes, Madam?"

"Do you know what happens when a person is trusted with a task but no one records whether the task was completed?"

Fang Ru considered the question.

"People remember different things."

"Precisely."

"And when they disagree?"

"They ask the person who was originally responsible."

Fang Ru looked at the book.

"Which may be someone who is no longer responsible."

Yuru smiled.

"You have understood the problem."

She placed a narrow slip beside the paragraph.

The author was not arguing that people should distrust everyone. He was arguing that trust did not remove the need for evidence.

That distinction was worth remembering.

Yuru continued reading until the morning light shifted across the table.

She had no intention of turning the passage into an instruction for the palace. The book belonged to her father, and the observation belonged to her.

She could read something useful without becoming responsible for putting it into practice.

That, she thought, was a habit worth cultivating.

The revised letter from Gao Wen arrived at Shen Mingzhao's residence shortly before midday.

The merchant had accepted several of her proposed changes.

The warehouse was now identified as the eastern receiving storehouse in the River Quarter. The inspection period was limited to three days after delivery to the warehouse, and the correction period would be five days after inspection, and the buyer would pay the remaining balance upon delivery to the named warehouse, subject to the agreed inspection.

The delivery period had also been extended.

Mingzhao read the letter carefully.

He Zhen had added a sentence stating that the merchant would not demand corrections for variations outside the written specifications.

That was sensible.

He had also agreed that changes to the colour or weave after approval of the sample would require a separate agreement.

That was better.

But the letter still contained a problem.

Mingzhao called Qiao Yue.

"Bring the original sample and the workshop report."

Qiao Yue placed both on the table.

Mingzhao compared the merchant's new wording with the workshop's proposed production schedule.

The merchant had accepted twenty-four days after approval of the trial sample and receipt of the deposit.

However, the inspection clause allowed three days after delivery to the warehouse, while the payment clause stated that the balance would be paid after inspection.

There was no provision for what would happen if the buyer failed to inspect within those three days.

Mingzhao drew a line beneath the sentence.

"Qiao Yue, send a message to He Zhen."

"What should it say?"

"Ask whether the balance becomes payable automatically if the buyer does not complete inspection within three days and has not submitted a written defect notice."

Qiao Yue wrote it down.

"Would the merchant agree?"

"He should, if he intends to follow the terms he has proposed."

Mingzhao looked again at the sample.

The silk itself was promising. The deposit was substantial. The delivery period was realistic, provided the trial batch passed examination.

The merchant had already accepted most of the safeguards.

There was no reason to reject the proposal.

There was also no reason to sign it before the final ambiguity was resolved.

"Send the question today," she said. "We will not begin production until the sample is approved and the contract is signed."

Qiao Yue folded the message.

"Should I tell Lin Shuyun to begin the trial?"

"She can prepare the materials, but no production should be counted as authorized work until the agreement is complete."

"That may delay the order."

"It may."

Mingzhao put the sample away.

"A delay before signing is less expensive than a dispute after delivery."

At the workshop, Lin Shuyun had already begun preparing for the possible trial.

She had examined the sample with the senior weaver and the dyer. Both agreed that the requested colour could probably be reproduced, but the difference between the merchant's sample and the workshop's existing stock was greater than it first appeared.

The dyeing process would need a separate trial.

The senior weaver had also found that the sample's thread tension was slightly tighter than the workshop's ordinary standard.

"Can we reproduce it?" Mingzhao asked.

"Yes, but we should not promise the full order until the trial is examined."

"How long will the trial take?"

"Two days, perhaps three, depending on the dye."

Mingzhao considered this.

"Then the delivery period should begin only after the trial is approved."

"It already does."

"Good."

Lin Shuyun showed her the revised production schedule.

The first stage would use a limited quantity of material. The dyer would prepare the colour, and the senior weaver would produce a small sample section. They would compare it with Gao Wen's original piece under daylight and indoor light.

If it passed, the workshop would prepare the full quantity.

The first ten bolts would be inspected separately before the remaining production continued.

Mingzhao looked at the schedule.

"Why ten?"

"Enough to establish whether the method is consistent without allowing a mistake to spread through the entire order."

"That is reasonable."

Lin Shuyun pointed to the workers' assignments.

"The trainees can assist with preparation and folding, but the senior workers will handle the parts requiring the most consistency. I have not counted the trainees as full finishers."

"Keep it that way."

Lin Shuyun nodded.

Mingzhao returned the schedule.

"Prepare the trial only after the contract is complete."

"You are very determined not to begin."

"I am determined not to confuse preparation with authorization."

Lin Shuyun smiled.

"That distinction seems to be spreading."

"It is a useful distinction."

"Some people may begin charging for it."

"Then we should not put it on the workshop door."

Lin Shuyun laughed.

At the Marquisate, Mingyuan was examining the certified copy of the four-cart entry.

The handwriting was still uncertain.

The first name might have been Zhao Fusheng.

The second character could also have been written as *Shun*.

The clerk had found an older transport list containing a Zhao Fusheng, but the list belonged to a different year. There was no proof that the same man had driven the carts in the record under examination.

Mingyuan placed the two documents side by side.

"Have you checked whether the older list refers to the same warehouse?"

"Yes, Young Master. It names the northern warehouse."

"And the same road?"

"The road is similar, but the destination is not recorded."

"Then it is a possible identification, not a confirmed one."

The clerk bowed.

Mingyuan wrote a note on the copy.

*Driver's name uncertain. Older list contains a person with a similar name, but identity and connection to this movement are not established.*

Steward Zhou, standing beside the desk, read it.

"You could have simply written that the driver was probably Zhao Fusheng."

"Probably is not a name."

"It is a useful word."

"It is useful when the evidence supports it."

The steward sighed.

"Your father used to settle these questions more quickly."

Mingyuan looked up.

"Did he settle them correctly?"

Steward Zhou did not answer at once.

"Sometimes."

"And the other times?"

"The records are why we are discussing them now."

Mingyuan nodded.

"Then perhaps we should be careful."

He placed the four-cart entry in the inquiry folder.

The old warehouse steward Zhang would be asked to examine the handwriting without being told the suspected name. If he recognized it, his response would be recorded as recollection unless supported by another document.

The grain figures remained unchanged.

Forty-seven sacks had been recorded in the original entry.

Twenty had been included in the later instruction.

Eighteen had confirmed receipts.

Two remained unconfirmed.

Twenty-seven still lacked an established destination.

The four-cart entry might be relevant.

It might not.

Mingyuan was beginning to understand that an investigation could produce more documents without producing a conclusion.

That was frustrating.

It was also preferable to pretending.

The Marquis's household accounts office was less pleased with this approach.

A clerk named Zhao had been assigned to compile a summary of unresolved matters. He had written the four-cart entry under the heading *Possible Grain Transport*.

Mingyuan crossed out the word *Grain*.

The clerk stared at the correction.

"But it was from the northern warehouse."

"That does not establish the cargo."

"Four carts were dispatched."

"That establishes the carts."

"The grain discrepancy occurred during the same period."

"That establishes the period."

The clerk looked at the page.

"Then what should I call it?"

"An unidentified transport movement from the northern warehouse."

The clerk wrote the new heading.

"That is longer."

"It is also accurate."

Steward Zhou looked as though he wanted to say something.

Mingyuan added, "If we later establish that it carried grain, we can change the heading with a dated correction."

The clerk nodded reluctantly.

Mingyuan continued through the summary.

The old purchasing payment remained unresolved. The surviving ledger entry bore the estate purchasing seal, but the original voucher had not been found. The seal register named Chen Rui as custodian on the relevant date. The recipient and purpose remained unknown.

Mingzhao's response had been preserved.

She did not recognize the entry, did not remember authorizing the payment, and could not identify its recipient or purpose.

No further question had been sent to her.

The clerk pointed to the entry.

"Should we place it under her personal advances?"

"No."

"Because she denies it?"

"Because we do not have evidence that it was an advance to her."

The clerk withdrew his hand.

Mingyuan returned the summary.

"Keep it in unresolved purchasing payments."

He had learned that a document could be made to say almost anything if someone was sufficiently eager to classify it.

He did not intend to let the accounts office do that.

Mingzhao received He Zhen's answer late in the afternoon.

Gao Wen agreed that if the buyer did not inspect the goods within three days and had not submitted a written defect notice, the balance would become payable on the fourth day.

The merchant also agreed that the buyer's warehouse would issue a signed receipt upon delivery.

Mingzhao read the reply.

Then she called Qiao Yue.

"Ask the agent to prepare the final contract with these terms included."

"Does that mean we are accepting?"

"It means we are ready to review the final document."

Qiao Yue smiled.

"That is not quite the same thing."

"No. It is not."

The final contract would need to identify the sample, attach the written specifications, state the delivery address, define the inspection and correction periods, and establish the payment schedule.

The deposit would be paid before production began.

The balance would become due after inspection or the expiration of the agreed inspection period without a written defect notice.

The workshop's capacity report had already established that the schedule was feasible if the trial passed.

There was now a reasonable basis for proceeding.

But Mingzhao still wanted to examine the contract itself.

She placed the correspondence in the order file.

For the first time since receiving Gao Wen's inquiry, she felt that the proposal might become a genuine opportunity.

She did not feel compelled to accept it immediately.

That was a more useful feeling than excitement.

In the palace guest residence, Yuru received a second letter from her mother.

This one concerned the provincial transport book her father had selected.

Madam Xu wrote that Duke Xu had put it aside and would wait until Yuru returned the two books already borrowed. She added that he had asked whether Yuru had found the household management book useful.

Yuru read the question aloud.

Fang Ru was arranging the tea tray.

"What did you tell him in your last letter?" she asked.

"That I disagreed with the author about personal roof inspections."

Fang Ru's expression became carefully neutral.

"Then he may be asking whether you have found anything else useful."

"Perhaps."

Yuru took up her writing brush.

She wrote that the book had some sensible passages, particularly those concerning the responsibilities of household stewards. She added that the author's recommendation for personal inspection of every roof remained excessive.

Then she paused.

She could almost imagine her father reading the letter and asking whether she had marked the passage.

He would probably want to discuss it.

He might even find the author's argument persuasive.

Yuru smiled and added one more sentence:

*If you disagree with me, please do not send a carpenter to prove the point.*

Fang Ru looked at the finished letter.

"Would the Duke do that?"

"He might consider it."

"Would Madam Xu permit it?"

"That is why I have written to my mother."

Fang Ru smiled.

Yuru sealed the letter.

It was a small exchange, entirely personal, and no one had asked her to resolve anything.

She returned to the provincial granary book.

The next chapter concerned the appointment of warehouse supervisors.

The author recommended that supervisors be evaluated not only by the quantity of grain they stored, but also by the accuracy of their records, the condition of the grain, and the timely completion of distributions.

Yuru read the passage slowly.

A warehouse could show a large quantity on paper while its grain deteriorated in damp rooms. A supervisor could deliver grain promptly while failing to record the actual quantity. Another might maintain perfect registers while allowing the distribution schedule to collapse.

No single measure was sufficient.

She marked the paragraph.

Then she closed the book.

The afternoon had passed without a document arriving from the palace.

She was pleased.

That evening, Mingzhao reviewed the final contract draft sent by Gao Wen's agent.

It was substantially improved.

The named warehouse was included. The inspection period was three days. The correction period was five. The delivery period began after the trial sample was approved and the deposit received. The payment terms specified the fourth-day obligation if the buyer failed to inspect or submit a defect notice.

The sample was attached and identified.

The specifications described the weave, colour standard, dimensions, and acceptable variation.

Mingzhao checked the clauses one by one.

Then she noticed that the contract described the deposit as payable "upon execution."

She looked at the previous letter.

The merchant had promised that the deposit would be paid before production began.

The two statements were not contradictory, but the timing could be clearer.

She added a small amendment.

*Production shall begin only after the deposit has been received in full.*

She also added that the deposit receipt would be recorded separately from the production authorization.

Qiao Yue read the amendment.

"Is that necessary?"

"Yes."

"The contract already says the deposit is payable upon execution."

"And the workshop should not begin merely because the contract has been signed. The money must arrive."

Qiao Yue nodded.

Mingzhao reviewed the rest of the document.

There were no further changes.

She placed the contract in a separate folder marked *Awaiting Final Execution*.

The order was not yet active.

The trial had not yet passed.

The deposit had not yet arrived.

The workshop had not yet received authorization.

Every part of the process remained in its proper place.

Mingzhao put away the documents.

She had once spent years ensuring that other people completed their responsibilities, often without receiving clear evidence that they had done so.

Now she was learning to make agreements that did not depend on her remembering everything for everyone else.

It was not an easy habit.

But it was becoming a useful one.

And, unlike the author's recommendation to inspect every roof, it did not require climbing anything.
