# **Chapter 93: The Former Empress Has Discovered That the Best Part of Reading About Provincial Granaries Is That No One Expects Her to Repair One. The Marquis's Eldest Daughter Has Received the First Trial Silk and Has Discovered That a Beautiful Sample Can Still Fail a Measurement. The Marquis's Heir Has Found the Warehouse Dispatch Archive and Has Learned That an Old Notebook May Contain More Questions Than Answers.**

**27th Day of the Sixth Month, 18th Year of Yonghe.**

Xu Yuru had reached the final section of the provincial granary book.

The author had moved from storage and inspection to the problem of distribution. He argued that a granary could not be judged solely by the quantity it held. It also had to release grain when needed, maintain accurate records, and preserve enough stock to meet its obligations.

Yuru read the chapter twice.

The author had included a particularly irritating example of a district official who boasted of maintaining a large reserve while refusing to release grain during a shortage because he feared the reduction would make his annual figures look less impressive.

The author called this a failure of purpose.

Yuru agreed.

A granary existed to store grain for use, not to produce impressive numbers in a report.

She turned the page.

The next passage concerned the difference between a reserve and an unused surplus. A reserve was held for an established purpose. A surplus was what remained after the required obligations had been met.

The distinction seemed obvious until the author described a district where the same quantity had been counted as both.

Yuru smiled.

"Fang Ru."

"Yes, Madam?"

"If someone counts the same grain twice, does it become twice as useful?"

"No, Madam."

"Then why do people keep trying?"

Fang Ru thought about it.

"Perhaps because the second entry makes the first look better."

Yuru looked up.

"That is an uncomfortably practical answer."

Fang Ru lowered her eyes.

Yuru closed the book.

She had reached the final section, but she would finish it later. There was no need to hurry.

She had borrowed the books for three months. She had no intention of returning them tomorrow merely to prove that she could read quickly.

She placed the provincial granary book beside the household management book.

"Which one tomorrow?" Fang Ru asked.

"The household management book."

"Are you returning to the roof chapter?"

"No. I have already inspected that argument."

Fang Ru smiled.

Yuru picked up her tea.

"Tomorrow I will see whether the author has anything sensible to say about servants."

The first trial silk was ready shortly after midday.

Lin Shuyun sent word that the dyeing process had produced a colour close to the merchant's sample. The senior weaver had completed the small trial section, and both were waiting for Mingzhao to examine it.

Mingzhao arrived at the workshop with Qiao Yue.

The sample was spread across a clean table.

At first glance, it appeared excellent.

The colour was even. The surface was smooth. The weave was dense without appearing stiff.

Mingzhao examined it beside the merchant's original piece.

"Under daylight?" she asked.

Lin Shuyun moved the samples closer to the open side of the workshop.

They compared them again.

The colour was close, but the trial silk had a slightly warmer tone.

Mingzhao examined the edge.

"Measure the width."

The senior weaver brought the measuring cord.

The trial section was slightly narrower than the written specification.

Lin Shuyun looked at it.

"The difference is small."

"Is it within the agreed variation?"

Lin Shuyun checked the contract.

"No. The width is outside the permitted range."

"Then it does not pass."

The senior weaver looked disappointed.

"The difference can be corrected by adjusting the loom."

"Then adjust it."

"The colour may change slightly if we alter the tension."

"Then test it again."

Mingzhao did not raise her voice.

She examined the sample once more.

"It is a good piece of silk. It is not yet the agreed piece of silk."

Lin Shuyun nodded.

The dyer explained that the colour difference might have resulted from the first trial mixture being slightly too warm. He proposed a minor adjustment to the dye proportions.

The senior weaver wanted to adjust the loom tension separately.

Mingzhao listened to both proposals.

"Do not change both at once."

The two workers looked at her.

"If you change the dye and the loom simultaneously, you will not know which adjustment corrected the difference or whether one change caused another problem. Test the colour first. Then adjust the width."

Lin Shuyun nodded.

"That is sensible."

Mingzhao looked at the trial piece.

"Keep this sample. Mark it as the first trial, not as approved material."

Qiao Yue recorded the instruction.

The sample was labelled and placed in a separate cloth wrapping.

The trial had failed.

But it had failed before the workshop had produced eighty bolts.

That was precisely why the first sample existed.

At the Marquisate, Mingyuan received a report from the archive clerk.

The clerk had found a separate bundle of old warehouse dispatch records in a storage chest near the northern warehouse accounts.

It was not the complete transport notebook Zhang had described.

However, it contained several loose pages from the relevant period.

Mingyuan went to the archive himself.

The pages were arranged by date, although several had become detached from their original bindings. Some recorded the number of carts dispatched. Others listed the cargo, destination, and receiving official.

One page contained a movement of four carts.

The date was close to the date of the unexplained grain entry.

The cargo column had been left blank.

The destination column contained only a partial mark.

Mingyuan studied it.

"Can this mark be identified?"

The archive clerk shook his head.

"It may be a warehouse abbreviation, but I cannot confirm which one."

"Does the page have a receiving signature?"

"No."

"Any attached receipt?"

"Not in this bundle."

Mingyuan examined the reverse side.

There was a faint line of writing, partly obscured by an old fold.

He turned the page toward the light.

The words were incomplete, but one phrase remained legible:

*...to be confirmed upon arrival.*

Mingyuan looked at the clerk.

"Is this a dispatch record or a receipt?"

"A dispatch record, Young Master."

"Then the phrase does not prove arrival."

"No."

"Preserve it as it is. Make a certified copy, but do not fill in the missing destination."

The clerk bowed.

Mingyuan continued through the loose pages.

There were records of grain, timber, household furnishings, and repair materials. Several entries had been copied into later registers, but not all.

He found no complete record explaining the twenty-seven sacks.

He did find a separate receipt for a grain delivery of eighteen sacks.

The date matched one of the confirmed receipts already recorded.

Mingyuan checked the figures.

It did not establish anything new.

He put the page aside.

"Has Zhang been shown the handwriting samples?"

"Not yet. They are being prepared."

"Send them with the certified copies. Keep the original pages here."

Mingyuan returned to the four-cart record.

It was tempting to believe that the four carts had carried the missing grain.

But the page did not say so.

It did not name the cargo.

It did not establish the destination.

It did not prove that the carts had reached another warehouse.

He placed the page in the inquiry folder.

The archive had provided another piece of evidence.

It had not provided an answer.

The Marquis's household had also begun compiling the list of documents required for the property division.

Mingyuan reviewed the first draft with Steward Zhou.

The list separated the remaining matters into categories:

- Personal advances and their repayment status.
- The seventy-two taels of study repairs paid from Mingzhao's funds.
- The disputed two hundred and twenty-eight taels.
- The estate furnishing expenditure.
- Grain and transport discrepancies.
- The old purchasing payment with missing voucher.
- The northern canal property, which remained outside the family division.
- The western field cultivation arrangement.

Mingyuan checked each item.

The nine confirmed advances remained confirmed.

The four payment-confirmed entries still required clarification of repayment arrangements.

The ten uncertain entries remained uncertain.

The seventy-two taels remained estate maintenance paid from Mingzhao's personal funds, with reimbursement undecided.

The two hundred and twenty-eight taels remained unverified and disputed.

The furnishing expenditure remained in the estate accounts.

The canal property was not to be included in the family division merely because the family had discussed it.

Steward Zhou looked at the list.

"Your father may find this too long."

"It is shorter than the argument that will follow if we leave the matters mixed together."

The steward sighed.

"Your sister may also find it too long."

"She will have the same list we have."

"She may not enjoy reading it."

"That is not a reason to omit the documents."

Steward Zhou looked at him.

"You have become difficult."

Mingyuan continued checking the figures.

"I am learning."

The workshop's second trial was ready the following afternoon.

The dyer had adjusted the colour mixture without changing the loom settings.

The result was closer to the original sample.

The senior weaver then adjusted the loom tension and produced a second section.

This time, the width fell within the permitted range.

Mingzhao examined the silk in daylight.

She compared the colour, weave, edge, and dimensions.

Lin Shuyun waited beside her.

"Does it pass?"

Mingzhao took her time.

The colour was now within the permitted variation. The weave was consistent. The width met the written standard. The edges were even.

She turned the sample over.

"Yes."

Lin Shuyun smiled.

The dyer let out a breath.

The senior weaver looked considerably more pleased than he had after the first trial.

Mingzhao checked the attached record.

The first trial had been marked as rejected for colour and width. The second had been marked as approved, with the dye adjustment and loom setting recorded.

"Keep both samples," she said.

"Both?"

"Yes. The first shows what did not meet the specification. The second shows what was approved."

Lin Shuyun nodded.

"Then we can begin the first ten bolts."

"Yes. The first ten will be inspected separately before the remaining production is authorized."

The senior weaver looked at the approved sample.

"Now we can work."

"Now you can work under an agreed standard," Mingzhao replied.

Lin Shuyun laughed.

The workers began preparing the looms.

The eighty-bolt order had passed its first obstacle.

It had not yet become an easy order.

At the Marquisate, Zhang examined the certified handwriting samples.

He studied the pages for a long time.

Then he pointed to one.

"This one resembles the hand that wrote the four-cart entry."

"Can you identify the clerk?" the messenger asked.

"Possibly Li Shun. I worked with him, but it has been many years."

"Are you certain?"

"No."

"Can you explain why?"

Zhang placed the pages side by side.

"The way he writes the number four is similar. The character for cart is also similar. But another clerk could have learned the same writing style."

The messenger recorded his words.

Zhang added that Li Shun had worked in the northern warehouse office during the relevant period, but he could not remember whether he had been responsible for transport records on the specific date.

Mingyuan received the report that evening.

He read it carefully.

The handwriting was a possible match.

The clerk's identity remained unconfirmed.

The four carts still had no established cargo or destination.

The missing twenty-seven sacks remained unexplained.

Mingyuan wrote an instruction to search the personnel and assignment records for Li Shun, including any temporary transfers.

He also asked whether the loose dispatch pages could be matched with corresponding receiving records in another warehouse.

The investigation would continue.

He did not write that the matter had been solved.

That evening, Yuru returned to the household management book.

She had skipped the roof chapter as promised and reached the section on the responsibilities of household servants.

The author argued that servants should be given clear duties, but he also believed that a household head should remain informed about all important matters.

Yuru read the passage.

Then she read it again.

The author had not entirely abandoned his earlier position.

He still believed that a household head should personally oversee important decisions, though he now acknowledged that daily work should be delegated.

Yuru shook her head.

"Fang Ru."

"Yes, Madam?"

"The author has improved."

"Has he?"

"He now permits the household head to delegate ordinary work."

"That sounds like progress."

"He still expects the household head to know everything."

Fang Ru smiled.

"Perhaps he has not finished learning."

Yuru laughed softly.

"Perhaps."

She marked the passage, but this time she added a note in the margin of her own reading slip:

*Information should reach the responsible person. That does not mean every action must be performed by that person.*

She looked at the sentence.

It was not a new principle.

She had known it for years.

She had simply failed to apply it to herself.

Yuru placed the slip between the pages and closed the book.

Outside, the evening was quiet.

She had no need to report the passage to anyone.

She had no need to change the palace's procedures.

She could simply read, think, and decide what she believed.

That was enough for the day.

At the workshop, the first two bolts of Gao Wen's order had begun to take shape.

At the Marquisate, the archive clerks were preparing another search list.

At the guest residence, the former Empress had closed her book.

The merchant's order was underway, but the first ten bolts still required inspection.

The grain inquiry had gained another handwriting clue, but no destination.

The property division remained incomplete.

And the former Empress still had several chapters to read before returning her father's books.

For once, none of these unfinished matters belonged to the same person.

That was a small improvement in the affairs of Great Yong.
