# **Chapter 9: The Empress Has Been Asked to Explain the Difference Between Advice and Approval. The Marquis's Eldest Daughter Has Been Asked to Explain the Difference Between Leaving and Abandoning the Family.**

**1st Day of the Fourth Month, 18th Year of Yonghe.**

The first day of a new month was traditionally a day for reviewing accounts, confirming household allocations, and checking whether the previous month's arrangements had survived contact with reality.

At the imperial palace, it was also the day on which the emperor discovered that three offices had submitted three different versions of the same regulation.

At the Qingyuan Marquisate, it was the day on which Shen Mingzhao discovered that her family had invited a legal adviser, an accountant, and a senior clan representative to discuss her departure.

Neither woman was particularly pleased with the morning.

Emperor Zhao Jingheng placed three documents on his desk.

The first was from the Department of Rites.

The second was from the Department of Revenue.

The third was from the Imperial Household Administration.

All three concerned the same question:

Who could authorize exceptional expenditure within the inner palace?

The Department of Rites believed that ceremonial expenses should remain under its supervision.

The Department of Revenue believed that all exceptional expenditure should be subject to financial verification.

The Imperial Household Administration believed that the authority of the empress should be preserved because it had historically been exercised by her office.

The emperor read the three positions again.

Then he looked at Wei Zhong.

"Which one of them has answered the question?"

Wei Zhong considered the documents.

"Each has explained why the matter should remain under its own supervision."

"That is not an answer."

"No, Your Majesty."

The emperor tapped the third document.

"The Imperial Household Administration says the empress's approval should remain necessary."

"Yes."

"Has it explained whether that approval is administrative, financial, or ceremonial?"

"No."

"Then it has not explained anything."

Minister Lu, who had been standing quietly, cleared his throat.

"Your Majesty, the difficulty is that these matters have long been handled together."

"By the empress."

"Yes."

"And now she has asked to relinquish the responsibility."

"Yes."

"Then why are we attempting to preserve the same arrangement by changing the wording?"

Minister Lu lowered his head.

Wei Zhong opened a separate document.

"Your Majesty, the review office has prepared a distinction between four functions. First, the authority to request an expenditure. Second, the authority to approve the expenditure. Third, the authority to verify the payment. Fourth, the authority to inspect the records afterward."

The emperor looked at the page.

"Explain."

"An administrator of a palace residence may request a necessary repair. The office responsible for the relevant budget may determine whether funds are available. The designated financial officer may verify payment. The review office may inspect the records afterward."

"And ceremonial expenditure?"

"The Department of Rites may determine whether the expenditure is required for a ceremony under established regulations. It should not necessarily control the payment."

"And the empress?"

Wei Zhong paused.

"Her office may retain a defined supervisory role while the transition is being considered, but it should not automatically receive every request."

The emperor leaned back.

"Better."

Minister Lu looked uncomfortable.

"Your Majesty, if the empress's approval is no longer required, some officials may interpret that as a reduction in the authority of her position."

The emperor looked at him.

"Is her position the same as the authority to approve every purchase of lamp oil?"

Minister Lu did not answer.

"An empress is not diminished because a storekeeper learns how to count."

Wei Zhong lowered his eyes.

The emperor picked up the third document and crossed out a paragraph.

"Return this. Ask the Imperial Household Administration to identify the exact regulation under which the empress's approval is required. If there is no regulation, it should say so."

"Yes, Your Majesty."

"And do not send it to her for approval."

Wei Zhong bowed.

The emperor paused.

"Send her a copy for information."

Minister Lu glanced up.

The emperor noticed.

"Is there a problem?"

"No, Your Majesty."

"Good. Then we have made one decision today."

He looked at the three documents.

"Unfortunately, there are still two more versions of the same question."

At Chengming Palace, Yuru was receiving a report from Fang Ru when the emperor's message arrived.

She read the short note.

It informed her that the review of exceptional expenditure was continuing and that the relevant offices had been instructed to identify the legal basis for their existing procedures.

She set it down.

Fang Ru watched her.

"Your Majesty, the Department of Rites has also asked whether the empress will continue to supervise the preparations for the next seasonal ceremony."

"Have they submitted a request?"

"Yes."

"Under which regulation?"

"They have not specified one."

"Then return it."

Fang Ru hesitated.

"Should we explain that the matter is under review?"

"No. Ask them to identify the authority under which they are requesting supervision."

"Yes, Your Majesty."

Yuru turned to the next page of her register.

There were still responsibilities that could not be transferred immediately.

Some required the emperor's decision. Others required new procedures. A few depended on the personal judgment of senior administrators whose experience could not be replaced by a sentence in a regulation.

But there was a difference between a matter requiring careful supervision and a matter requiring Yuru to sign a document because nobody wanted to take responsibility.

She had begun to see that the distinction was more important than she had initially understood.

Fang Ru placed another report on the table.

"This concerns the palace children's seasonal clothing."

Yuru looked at it.

"Who prepared it?"

"The office responsible for the princes' and princesses' household supplies."

"Who verified the quantities?"

"The stores office."

"Who approved the expenditure?"

"The financial office."

"Then why is it here?"

"They have asked whether Your Majesty wishes to review the proposed colors."

Yuru stared at the report.

For a moment, she seemed unable to decide whether to laugh.

"Are the colors governed by a ceremonial rule?"

"Some are."

"Then the Department of Rites should identify the rule."

"Yes, Your Majesty."

"Are the children being dressed for a formal occasion?"

"No. These are ordinary seasonal garments."

"Then why do I need to approve the colors?"

Fang Ru lowered her head.

"I believe they are accustomed to asking."

Yuru closed the report.

"Then they may become accustomed to not asking."

Fang Ru took it away.

The empress looked toward the window.

It was only the first day of the fourth month.

She had not expected the palace to become efficient in eight days.

She had not expected it to become efficient in eight years.

Still, the documents were beginning to travel in different directions.

That was a beginning.

The Crown Prince had also received a copy of the new distinction between request, approval, verification, and review.

He read it in the Eastern Palace with his senior steward, Liang Fu.

"The financial office will verify the expenditure," Liang Fu said. "But who determines whether the repair is necessary?"

"The residence administrator should identify the problem."

"And if the administrator exaggerates the damage?"

"Then the stores office should inspect it before the expenditure is approved."

"And if the stores office disagrees?"

The Crown Prince put down the document.

"Then the disagreement should be recorded, with the reasons from both sides."

Liang Fu looked at him.

"That may take time."

"An unnecessary repair also takes money."

"Yes, Your Highness."

The Crown Prince returned to the document.

"How many requests for repair have we received this month?"

"Eleven."

"How many are urgent?"

"Three."

"Then deal with those three first. The others can be inspected according to their condition."

Liang Fu bowed.

The Crown Prince made a note.

He was beginning to understand why his mother had been so particular about the difference between a record and a conclusion.

A person could write that a wall was damaged.

That did not mean it needed to be rebuilt.

A person could write that a purchase was necessary.

That did not mean the price was reasonable.

And a person could write that an expenditure had been approved.

That did not mean the money had reached the intended recipient.

The more he examined the accounts, the more he understood that the palace's problem was not a lack of authority.

It was that authority had become tangled with convenience.

At the Qingyuan Marquisate, Shen Mingzhao entered the family council room precisely at the appointed hour.

Her father, Shen Guowei, sat at the head of the table.

Madam Liang sat to his right.

Old Madam Shen occupied the most comfortable chair.

Shen Mingyuan and Shen Mingxiu were also present.

Beside them sat Mr. Chen, the family's former legal adviser, and an older gentleman whom Mingzhao had not met before.

Her father introduced him.

"Mr. Liang is a senior representative of our clan. He has been informed of the matter and has agreed to attend as an impartial observer."

Mingzhao bowed.

Mr. Liang returned the greeting.

He was in his sixties, with a thin face and a careful manner.

"Miss Shen," he said, "I understand that you wish to divide the family property and establish an independent household."

"That is correct."

"Your father says you are dissatisfied with the current arrangements."

"I am dissatisfied with several matters. The division itself is a legal and financial question."

Mr. Liang nodded slowly.

"Then let us begin with the reason for your decision."

Mingzhao looked at him.

"Is that necessary?"

"It is useful."

"For whom?"

"For everyone present."

Mingzhao took her seat.

"Very well. I have managed the household accounts for six years. I have also handled estate repairs, grain purchases, commercial arrangements, staff wages, and several family obligations. I no longer wish to continue managing those matters without a defined position, authority, or compensation."

Madam Liang frowned.

"You have never been denied authority."

"I have been given responsibility. Those are not the same thing."

Mingyuan shifted in his seat.

Mingzhao continued.

"I have also requested a proper accounting of the household funds, including payments charged to individual residences and expenses whose classification is unclear. The examination is ongoing. I do not intend to use unresolved figures as the basis for a settlement."

Mr. Liang looked at her.

"Do you believe your family has treated you unfairly?"

"I believe that several arrangements have been unfair. Whether each was intentional is a separate question."

Old Madam Shen tapped her cane.

"Your mother and father raised you. They gave you a place in this family. Now you speak as though you were an outside creditor."

Mingzhao turned toward her grandmother.

"I am not an outside creditor. I am a member of this family. That is why I want the accounts settled properly rather than leaving them to become disputes later."

"Must everything be written down?"

"Where property is concerned, yes."

The old madam looked displeased.

Mr. Chen intervened.

"Perhaps we should distinguish the legal questions from the family questions."

Mingzhao nodded.

"That would be helpful."

Mr. Liang opened his folder.

"Then we shall begin with the categories of property."

He read from the list.

"First, property independently belonging to Miss Shen, including personal inheritance, dowry assets, and property acquired in her own name."

"Correct."

"Second, commercial interests held independently or through partnerships."

"Correct."

"Third, funds advanced by Miss Shen to the family estate, provided they can be established by records."

"Correct."

"Fourth, property belonging to the Marquisate as a whole."

"Correct."

"Fifth, outstanding liabilities and obligations."

"Correct."

Mr. Liang looked up.

"You are not claiming that all property managed by you belongs to you?"

"No."

"Nor are you claiming that all household expenditure should be repaid to you?"

"No."

"Then the matter is more limited than I was led to believe."

Mingzhao glanced at her father.

Her father looked away.

Madam Liang spoke.

"She has not yet explained what she expects from the family."

Mingzhao answered calmly.

"I expect an accurate inventory, an independent valuation, an examination of the accounts, and a settlement of the claims that are supported by evidence."

"And after that?"

"I will leave."

The room became quiet.

Mr. Liang looked at her.

"You intend to leave even if the settlement is generous?"

"I do not intend to remain merely because the settlement is generous."

Shen Guowei's expression tightened.

"Must you make this so final?"

"You asked me to explain my intention."

"I asked you to consider the family."

"I have considered the family for six years."

Her voice was not raised.

That made the words more difficult to dismiss.

Mr. Liang turned to the next document.

"Your father has asked whether your departure can be delayed until the settlement is complete."

Mingzhao considered the question.

"That depends on the reason for the delay."

"The family must continue functioning."

"Then the family should appoint someone to manage it."

"Your brother is the heir."

"Then he should begin learning."

Mingyuan's expression changed.

"Are you suggesting I cannot manage the estate?"

"I am suggesting that you have not managed it."

"I have responsibilities outside the household."

"So did I."

"You have made this sound as though I have done nothing."

"I have not said that."

"You have implied it."

Mingzhao looked at him.

"Then I will be precise. You have not been responsible for the estate accounts, the warehouse inventories, the household wages, or the repair records. If you are to manage those matters in the future, you will need to learn them."

Mingyuan opened his mouth.

Then closed it.

He had no answer that did not require him to claim experience he did not possess.

Shen Guowei intervened.

"Your brother will take over gradually."

"Then the transfer should be documented."

"Must you insist on documentation for everything?"

"Yes."

Old Madam Shen looked toward Mr. Liang.

"Is this how young women speak to their elders now?"

Mr. Liang folded his hands.

"Madam, a property settlement cannot be completed through memory and goodwill alone."

The old madam was not pleased to hear this from another elder.

Madam Liang turned to Mingzhao.

"You speak of leaving as though the family has no place for you."

Mingzhao looked at her mother.

"The family has a place for me when it needs work done."

Madam Liang's expression stiffened.

"That is not fair."

"Then tell me which part is incorrect."

Madam Liang did not answer immediately.

Mingzhao looked down at the papers.

She knew her mother had not spent the last six years deliberately planning to make her miserable. She also knew that Madam Liang had benefited from the arrangement, and had repeatedly asked her to accept it because the family required stability.

The difference between intention and consequence had never been enough to make the consequences disappear.

Mr. Chen cleared his throat.

"We should not attempt to settle every grievance in one meeting."

"Agreed," Mingzhao said.

Mr. Liang nodded.

"Then I propose that the family first confirm the inventory process. The accountant should continue his examination. The valuation should be independent. The parties should not make deductions based on unverified figures."

Mingzhao agreed.

Shen Guowei looked relieved.

"Good. Then we can discuss the date of her departure after the accounts are completed."

Mingzhao looked at him.

"No."

His relief vanished.

"I have agreed to complete the settlement properly. I have not agreed to let the family decide when I may leave."

Mr. Liang watched her carefully.

"Then what do you propose?"

"Once the necessary property and liabilities have been identified, we can establish a reasonable date for the transfer of household responsibilities. My departure should not depend on the family being entirely comfortable with it."

Nobody spoke for a moment.

Then Mr. Liang nodded.

"That is a more practical proposal."

Shen Guowei looked at the older man.

He had hoped for an ally.

Instead, he had received a timetable.

After the meeting, Shen Mingxiu found her sister in the eastern corridor.

Mingzhao was holding a folder.

"Second Sister," Mingxiu said.

Mingzhao stopped.

"What is it?"

"May I ask you something?"

"You may."

"Are you planning to take the old household staff with you?"

Mingzhao looked at her.

"Some may wish to leave with me. I will not take anyone without their consent."

"Mother is worried that the servants will become divided."

"Then she should make their employment arrangements clear."

Mingxiu hesitated.

"And what about the people who have worked under you for years?"

"They will receive their wages according to the existing arrangements. Any outstanding payments should be recorded before the transfer."

Mingxiu looked down the corridor.

"Some of them are afraid."

"Of what?"

"That they will be blamed if the accounts are wrong."

Mingzhao's expression changed slightly.

"Have they said that?"

"Not directly."

"Who?"

"The warehouse clerks. Two of the older servants. They heard that the accountant is examining the records."

Mingzhao was silent.

She had expected the family to resist the examination.

She had not considered that the people who had maintained the records might fear being made responsible for every discrepancy.

"Tell them that the accountant is examining the records, not searching for a person to punish."

"Will they believe that?"

"They may not."

Mingxiu gave a small, humourless smile.

"Then perhaps you should tell them yourself."

Mingzhao looked at her.

"Why are you concerned?"

"Because if the servants become frightened, they will hide mistakes. Then the accounts will become worse."

Mingzhao studied her younger sister.

That was a practical answer.

It was also a more considerate answer than she had expected.

"I will speak to the senior steward," Mingzhao said. "He can inform the staff of the purpose of the examination."

Mingxiu nodded.

"Thank you."

She turned to leave.

"Mingxiu."

Her younger sister stopped.

"Do you intend to take over any household responsibilities?"

Mingxiu hesitated.

"I do not know."

"Then you should decide."

"Everyone assumes I will help Mother."

"Will you?"

"Some matters, perhaps. But I do not want to manage the entire household."

Mingzhao looked at her for a moment.

"Then say so before they assign it to you."

Mingxiu gave a small smile.

"You make that sound simple."

"It is not simple. It is still necessary."

Mingxiu left.

Mingzhao watched her go.

She had no intention of making her sister the next person trapped in the same arrangement.

That did not mean she intended to solve her sister's problems for her.

At the palace, the Second Prince had encountered a different problem.

He had decided to define exceptional expenditure.

The first draft had been simple.

Any purchase exceeding ten taels required his approval.

His steward had pointed out that this would require him to approve a large number of routine purchases.

The second draft raised the threshold to twenty taels.

The steward then explained that some necessary purchases were inexpensive individually but substantial in total.

The third draft required approval for any purchase exceeding twenty taels, or any series of related purchases exceeding fifty taels in a month.

The steward had asked how related purchases would be identified.

The Second Prince had stared at him.

Now he was sitting before his father with three drafts.

The emperor read them.

"Why are there three?"

"Because the first was impractical."

"And the second?"

"Also impractical."

"And the third?"

"I have not decided."

The emperor placed the papers down.

"At least you have discovered that a rule can create a problem even when it is intended to solve one."

The Second Prince looked offended.

"I was aware of that."

"Then you should have remembered it before writing three versions."

Zhao Chengyu pressed his lips together.

The emperor continued.

"Why do you want a threshold?"

"To prevent unnecessary expenditure from being submitted to me."

"Then why should the threshold be based only on price?"

The Second Prince thought.

"Because large purchases require greater scrutiny."

"Some do. Others are routine. A large quantity of grain may be ordinary. A small payment to an unfamiliar person may be unusual."

The Second Prince looked down at the drafts.

"Then the rule should distinguish ordinary purchases from exceptional ones, not merely use a fixed amount."

"That is closer."

"How should I define them?"

"That is what you are learning."

The Second Prince looked up.

"Father, the Empress has managed these matters for years. Why did she never establish a simpler procedure?"

The emperor's expression changed.

"She established many procedures."

"Then why are we reviewing them?"

"Because some were written, some were informal, and some existed only because she knew what needed to be done."

The Second Prince was quiet.

The emperor picked up the first draft again.

"You are not being asked to replace your mother in one day. You are being asked to learn how your own household functions."

"I understand."

"Do you?"

Zhao Chengyu sighed.

"I am beginning to."

The emperor returned the drafts.

"Continue."

The Second Prince collected them.

He had begun to suspect that the palace's accounts were designed to make every prince regret being born into an imperial family.

That evening, Yuru met the emperor in the private study.

She had received the report on exceptional expenditure and had reviewed the proposed division of responsibilities.

The emperor looked at her.

"Do you agree with the distinction?"

"Mostly."

"Mostly?"

"The request, approval, verification, and review functions should be separate. But the proposed reporting route is too complicated for ordinary matters."

The emperor gestured for her to continue.

"An ordinary repair request should not pass through four offices before a clerk can replace a broken door hinge. The procedures should become more demanding as the risk or cost increases."

"That sounds reasonable."

"It is also why the threshold cannot be determined by price alone."

The emperor smiled faintly.

"Chengyu discovered that today."

"I heard."

"He has written three versions."

"Only three?"

The emperor looked at her.

Yuru lifted her teacup.

"I am being generous."

He laughed.

Then his expression became more serious.

"The Empress Dowager spoke with you."

"Yes."

"What did she say?"

"That the position carries duties."

"She has said that to me as well."

"She also asked whether the Crown Prince understands what he will inherit."

The emperor nodded.

"That is a reasonable concern."

"It is."

He studied her.

"Are you asking me to make a decision soon?"

"I am asking you to continue the review."

"That is not an answer."

"It is the answer I can give today."

The emperor leaned back.

"You have become very good at giving answers that do not give me what I want."

Yuru looked at him.

"You have become very accustomed to asking questions that do not require you to make a decision."

He was silent.

Then he laughed again, though this time the sound was quieter.

"Perhaps."

Yuru placed her cup down.

"The palace is not ready for a sudden change. I know that. But the review must not become an excuse to preserve every arrangement indefinitely."

"I know."

"Do you?"

The emperor looked at her.

For a moment, the question seemed to return to him exactly as he had asked it of the Crown Prince.

Then he nodded.

"I am beginning to."

Yuru stood.

"That is a beginning."

She bowed and left.

The emperor remained in his study.

He looked at the empty space where her documents would ordinarily have been placed.

There were still several reports waiting.

He could summon her.

He could ask her to review them.

He could also send them to the offices responsible for them.

He chose the latter.

It was a small decision.

It was also the sort of decision he had avoided making for years.

The following morning, Mingzhao received a note from Sun Qiming.

The accountant had spoken with the senior steward and had obtained the names of two former warehouse clerks who might know more about the missing fifteen sacks.

He had also received a preliminary response from the former northern warehouse steward.

The steward was willing to meet.

Mingzhao read the note twice.

Then she turned to the second page.

The accountant had also confirmed that the three hundred taels could not yet be classified as a personal debt of Shen Mingyuan. The evidence was insufficient.

She placed the note beside the property inventory.

Qiao Yue entered.

"Miss, the broker has sent the repair inspector's estimate."

"Put it here."

"And the former warehouse steward has agreed to meet the accountant?"

"Yes."

"That is good news."

"It is progress."

"Is that different?"

"Good news is something that makes you happy. Progress is something that moves the matter forward."

Qiao Yue considered this.

"Then I hope we receive some good news soon."

Mingzhao looked at the estimate.

"The roof requires one hundred and twenty taels. The drainage requires another thirty. The kitchen needs repairs."

Qiao Yue sighed.

"Perhaps we should hope for good news about the house first."

Mingzhao turned the page.

There was a note at the bottom from the inspector.

The roof was repairable, but several beams would need replacement. The kitchen drainage was poorly designed. The courtyard well required cleaning before the house could be occupied.

Mingzhao put down the paper.

"Add another twenty taels to the reserve."

Qiao Yue looked at her.

"That is not good news."

"No."

"Is it progress?"

Mingzhao picked up her brush.

"Unfortunately."

She added the amount.

The new total was larger.

The house was still not purchased.

The settlement was still incomplete.

The family still had not agreed on a departure date.

But the accounts were becoming clearer, the responsibilities were beginning to move, and the two women were learning that leaving an old arrangement required more than declaring that they no longer wished to remain inside it.

It required records.

It required decisions.

And, in the case of the imperial palace, it apparently required an entire department to determine who had authority over a broom.

**End of Chapter 9.**
