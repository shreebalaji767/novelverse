# **Chapter 84: The Former Empress Has Received Two Books and Has Not Yet Been Required to Read Them on a Schedule. The Marquis's Eldest Daughter Has Dispatched One Hundred and Seventy-Two Bolts and Has Discovered That a Completed Order Still Has to Travel. The Marquisate's New Procedure Has Survived Another Morning and Is Beginning to Acquire Opinions.**

**18th Day of the Sixth Month, 18th Year of Yonghe.**



The former Empress had two books waiting for her.



This was not, by itself, a difficult situation.



The first book had been read, marked in several places, wrapped carefully, and set aside for return to her father. The second and third books had been selected by the Duke of Xu and were waiting at the eastern courtyard of his residence. They concerned household estates and provincial granaries, respectively.



Her father had sent word that she could borrow them whenever convenient.



He had not sent a date.



Yuru considered this an excellent arrangement.



A book that was available without a deadline was a pleasant thing. A book that came with a deadline, a report, and a request for recommendations was generally the beginning of an appointment.



She sat beside the guest residence's window, looking at the wrapped volume on the low table.



Fang Ru entered with a small tray.



"Madam, the morning tea."



Yuru moved the book slightly to make room.



Fang Ru glanced at the parcel. "You intend to return that one on your next visit?"



"Yes."



"And borrow the other two?"



"That is what Father suggested."



"Then you will have three books."



"One returning and two arriving."



"That is still a net increase of one."



Yuru looked at her.



Fang Ru placed the tea down. "I am only observing the arithmetic."



"An observation of considerable importance."



"I thought so."



Yuru poured her tea.



She had once kept a record of palace books borrowed by officials, their expected return dates, and the number of reminders required before they returned them. The register had been simple. The people using it had not.



Some borrowed books and returned them promptly. Others forgot. A few insisted that a book had been returned because they had sent a servant to the library, although the servant had delivered a different volume.



One official had returned a book with a note explaining that he had found its contents useful but could not remember where he had placed the final section.



Yuru had spent an entire afternoon locating the missing section.



She took a sip of tea.



"Do you think the eastern courtyard library has a record of borrowed books?" Fang Ru asked.



"Certainly."



"Will your father record the two books?"



"Probably."



"Will he record the date?"



"Almost certainly."



"Will he ask when you intend to return them?"



Yuru considered this.



"He may."



"Then perhaps we should borrow only one."



Yuru smiled faintly. "You are becoming cautious."



"I have spent too long in a household where every simple request develops an attached list."



"These are books."



"Today."



Yuru looked at the wrapped volume again.



She had enjoyed reading her father's first book. Not every passage had been useful to her, and not every observation required an action. She had been able to read it, consider it, and leave its problems on the page.



That was a small but unfamiliar pleasure.



"I shall not hurry," she said.



Fang Ru nodded. "Then the books will wait."



"They have done so already."



"And are likely to continue."



"Books are generally better at waiting than people."



Fang Ru looked toward the door.



"That depends on the people."



Yuru followed her glance.



A palace attendant had arrived with a small covered basket. It contained no documents, no formal invitation, and no request for an audience. The basket had been sent from the Dowager's residence with a short personal note.



The note asked whether the former Empress had been enjoying the warmer weather and whether she had found the fruit from her natal family pleasant.



Yuru read it once.



"Prepare a reply," she said. "Tell Her Majesty that the fruit was very good, and that I have been taking walks when the weather permits."



Fang Ru brought paper and ink.



Yuru paused before writing.



She could have added something about the books. She could have described the garden path, the quiet pavilion, or the fact that she had recently walked farther than she had intended.



Instead, she wrote a few simple sentences.



The Dowager had asked a personal question.



Yuru gave a personal answer.



When she finished, Fang Ru examined the note.



"Short."



"She did not ask for a report."



"That is true."



"Then there is no reason to provide one."



The attendant accepted the reply and departed.



Yuru returned to her tea.



The books could wait.



For once, she did not feel obliged to prove that she was using her time productively.

The second consignment of silk bolts left the workshop shortly after the morning bell.



There were one hundred and seventy-two.



They had been counted, packed, and checked against the dispatch list. The four additional containers required for the second consignment had been recorded separately, including the cost of their manufacture and the reason for their use.



Mingzhao stood beside the workshop's loading area while the carrier's men secured the final bundles.



She had already checked the count twice.



She did not intend to check it a third time merely because the carts were about to move.



"One hundred and seventy-two," Lin Shuyun said, consulting the dispatch sheet. "The first consignment contained twenty-eight. Together, they make two hundred."



"Provided the first twenty-eight remain twenty-eight."



Lin looked at her.



Mingzhao tapped the carrier's receipt with one finger. "I am not accusing the road of arithmetic."



"That is reassuring."



"It would be difficult to conduct business if we had to accuse every road."



The carrier's supervisor approached.



"The second consignment is ready. We can depart."



"Your men have the transfer instructions?"



"Yes."



"The first consignment's receipt has been included?"



"Attached to the dispatch record."



"And the receiving point has been informed that the two consignments belong to the same order but are separately counted?"



The supervisor nodded. "The instructions say so."



"Good. Then sign the receipt."



He signed.



Mingzhao examined the document before allowing the carts to leave. The number of containers matched the packing list. The number of bundles matched the dispatch schedule. The carrier's responsibility began at the workshop gate and ended at the designated transfer point.



The buyer's final warehouse was elsewhere.



There was no reason to pretend that the two places were the same.



When the carts began moving, Lin Shuyun watched them disappear through the workshop entrance.



"Two hundred bolts accepted," she said. "Now two hundred bolts dispatched."



"Not yet."



Lin turned to her.



"Twenty-eight dispatched yesterday. One hundred and seventy-two dispatched today. That is the full order leaving the workshop."



"Yes."



"But the buyer has not received all two hundred at the final warehouse."



Lin looked at the road.



"You are determined to deny us even the pleasure of finishing a sentence."



"I have no objection to finishing sentences. I object to finishing accounts before the goods arrive."



Lin laughed.



Mingzhao handed the signed carrier receipt to the workshop clerk.



"Enter today's date. Keep the first consignment's receipt separate. Do not combine them into one delivery record."



The clerk bowed and withdrew.



Lin folded her arms.



"At this rate, the workshop will soon require a clerk whose only duty is to prevent other clerks from making a single careless sentence."



"That is called supervision."



"Then you have discovered a new profession."



"I have discovered that people are capable of writing 'delivered' when they mean 'dispatched.'"



"An important discovery."



"One that has cost several people their afternoon."



They returned to the office.



On the table lay Sun Qiming's preliminary cost statement.



He had divided the expenses into material, ordinary wages, training wages, inspection, packing, and transport. Several amounts remained provisional because the final transport charges had not been settled.



The additional four containers appeared under packing expenses.



Mingzhao read the entry.



"Why is the second consignment's packing cost recorded separately?"



"Because it was separately authorized," Sun replied. "The original estimate did not include the four additional containers."



"Did you compare their cost with the expected damage risk?"



"I compared it with the cost of replacing damaged goods and the possibility of delaying delivery while corrections were made."



"And?"



"The additional packing was reasonable. It does not mean it was free."



Mingzhao looked up.



"I did not ask whether it was free."



"I know. I am preventing the workshop from celebrating an expense as a saving."



Lin sat down.



"That is becoming a popular occupation."



Sun ignored her.



"The preliminary figures are consistent. The revised folding method reduced working time in some stages. Training and additional inspection cost more than originally estimated. The net effect is smaller than the first calculation suggested."



"How much smaller?" Mingzhao asked.



"I will not give you a final figure yet."



"Because the transport cost is unknown?"



"Because transport cost is unknown, the remaining payment has not been received, and the first consignment has not yet been confirmed at the buyer's warehouse."



Mingzhao nodded.



"Then do not calculate profit."



"I have not."



"Good."



Sun closed the statement.



"The buyer has already accepted all two hundred bolts. That is a significant step."



"It is."



"And the carrier has now taken the second consignment."



"It has."



"Then the production stage is complete."



Mingzhao considered the distinction.



"Production is complete. Dispatch is complete. Delivery is not."



Sun looked at her for a moment.



"Your definitions are becoming very expensive."



"They are cheaper than a dispute."



Lin picked up the cost statement and read the headings.



"Material, wages, training, inspection, packing, transport. You have not included the cost of listening to Mingzhao explain the difference between dispatch and delivery."



"That would be difficult to quantify," Sun said.



"It would be the largest expense."



Mingzhao took the paper from her.



"Then we shall leave it out."



She placed the statement back on the table.



The workshop had completed its order. The buyer had accepted the goods. The first consignment was on its way through the transfer system, and the second had just departed.



It was progress.



It was not yet settlement.



Mingzhao was beginning to understand that independence did not mean that every problem ended when she made a decision. Some decisions merely changed the kind of waiting required.



She disliked waiting.



She disliked even more the idea of pretending that waiting was the same as completion.

At the Marquisate of Qingyuan, the new purchasing procedure had entered its second full day of use.



The consolidated purchasing register lay open on the accounts-office desk.



Liang Wen, the clerk assigned to maintain it, had arranged the day's entries in chronological order. Each purchase had a separate authorization, purchasing record, receipt confirmation, and payment entry where applicable.



The system was not elegant.



It was, however, legible.



Steward Zhou De stood beside the desk, examining a record for household lamp oil.



"Why is this entry still marked pending?"



Liang Wen pointed to the receipt column.



"The purchasing clerk recorded delivery of twelve jars. The receiving steward recorded ten jars received."



"Then two jars are missing."



"Not necessarily."



Zhou looked at him.



Liang Wen swallowed.



"The purchasing clerk says the supplier delivered twelve. The receiving steward says ten arrived at the storeroom. The carrier's note says twelve jars were loaded, but it does not state how many were unloaded."



"Where are the other two?"



"We do not know."



"Then why does the entry say pending?"



"Because the difference has not been verified."



Zhou looked again at the register.



"Could you not write that two jars were lost in transport?"



"Not without evidence."



"Could they have been left at the gate?"



"Possibly."



"Could the receiving steward have counted incorrectly?"



"Also possible."



Zhou stared at him.



Liang Wen remained still.



The old procedure had allowed such discrepancies to disappear into a final total if someone senior decided that the difference was small enough. The new procedure did not permit that. The original entry remained visible, and the unresolved amount had to be explained separately.



It was a simple rule.



It was also irritating.



"Send someone to check the gate records," Zhou said.



Liang Wen wrote down the instruction.



"And ask the carrier whether he has a second unloading record."



"Yes."



Zhou pointed at the pending entry.



"Do not change the twelve to ten."



"I will not."



"Do not change the ten to twelve."



"I will not."



"And do not write that the two jars are missing until we know whether they were ever unloaded."



Liang Wen bowed.



"I understand."



Zhou walked away.



He had spent years managing the estate by knowing which problems could be solved through experience and which required a question to be asked. He did not dislike the new procedure because it was wrong.



He disliked it because it made every uncertainty visible.



An uncertainty that remained in a man's memory could be ignored until it became important. An uncertainty written in a register could be found by anyone who opened the book.



He was beginning to suspect that this was the point.



Across the room, Shen Mingyuan was reviewing a separate report.



The report concerned the old grain discrepancy.



The figures had not changed.



Forty-seven sacks had appeared in the original record. Twenty had been included in the instruction. Eighteen had been recorded as received. Two remained unconfirmed. Twenty-seven had no established destination.



Zhang, the former warehouse steward, had clarified that the warehouse used separate totals for the entire stock and the amount immediately available for issue. That explained why two books could show different totals without one necessarily being incorrect.



It did not explain the twenty-seven sacks.



Mingyuan placed the report beside the original copies.



"Has Zhang identified the receiving party?"



"No," said the accounts clerk.



"Has he identified a transport instruction?"



"No."



"The separate transport payment with no destination?"



"Still not connected."



Mingyuan nodded.



"Keep them separate."



The clerk hesitated. "Would it not be simpler to list them together as one unresolved matter?"



"No. They are two unresolved matters. If we combine them, someone may assume they belong together."



The clerk looked at the papers.



"That would be convenient."



"It would also be unsupported."



Mingyuan closed the folder.



The procedure he had introduced was beginning to influence the way he examined older accounts. He could no longer tolerate a sentence that made two uncertain things appear to be one certain thing.



This was inconvenient for everyone.



It was especially inconvenient for him.



He had inherited the estate and, with it, the expectation that he would eventually understand every account. He was discovering that the first responsibility of an heir was not to know everything.



It was to stop pretending that he did.



"Continue the search for the original payment voucher," he said. "The entry attributed to my sister remains unverified."



The clerk bowed.



"We have searched the general purchasing bundle."



"Then check the related storage lists. Do not assume the voucher was filed correctly."



"Yes, young master."



Mingyuan returned to the report.



The old voucher might be found.



It might not.



Until then, no one could reasonably claim that the entry proved his sister had received money, authorized the purchase, or personally handled the payment.



He did not like the uncertainty.



But he had begun to understand that uncertainty was not a permission to invent an answer.

At the temporary residence near the eastern market, Mingzhao received the workshop's dispatch report shortly before noon.



Qiao Yue read it aloud.



"The second consignment of one hundred and seventy-two bolts has left the workshop. Carrier's receipt signed. Four additional packing containers recorded separately. First consignment of twenty-eight remains at the designated transfer point."



Mingzhao nodded.



"Any confirmation from the transfer point?"



"Not yet."



"Then record that."



Qiao Yue lowered the paper.



"Would you like me to write that the order has been dispatched in full?"



"Yes. Add that delivery to the buyer's warehouse remains pending."



Qiao Yue wrote it down.



"Production complete. Full dispatch complete. Final delivery pending."



"Correct."



"Payment pending."



"Correct."



"Final profit pending."



"Correct."



Qiao Yue looked at her.



"Would you like to add that the young lady is pending?"



Mingzhao raised her eyes.



"Why?"



"Because every other matter appears to be waiting for something."



"I am not waiting for anything."



"Then perhaps the young lady is the only completed item."



Mingzhao took the report.



"I am sitting here."



"Exactly. A rare achievement."



Mingzhao folded the paper.



She had spent years in the Marquisate being described as the person who could manage everything. The phrase had been used as praise, as expectation, and occasionally as a convenient excuse for not asking whether she wished to manage it.



Now, in her own rented rooms, she had a workshop, a business partner, a bookkeeper, a contract, and a collection of accounts that were not yet finished.



She was still responsible for her own decisions.



But she was not responsible for every decision made by everyone around her.



That distinction had taken longer to establish than she had expected.



Qiao Yue brought in another letter.



"This is from the Marquisate."



Mingzhao did not reach for it immediately.



"Who sent it?"



"The accounts office. It is addressed to you."



"Does it contain a question?"



"I cannot say. The envelope is sealed."



"Then we shall discover whether it contains a question after opening it."



Qiao Yue handed it over.



The letter was brief. Mingyuan informed her that the revised purchasing procedure had been introduced and that the first discrepancies were being recorded under the new system. He also stated that the search for the original voucher continued, with no conclusion yet.



There was no request for her to return.



There was no request for her to supervise the register.



There was no instruction to examine the estate's general purchases.



At the end, Mingyuan had written:



*Your corrections have been included in the final procedure. The accounts office will proceed under the assigned responsibilities. If a specific question arises concerning an old entry for which you have direct knowledge, I will send it separately with the relevant record.*



Mingzhao read the letter twice.



Qiao Yue watched her.



"Is there a request?"



"No."



"An invitation?"



"No."



"An accusation?"



"No."



"Then it is an unusually peaceful letter from the Marquisate."



Mingzhao set it down.



"It is a report."



"That is even more unusual."



She looked again at the final sentence.



Mingyuan had not asked her to take responsibility for the procedure simply because she had helped improve it. He had acknowledged her corrections and left the work with the people appointed to do it.



That was what she had asked for.



She did not feel triumphant.



She felt relieved, and slightly irritated that relief should be such a small thing.



"Prepare a short reply," she said.



Qiao Yue brought paper.



Mingzhao dictated:



*I have received your letter. I am pleased the procedure has been introduced. Continue to preserve original entries and distinguish verified facts from unresolved matters. I will respond to specific questions concerning my own records when the relevant documents are provided.*



Qiao Yue wrote it down.



"Anything else?"



"No."



"Not even a reminder that you are not returning?"



"He knows."



"Perhaps he should be reminded."



"Not today."



Qiao Yue sealed the reply.



Mingzhao returned to the workshop report.



There were still two hundred bolts to reach the buyer's warehouse. The final transport charges remained unsettled. The remaining payment had not arrived. Sun Qiming had not completed the final account.



The Marquisate's grain discrepancy remained unresolved.



The old payment voucher remained missing.



The property division remained incomplete.



The northern canal property remained a possibility rather than an acquisition.



Nothing had been magically settled by the arrival of one letter.



But one small thing had changed.



For the first time in many years, the Marquisate had sent Mingzhao an update about its own work without attaching the expectation that she would take it over.



She folded the workshop report and placed it beside the cost statement.



"Qiao Yue."



"Yes?"



"Tomorrow, ask Sun Qiming when he expects to finish the cost review."



"Because you want the final profit?"



"Because I want to know when the figures will be ready."



"That is a distinction."



"It is an important one."



Qiao Yue nodded gravely.



"Then I shall ask him for a date, not a profit."



"Good."



"And if he gives us a profit instead?"



"Tell him to finish the accounts first."



Qiao Yue left, carrying the reply to the Marquisate.



Mingzhao remained at the table.



Outside, the market continued its business. Vendors called out prices, carts rattled over the road, and someone nearby argued that a basket of pears had been weighed incorrectly.



The world had not become simpler because she had left her family estate.



It had merely become a place where the problems in front of her were more often her own.



She found that preferable.



For now.

That afternoon, in the western guest residence of the Imperial Palace, Yuru finished her tea and opened the small basket sent by the Dowager.



Inside were several pieces of fresh fruit.



Fang Ru arranged them on a plate.



"Another gift," she said.



"A personal one."



"Would you like it recorded?"



Yuru considered the question.



The earlier confusion over the fruit basket had been corrected. Her personal belongings and the guest residence's stores were now kept separately. There was no need to turn every piece of fruit into a matter requiring several officials.



"Record the basket as received from the Dowager," she said. "The fruit is for personal consumption."



Fang Ru nodded.



"And the two books?"



"They are not here yet."



"The one you intend to return?"



"That is already recorded as my father's property."



Fang Ru looked at the wrapped volume.



"Then our records are complete."



"For today."



Fang Ru placed a pear on the plate.



Yuru picked it up.



The palace had not collapsed.



The Marquisate had not collapsed.



The workshop had not collapsed.



A shipment of silk was travelling toward a buyer, an estate clerk was learning how to record a discrepancy, and a young heir was discovering that uncertainty could be written down without being concealed.



None of these matters required Yuru to intervene.



She took a bite of the pear.



It was sweet.



She decided that was sufficient achievement for the afternoon.
