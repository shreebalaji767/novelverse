# **Chapter 113: The Former Empress Has Discovered That a Pleasant Afternoon Need Not Be Filled with Visitors, the Marquis's Eldest Daughter Has Discovered That a Supplier's Sample Is Not a Promise of Quality Until It Has Been Examined, and the Marquis's Heir Has Discovered That Six Boxes of Miscellaneous Papers May Contain More Questions Than Six Boxes of Properly Labelled Records.**

**17th Day of the Seventh Month, 18th Year of Yonghe.**

The former Empress spent the afternoon doing something that would once have been considered an unreasonable use of her time.

She sat beneath the covered gallery and did nothing in particular.

There was a book beside her. There was tea on the small table. A light breeze moved through the courtyard, carrying the scent of the fragrant wood the Dowager had sent the previous day.

Yuru had read several pages before putting the book down.

She was not tired. She had simply reached a passage that did not interest her.

For years, she had treated unfinished reading as a small failure. A book had been borrowed, a chapter opened, and a certain amount of time allotted. If she stopped before reaching the end, she had usually found something else to do that could be described as useful.

Now she watched a leaf drift across the courtyard.

It came to rest beside a stone basin.

A servant passing through the gallery saw it and bent to pick it up.

Yuru did not stop him.

The servant carried on.

Fang Ru, who was arranging a small writing box, glanced at the book.

"Madam, shall I bring another one?"

"No."

"Would you prefer the tea?"

"I have it."

"Would you like me to ask whether the Dowager is receiving visitors?"

Yuru looked at her.

"Why would I do that?"

Fang Ru paused.

"I thought you might wish to visit."

"I may visit another day."

"Yes, Madam."

Yuru leaned back slightly.

The palace was not silent. There were distant footsteps, the occasional sound of a door opening, and the quiet movement of attendants carrying out their duties. But none of those sounds required her attention.

She had once believed that an afternoon without a task would inevitably become an afternoon in which someone discovered a task for her.

That belief had not entirely disappeared.

It had merely become less reliable.

After a while, she picked up the book again.

Fang Ru continued arranging the writing box.

Neither woman spoke for some time.

Then Yuru turned a page.

"Fang Ru."

"Yes, Madam?"

"Have you ever read a book simply because you liked the title?"

Fang Ru thought about it.

"No."

"Why not?"

"I usually read what I am asked to read."

"Then perhaps you should choose one yourself."

Fang Ru looked uncertain.

"From the residence library?"

"Yes. You may borrow one."

"Would that be proper?"

Yuru closed her book.

"You are asking whether an attendant may borrow a book from a library?"

"I am asking whether I should."

"Those are different questions."

Fang Ru lowered her eyes.

Yuru's expression softened.

"Choose something you wish to read. You do not need to choose something that would be useful to me."

Fang Ru was quiet for a moment.

Then she nodded.

"Yes, Madam."

Yuru reopened her book.

The afternoon continued without producing a decision of importance.

She considered it a satisfactory afternoon.

At the old accounts storehouse, Mingyuan discovered that the first of the six boxes contained a dispute between two people who had been dead for eleven years.

The box had been brought into the records room shortly after the morning meal. Its lid was warped, and the outer paper label had lost several characters. The remaining words identified it as a collection of miscellaneous northern office papers.

Steward Zhou had arranged for two clerks to record each bundle.

Mingyuan stood beside the table while the storehouse keeper removed the first packet.

The papers inside were tied with faded cord.

"Do not separate the sheets yet," Mingyuan instructed. "Record the bundle as it is first."

The clerk wrote down the condition of the packet.

The storehouse keeper then loosened the cord.

The first documents were old requests for repairs to a roadside granary. The second bundle contained receipts for timber. The third held a disagreement about the boundary of a drainage ditch.

Mingyuan read the heading.

"Is this the same northern canal property mentioned in the old land records?"

The keeper shook his head.

"No, Young Master. This is a drainage channel beside the northern grain road. It is not the canal property under discussion."

"Then record the distinction."

The clerk did so.

A fourth packet contained correspondence about cart repairs. The fifth held a list of workers assigned to a temporary storehouse.

Mingyuan looked through the pages.

There was no northern dispatch book.

The sixth packet was smaller.

Its label had been written over an earlier one, and the ink had faded enough that only the final characters remained.

The storehouse keeper placed it on the table.

"This is the last bundle in the first box."

Mingyuan examined the outer paper.

"Record the original label as far as it can be read. Do not replace it with a new title."

The clerk wrote down the visible characters.

The packet contained loose sheets, several receipts, and two pages of a damaged register.

Mingyuan picked up the first page.

It concerned the repair of a cart axle.

The second was a list of payments to temporary transport workers.

He turned the page over.

There was a faint mark near the edge where a larger sheet had once been attached.

"Was this page originally part of a bound register?"

The storehouse keeper examined it.

"It may have been. The edge has old stitching holes."

"Does the bundle contain the other pages?"

"Not that I can see."

Mingyuan set it down.

The clerk recorded the condition.

The box had produced no answer about the four carts.

It had, however, revealed that miscellaneous papers had been stored together with records from several different offices.

That made the missing dispatch book harder to locate.

It also explained why the later transfer register had been incomplete.

Mingyuan looked at the six boxes listed on the storehouse inventory.

"Continue with the second box."

Steward Zhou glanced at the time.

"Young Master, we have examined only one box. The clerks may need the rest of the day."

"Then they may continue tomorrow if necessary."

"Should we ask the records office to send another clerk?"

"Only if the current clerks cannot keep up with the inventory."

Steward Zhou nodded.

Mingyuan looked again at the damaged register page.

"Have this page copied exactly as it is. Do not fill in the missing characters."

"Yes, Young Master."

He moved away from the table.

The first box had not yielded the book.

It had yielded a damaged page, an old boundary dispute, and several receipts for cart repairs.

Someone might have called the search unsuccessful.

Mingyuan did not.

The inventory now contained a record of what the box actually held.

That was more than they had known yesterday.

At the workshop, Mingzhao examined the supplier's sample.

The sample had arrived shortly before noon.

It was a length of cloth cut from the actual batch, accompanied by a written statement of the material's origin, thread count, and quoted price.

Mingzhao laid it beside the previously approved reference sample.

Luo Shun stood opposite her.

"The supplier says this is from the batch he will sell us."

"Did you watch him cut it?"

"No. The messenger brought it sealed."

"Then we have his written statement and the sample. We do not have independent confirmation that every bolt in the batch is identical."

Luo Shun nodded.

"Should we ask him to open one full bolt?"

"Not yet. First examine this."

Mingzhao measured the width.

Then she checked the weave and examined the surface under the light.

The sample was close to the reference cloth, but there was a slight difference in the density of the weave.

She called one of the experienced workers.

"Look at these two pieces."

The worker examined them.

"The new sample is a little tighter."

"Would that affect the folding?"

"Not much, but it may affect the way the cloth falls."

"Would the difference be visible in a finished bolt?"

"Possibly, depending on the dye."

Mingzhao looked at the foreman.

"Did the supplier state that the batch had already been dyed?"

"No. He quoted the undyed material."

"Then the sample is useful for checking the weave, not the final colour."

"Yes."

She returned to the paper quotation.

The material price was acceptable. The minimum quantity was still limited enough for the trial. But the colour would depend on the dyeing process, and the merchant who might inspect the finished goods had expressed interest in a particular shade.

Mingzhao set the sample down.

"We will not purchase the full quantity today."

Luo Shun looked at her.

"Would you like the supplier to dye a smaller sample?"

"Yes. Ask for the proposed shade using the reference piece. The dyeing cost must be written separately."

"Should we pay for the sample?"

"If the price is reasonable, yes. Record it as a sample expense. Do not include it in the material cost of the six bolts."

The foreman nodded.

Mingzhao continued.

"If the dyed sample matches, we can authorize the materials. If it does not, we do not proceed merely because the supplier has already prepared the undyed cloth."

"Understood."

"Also, the merchant must be told that the six-bolt trial is intended for inspection. He has not promised to buy it."

"I will make that clear."

The foreman gathered the papers.

Mingzhao retained the two cloth samples.

She examined them again after he left.

A difference that appeared small on a loose piece might become obvious when repeated across six bolts. The workshop had learned this from earlier work. A sample could look acceptable on a table and still behave differently during folding, finishing, or handling.

She had no intention of paying for six bolts before she knew what the finished cloth would be.

There was a knock.

Qiao Yue entered with a message.

"The Marquisate has sent another letter."

Mingzhao did not look up immediately.

"What does it say?"

"The records office requests that you identify whether the damaged register page from the first box is connected with the four-cart dispatch."

Mingzhao put down the sample.

"Have they sent a copy?"

"Yes."

Qiao Yue handed her the paper.

Mingzhao examined the page.

The writing concerned cart repairs and temporary transport payments. There was no cargo entry, no destination, and no reference to the four-cart dispatch.

"Why do they ask me?"

"The message says that you managed the estate accounts during the relevant period."

Mingzhao read the letter again.

She had been responsible for the estate's accounts, but that did not mean every old record belonged to her knowledge. The damaged page had not yet been compared with the original transfer list or any surviving dispatch record.

She wrote a brief reply.

*The copy provided contains no identifiable reference to the four-cart dispatch. I cannot establish a connection from the page alone. Please compare its date, office heading, and register sequence with the surviving movement records before drawing any conclusion.*

She handed the reply to Qiao Yue.

"Send it."

Qiao Yue looked at the damaged page.

"Could it still be related?"

"It could."

"Then why not say so?"

"Because a possibility is not a conclusion."

Qiao Yue nodded.

Mingzhao returned to the cloth samples.

The workshop had enough uncertainty of its own.

She did not intend to lend the Marquisate any additional certainty that it had not earned.

At the palace, Yuru received a visitor in the late afternoon.

It was not the Dowager.

It was a young woman from the Xu family, the daughter of one of Yuru's cousins. She had visited the palace before Yuru's resignation, but had never been comfortable speaking freely in the former Empress's presence.

Today she arrived carrying a small basket.

Yuru welcomed her into the sitting room.

"What have you brought?"

The young woman placed the basket on the table.

"Some dried fruit from my mother's household. She said you might like it."

Yuru looked inside.

There were several small packets, each wrapped in paper.

"Your mother has sent enough for a household."

"She said you might share it with the attendants."

"Then she has sent enough for two households."

The young woman smiled.

Yuru invited her to sit.

They spoke first about the Xu family, then about the young woman's studies. She had been learning to keep household accounts under the supervision of an older aunt.

"I find the figures difficult," she admitted.

"Which part?"

"Remembering which payments belong to which account."

Yuru considered this.

"Then do not rely on memory."

The young woman looked surprised.

"Should I write everything down?"

"Write the date, amount, recipient, purpose, and the account from which the money was paid. If one of those is unknown, leave it unknown until you have evidence."

"Would that not make the account look incomplete?"

"It would make it incomplete."

The young woman waited.

Yuru continued, "An incomplete account is preferable to a complete-looking account that is wrong."

The young woman nodded slowly.

"That sounds simple."

"It is simple."

"Is it easy?"

Yuru smiled.

"That is another matter."

The young woman laughed.

They spoke for some time longer. The visitor asked whether Yuru had chosen a permanent residence.

"Not yet," Yuru replied.

"Will you return to the Duke's residence?"

"Perhaps."

"Or remain here?"

"Perhaps."

The young woman seemed uncertain whether she was allowed to ask another question.

Yuru poured tea.

"You may ask."

"Do you not feel uncomfortable staying somewhere temporary?"

"Sometimes."

"Then why not decide?"

"Because I have not yet decided."

The young woman looked at her.

It was evidently not the answer she had expected.

Yuru added, "There is no benefit in choosing a residence merely to end the discomfort of not having chosen one."

The visitor considered this.

Then she looked down at her cup.

"I think I understand."

Yuru did not ask her to explain.

The visit ended before the evening meal. The young woman left with a packet of dried fruit for her mother and a promise that Yuru would visit the family when she wished.

After she had gone, Fang Ru collected the cups.

"Was she comfortable?"

"More comfortable than when she arrived."

"She seemed to enjoy speaking with you."

"Then I am glad she came."

Fang Ru glanced at the basket.

"Shall I divide the fruit among the attendants?"

"Yes. Keep a little for yourself."

Fang Ru hesitated.

"Madam, it was sent for you."

"And I am sharing it."

"Yes, Madam."

Yuru looked toward the window.

The sun was beginning to lower.

She had received a visitor, answered a question, shared some fruit, and spent an afternoon without arranging anything for the imperial household.

It had been a good day.

At the Marquisate, Mingyuan received Mingzhao's reply before the evening meal.

He read it twice.

"She says the damaged page does not establish a connection."

Steward Zhou stood beside the desk.

"That is correct."

"She has asked us to compare the date, office heading, and register sequence."

"That is also reasonable."

Mingyuan looked at him.

"You agree with her?"

"I agree that the page should be examined before we decide what it means."

Mingyuan put the reply down.

"Then we will compare it."

Steward Zhou waited.

"Young Master, the records office has asked whether you wish to continue opening the remaining boxes tomorrow."

"Yes."

"All six?"

"Yes."

"Even if the northern dispatch book is not found?"

Mingyuan looked at the transfer list.

"Especially then."

Steward Zhou nodded.

Mingyuan continued, "We should also identify which office produced each bundle. The labels are not reliable enough by themselves."

"That will take time."

"I know."

Steward Zhou did not argue.

He had spent many years managing the estate's daily operations. He knew that the quickest way to finish a search was sometimes to stop asking questions. It was also the quickest way to make the same problem return.

Mingyuan looked at the grain schedule.

The unexplained twenty-seven sacks remained unexplained.

The four-cart dispatch remained a recorded movement with unknown cargo and incomplete destination.

The old purchasing voucher remained missing.

The damaged page had not changed any of those facts.

He signed the instruction to continue the inventory.

Then he set the brush down.

"Send the records office a copy of Mingzhao's reply. Ask them to compare the page with the movement-record sequence before requesting another opinion from her."

"Yes, Young Master."

Steward Zhou collected the papers.

As he reached the door, Mingyuan spoke again.

"Steward Zhou."

"Yes?"

"How many boxes have we examined?"

"One."

"How many remain?"

"Five."

Mingyuan looked at the inventory.

"Then tomorrow we shall examine the second."

Steward Zhou bowed.

The investigation was proceeding.

It was proceeding so slowly that the family might eventually discover the virtue of patience.

Unfortunately, the family had not yet been informed that patience was required.

And the six boxes, which had been labelled miscellaneous because no one had wished to identify their contents, were already demonstrating that the old records had possessed a much more complicated understanding of the word miscellaneous than anyone had expected.
