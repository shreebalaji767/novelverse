# **Chapter 2: The Family Wants a Peaceful Discussion. The Empress Wants a Department That Can Count.**

**24th Day of the Third Month, 18th Year of Yonghe.**

The Shen family had prepared a generous breakfast.

This was not a sign of affection.

Shen Mingzhao knew her family well enough to recognize the difference between an ordinary breakfast and one arranged for a negotiation. The table in the main reception hall had been filled with delicate pastries, steamed buns, fresh fruit, warm porridge, and several dishes that were usually reserved for important guests.

There were also more servants than usual.

Her mother had clearly decided that a dispute over family property should not look like a dispute over family property.

Mingzhao arrived at the appointed hour.

She wore a pale green robe with a narrow silver border. Her hair was arranged in a simple style, and she carried no document case. The documents required for the settlement were already being prepared in her eastern courtyard. She had no intention of bringing every account book to a breakfast table.

Qiao Yue followed her as far as the entrance.

"Young Miss, should I wait outside?"

"Yes."

"Should I bring the account records if they ask?"

"No. They know where the records are."

Qiao Yue nodded.

Mingzhao entered.

Old Madam Shen was already seated. Madam Liang sat beside her, while Shen Guowei occupied the opposite chair. Shen Mingyuan and Shen Mingxiu had also arrived.

A servant poured tea.

"Grandmother. Father. Mother."

Mingzhao greeted them and sat down.

Old Madam Shen looked at her for a moment.

"You look tired."

"I slept well, Grandmother."

"Then you should eat more."

"Thank you."

The old lady gestured to the dishes.

"Eat first. We can discuss matters afterward."

Mingzhao picked up her chopsticks.

She had no objection to eating.

If her family wished to spend money on a good breakfast before arguing about money, she saw no reason to interfere.

For several minutes, the conversation remained ordinary. Old Madam Shen asked about the spring weather. Madam Liang mentioned that a distant relative had sent a letter. Shen Mingxiu spoke about an upcoming gathering at the residence of a countess.

Mingzhao listened without contributing much.

Then Madam Liang put down her chopsticks.

"Mingzhao, your grandmother and I discussed your request last night."

Mingzhao looked up.

"I see."

"Your father also considered it."

Shen Guowei cleared his throat.

"We believe there may have been a misunderstanding."

"There was no misunderstanding."

Madam Liang's smile tightened.

"You are still young. It is natural to feel dissatisfied when responsibilities become heavy. But a family is not a place where every task must be calculated."

"Then the family should have no objection to calculating its property."

The table went quiet.

Shen Mingyuan lowered his head toward his porridge.

Shen Mingxiu glanced at her sister.

Old Madam Shen tapped her chopsticks lightly against the edge of her bowl.

"Do not answer every sentence with another argument."

Mingzhao lowered her chopsticks.

"I am listening, Grandmother."

Her grandmother regarded her.

"You have been managing the household for years. Your father acknowledges your work. Your mother acknowledges it. Your brother knows how much you have helped him. Why turn a family matter into a formal division?"

"Because acknowledgment does not settle ownership."

Madam Liang sighed.

"Must you speak as though we are strangers?"

"No. If we were strangers, I would have required a contract before lending the household money."

Shen Guowei's expression darkened.

"Mingzhao."

She stopped.

He leaned forward.

"Your request concerns more than your personal property. The family estate is not a shop that can be divided whenever someone becomes dissatisfied."

"I know."

"The ancestral lands cannot be divided carelessly. The title and the estate are connected. The household has obligations."

"I know."

"Then why are you demanding a division?"

"I am not demanding that the ancestral estate be broken apart. I am asking for my personal property, documented claims, and any portion that can legally be settled to be identified. The property that cannot be divided will remain with the family."

Her father looked at her.

"Do you intend to leave the Marquisate?"

"Yes."

"Where will you go?"

"I am looking for a residence."

"Have you already found one?"

"No."

"Then you have not considered this properly."

Mingzhao took a sip of tea.

"I have considered it sufficiently to know that I need a residence. I have not considered it sufficiently to purchase one without inspecting the property."

Shen Mingyuan could not help speaking.

"Eldest Sister, surely you do not need to move out simply because you are unhappy with the household accounts."

Mingzhao turned to him.

"How many times have you asked the steward to pay an expense without submitting the supporting documents?"

He frowned.

"I do not remember."

"That is precisely the problem."

"I am the heir. Some matters must be handled quickly."

"Then you should be particularly careful about the records. You will inherit responsibility for the estate, not merely its name."

Shen Mingyuan's face flushed.

"Are you saying I cannot manage it?"

"I am saying you have not yet demonstrated that you can."

"Enough!" Madam Liang interrupted.

She looked at her daughter with unmistakable displeasure.

"Your brother is still learning. You should guide him, not humiliate him."

"I have guided him."

"Then continue."

Mingzhao looked at her mother.

"Why?"

Madam Liang stared.

The question was so simple that she seemed unable to decide which part of it to answer.

Mingzhao continued, "If I remain responsible for teaching him, managing the accounts, checking the suppliers, and correcting the household's mistakes, when will he become responsible for anything himself?"

"That is a matter for your father."

"Then Father should arrange it."

Shen Guowei's jaw tightened.

The discussion had reached the point he had hoped to avoid.

He had expected his daughter to complain. He had expected her to demand an apology, perhaps an increase in her allowance or a promise that her younger brother would be more careful.

He had not expected her to insist on documents.

It was difficult to negotiate with someone who had already decided that feelings were not the same thing as evidence.

Old Madam Shen set down her chopsticks.

"Let us speak plainly. What would satisfy you?"

Mingzhao looked at her grandmother.

"A formal inventory, independent valuation where necessary, recognition of my personal property, verification of my loans to the household, and a written settlement of any property that can lawfully be divided."

"And after that?"

"I will establish my own household."

"Will you still visit?"

"If I am welcome, and if I wish to."

Old Madam Shen frowned.

"You make it sound as though we must invite you to your own family home."

"I am not asking for an invitation to visit. I am asking for the right to decide where I live."

For a while, no one spoke.

Then Shen Guowei finally said, "I will appoint a family representative to review your claims."

Mingzhao nodded.

"Please appoint someone who is not responsible for the accounts being reviewed."

Her father looked irritated.

"Do you suspect your family of dishonesty?"

"I suspect that a person should not be asked to verify their own work."

Shen Mingxiu lowered her eyes.

The answer was difficult to argue with.

Madam Liang's expression remained cold.

"Your father will decide who is suitable."

"Of course."

Mingzhao put down her teacup.

"May I ask one more thing?"

"What?" Shen Guowei asked.

"Until the settlement is complete, who will approve the ordinary household expenses?"

Her father looked at her.

"You have always handled them."

"I will continue to handle necessary matters during the transition. But I will not approve undocumented expenditure or new commitments that have not been reviewed."

Shen Mingyuan frowned.

"Does that mean I must submit a receipt every time I need money?"

"If you are requesting payment from the household account, yes."

"Even for small amounts?"

"Small amounts become large amounts when no one records them."

He looked toward his mother.

Madam Liang sighed.

"Your sister has become very particular."

Mingzhao picked up her chopsticks again.

"That is why the accounts are still in order."

The breakfast ended without a settlement.

But it had produced something useful: her father had agreed to appoint a representative.

Mingzhao considered that progress.

It was not much progress.

Still, she had never expected much from one breakfast.

By midmorning, the first official response to the Empress's resignation review had reached the Grand Secretary's office.

It was not a report.

It was a request for clarification.

The Ministry of Rites had asked whether it was expected to assume responsibility for ceremonial expenditure currently approved through Chengming Palace.

The Department of Palace Stores had asked whether the Ministry of Revenue would provide additional funds if procurement authority were transferred.

The Ministry of Personnel had asked whether palace administrators would remain under the existing appointment system or be reorganized.

The Grand Secretary read the three documents in silence.

His chief clerk stood nearby.

"Your Excellency, should we send the requests to His Majesty?"

"Not yet."

"Should we reply?"

"Yes."

The Grand Secretary picked up his brush.

"Tell the Ministry of Rites that it is not being asked to assume expenditure. It is being asked to identify which ceremonial matters fall under its existing authority."

"Yes, Your Excellency."

"Tell the Department of Palace Stores that it must first provide a complete account of its current procurement responsibilities. The question of additional funds is premature."

"Yes."

"And the Ministry of Personnel?"

He paused.

"Tell them to identify the appointment rules currently in force. We are not reorganizing the entire palace merely because the Empress has submitted a petition."

The clerk bowed.

"Understood."

The Grand Secretary returned to the review documents.

He had been given a straightforward task: identify the Empress's responsibilities and determine how they could be transferred.

Instead, every department seemed determined to establish that the task belonged to someone else.

He reached for the first list.

The opening item concerned the maintenance of palace residences.

He read the existing arrangement.

The Department of Works supervised repairs. Palace Stores supplied materials. The inner palace administrators submitted requests. The Empress's office approved the final schedule.

The Grand Secretary frowned.

"Why does the Empress approve the final schedule?"

The clerk consulted the notes.

"Because the residences are occupied by members of the imperial family, Your Excellency."

"That explains why the Empress should be informed. It does not explain why she must approve the replacement of roof tiles."

The clerk remained silent.

The Grand Secretary placed a mark beside the entry.

"Continue."

The next item concerned the distribution of seasonal clothing.

The next concerned the appointment of senior servants.

The next concerned the education arrangements for the younger princes and princesses.

By the time he reached the twentieth item, the Grand Secretary had begun to understand the scale of the problem.

The Empress's office was not merely an office.

It was the place where every unfinished matter eventually arrived.

At Chengming Palace, Xu Yuru was unaware of the departmental disagreement.

She was reviewing the palace stores inventory when Madam Fang entered.

"Your Majesty, the Department of Palace Stores has submitted its revised proposal."

Xu Yuru looked up.

"Already?"

"They have removed the request for twelve hundred new blankets."

"Good."

"They now request six hundred."

Xu Yuru put down the inventory.

"How many blankets remain usable?"

"One hundred and sixty in storage, according to the last report."

"How many require repair?"

"One hundred and twenty."

"And how many are damaged beyond repair?"

"That has not yet been confirmed."

Xu Yuru looked at Madam Fang.

"Then what exactly have they calculated?"

Madam Fang hesitated.

"They say the number six hundred is a preliminary estimate."

"An estimate based on what?"

"The expected requirements of the coming winter."

"Have they calculated the number of people who require blankets?"

"They have not attached that figure."

"Have they calculated the expected loss of existing stock?"

"No."

"Have they inspected the warehouse?"

"Not yet."

Xu Yuru closed the proposal.

"Return it."

Madam Fang accepted the paper.

"Should I explain the required calculations?"

"Yes. Ask them to provide the current stock, the number of blankets requiring repair, the number beyond repair, the number of people requiring supplies, and the expected reserve. Then they may submit a purchase request."

"Understood."

Madam Fang turned to leave.

"Your Majesty," she said, stopping at the door, "the Department of Palace Stores has also asked whether the Empress's office will continue approving all procurement schedules."

Xu Yuru looked at her.

"Why are they asking that?"

"They heard that the Emperor has ordered a review of the inner palace administration."

Xu Yuru's fingers rested against the edge of the desk.

The review had begun.

She had expected it to begin.

She had not expected the departments to ask her whether they should continue sending her the same work.

"Tell them that until His Majesty issues a decision, the existing rules remain in force. Routine matters should be handled according to their current authority."

"Yes, Your Majesty."

"And tell them that a proposal is not a decision. If they have the authority to calculate a requirement, they should calculate it."

Madam Fang bowed.

"Yes, Your Majesty."

When she left, Xu Yuru looked at the empty space where the proposal had been.

She had not yet told the palace administrators that she intended to resign.

The Emperor had not accepted the petition.

The review had only begun.

But already, the departments were behaving as though the most important question was whether they could continue asking her to do their work.

Xu Yuru took a slow breath.

Perhaps the transition would be more difficult than she had anticipated.

At the Qingyuan Marquisate, the appointment of a family representative had become a new source of disagreement.

Shen Guowei had originally intended to appoint the household's chief steward.

Madam Liang had objected.

The chief steward, she argued, was too closely connected to Mingzhao's accounting work.

Shen Mingyuan had suggested a senior manager from his own residence.

Mingzhao had rejected the suggestion before her father could consider it.

The manager in question had personally signed several of the expenditure records under review.

It would be unreasonable to ask him to verify his own signatures.

By noon, the family had not agreed on anyone.

Steward Zhou brought the news to Mingzhao.

She was in her courtyard, examining a list of commercial investments.

"The Marquis has not appointed a representative?" she asked.

"Not yet, Young Miss."

"Why?"

"Several candidates have been suggested, but none has been accepted."

"Have they suggested an independent accountant?"

"Not yet."

"Then suggest one."

Steward Zhou hesitated.

"Would the family agree?"

"That is their decision. We need someone qualified to review the figures."

He nodded.

Mingzhao turned to the next page.

The investments listed there were not large enough to make her wealthy. They consisted of modest shares in two trading partnerships, a small interest in a silk warehouse, and a loan secured against a merchant's inventory.

The assets were valuable, but their value depended on accurate records and actual business performance.

She had no intention of treating the estimated figures as money already in her possession.

"Have the trading partners been informed that I am reviewing my accounts?"

"Not yet."

"Send a notice. Do not tell them the family is being divided. Simply inform them that I am reviewing my personal investments and will confirm the current balances."

"Yes, Young Miss."

"And ask for copies of the partnership agreements."

"Understood."

Qiao Yue entered with a small tray.

"Young Miss, the kitchen has prepared lunch."

"Put it there."

Qiao Yue glanced at the account books.

"Have you been working since breakfast?"

"Yes."

"Then you should eat."

"I will."

Qiao Yue looked at the stack of papers.

"How many documents must be reviewed?"

"All of them."

"All?"

"All the documents that concern my property."

Qiao Yue sighed.

"Then perhaps we should buy a residence with a large study."

Mingzhao looked up.

"We have not even found a residence."

"But we will need a study."

"We will need a roof first."

Qiao Yue nodded.

"And a kitchen."

"And a kitchen."

"And servants' rooms."

"Yes."

"And a proper courtyard."

Mingzhao put down her brush.

"Qiao Yue."

"Yes, Young Miss?"

"Are you planning to purchase the residence yourself?"

Qiao Yue lowered her head.

"No."

"Then let me finish the accounts before you design the house."

Qiao Yue smiled faintly and placed the lunch tray on the table.

Mingzhao looked at the food.

For the first time that day, she realized how hungry she was.

She set aside the documents.

There would be time to review them after lunch.

There would be time to argue with her family tomorrow.

There would be time to find a residence, negotiate a settlement, and establish an independent household.

She had spent six years treating every delay as a personal failure.

She was beginning to understand that some matters simply required time.

That did not mean she had to surrender control of them.

In the afternoon, the Emperor received the first preliminary report.

It was considerably shorter than he expected.

"Only twelve responsibilities have been reviewed?" Zhao Jingheng asked.

The Grand Secretary bowed.

"Yes, Your Majesty."

"And how many have been assigned?"

"Five."

"Five?"

"Three are already handled by established departments. Two require clarification of authority."

"What about the remaining seven?"

"They overlap between offices."

The Emperor looked at the report.

"Explain."

The Grand Secretary opened the relevant schedule.

"The Department of Palace Stores supplies materials for residence repairs. The Department of Works supervises the repairs. The inner palace administrators arrange access to the residences. The Empress's office approves the final schedule and resolves disagreements."

Zhao Jingheng frowned.

"Why can they not coordinate among themselves?"

"The departments have different regulations concerning expenditure, procurement, and access."

"Then the regulations should be clarified."

"Yes, Your Majesty."

The Emperor turned to the next page.

"Seasonal clothing?"

"The Department of Palace Stores purchases the clothing. The inner palace administrators distribute it. The Empress's office approves the list of recipients."

"Why?"

"The list includes members of the imperial family and senior palace officials."

"That explains the list. It does not explain why the Empress must approve every distribution."

The Grand Secretary bowed.

"That question is included in the review."

Zhao Jingheng set down the report.

"How many of the eighty-seven responsibilities are routine?"

"We have not yet established a final number."

"Then establish it."

"Yes, Your Majesty."

The Emperor looked toward the window.

He was beginning to understand why Xu Yuru had submitted a transition plan rather than simply asking to stop working.

The problem was not that the palace had no officials.

The problem was that the officials had become accustomed to passing responsibility upward until it reached the person least likely to refuse.

"Grand Secretary," he said.

"Your Majesty?"

"Do not let this review become an excuse for every department to demand additional staff and funds."

The Grand Secretary's expression remained solemn.

"I will be careful."

"And do not let them use the Empress's petition to argue that the imperial household is in disorder."

"Understood."

The Emperor paused.

"Has the Empress been informed of the review's progress?"

"No, Your Majesty."

"Good. Do not send her the reports yet."

The Grand Secretary bowed.

Zhao Jingheng picked up the next memorial.

He had other matters to attend to.

The empire did not stop because the Empress wished to resign.

Unfortunately, the inner palace seemed determined to make the resignation itself a matter requiring the entire empire.

That evening, Xu Yuru received a visitor.

It was her daughter, Princess Zhao Minglan.

At sixteen, the Third Princess had developed a habit of appearing in Chengming Palace whenever she was curious about something. She was not careless enough to interrupt formal audiences, but she was sufficiently observant to notice when her mother's daily routine changed.

She entered carrying a small wooden box.

"Mother."

Xu Yuru looked up from her papers.

"Minglan. What is that?"

"A set of account books."

"Why are you bringing account books to my study?"

"Because I wanted to ask you something."

The princess placed the box on the table.

Xu Yuru opened it.

Inside were three small ledgers, each containing records of the princess's monthly allowance and personal expenditure.

The handwriting was neat, though the calculations were not entirely consistent.

Xu Yuru turned several pages.

"Who prepared these?"

"I did."

"Why?"

Minglan sat down.

"I want to know how much I spend each month."

"That is sensible."

"I also want to know whether I have enough saved to purchase books for the spring examination."

Xu Yuru looked at her daughter.

"You are not taking the examination."

"I know. But I want to purchase the recommended texts and study them."

"Why?"

"Because the Crown Prince says I should understand the reports I read. He says that if I wish to discuss administration, I should know how the figures are prepared."

Xu Yuru turned another page.

"That is good advice."

Minglan leaned forward.

"Mother, can you teach me how to review these accounts?"

Xu Yuru studied her daughter's face.

The request was ordinary.

It was also precisely the sort of request she wanted to encourage.

She pointed to a line in the ledger.

"What does this entry mean?"

"Three strings for paper."

"How many sheets did you purchase?"

Minglan hesitated.

"I did not record the number."

"Then how will you know whether the price was reasonable?"

"I can ask the shop."

"That will help. But next time, record the quantity, price per unit, and total amount."

Minglan nodded.

Xu Yuru moved to the next entry.

"And this?"

"Two strings for ink."

"How much ink?"

"One box."

"Then write one box, not merely two strings. Money tells you what you paid. Quantity tells you what you received."

Minglan listened carefully.

For several minutes, they reviewed the small accounts together.

Then the princess looked at the documents on her mother's desk.

"Mother, why are there so many papers?"

Xu Yuru glanced at the stack.

"Because the palace has many matters to manage."

"Are you doing all of them?"

"Not all of them."

Minglan looked at the pile.

"Are you sure?"

Xu Yuru paused.

The princess had asked the question without any hidden meaning.

That made it more difficult to dismiss.

"I am working on changing the arrangement."

"What arrangement?"

Xu Yuru considered how much to explain.

She did not want her daughter to hear rumors from servants or misunderstand the situation.

"I have asked your father to review the administration of the inner palace. There are responsibilities that should be handled by the departments assigned to them, rather than sent to me."

"Why?"

"Because a person should not become the only support holding up an entire system."

Minglan looked down at her account books.

"Then who will handle them?"

"The people whose duties they are."

"And if they do not know how?"

"Then they must learn."

The princess thought about this.

"Does that mean you will have more time?"

"That is the intention."

"Then perhaps you can teach me more about accounts."

Xu Yuru smiled slightly.

"If you are willing to study them properly."

"I am."

"Then we shall begin with your own expenses."

Minglan looked at her ledger.

"All of them?"

"All of them."

The princess's expression became less certain.

Xu Yuru returned the ledger to her.

"Administration begins with knowing what you have spent."

Minglan sighed.

"That sounds like a great deal of work."

"It is."

The Empress looked at her daughter.

"But it is better to understand the work than to pretend it does not exist."

The next morning's family meeting at the Marquisate would not settle the division.

The palace review would not settle the Empress's resignation.

Neither woman had received what she wanted.

But both had achieved something important.

Xu Yuru had forced the imperial household to examine the responsibilities it had allowed to accumulate around her.

Shen Mingzhao had forced her family to acknowledge that her property and her labor were not the same thing as the Marquisate's inheritance.

The consequences were only beginning.

At the end of the day, the Grand Secretary placed a new report on the Emperor's desk.

Zhao Jingheng read the heading.

**Preliminary Review of the Eighty-Seven Responsibilities Listed in Her Majesty the Empress's Petition.**

He turned to the first page.

Then the second.

Then the third.

On the fourth page, he stopped.

"Why," he asked slowly, "is the Empress listed as the approving authority for the annual replacement of palace brooms?"

The Grand Secretary lowered his head.

"Your Majesty, the department has followed this procedure for many years."

"Does the Empress personally inspect the brooms?"

"No, Your Majesty."

"Does she determine how many are required?"

"No, Your Majesty."

"Does she negotiate their price?"

"No, Your Majesty."

"Then why does she approve the purchase?"

The Grand Secretary was silent.

Zhao Jingheng looked at the report.

After a long pause, he said, "Find out."

The Grand Secretary bowed.

"Yes, Your Majesty."

The Emperor turned another page.

The next item concerned the replacement of kitchen ladles.

He put the report down.

For eighteen years, he had believed the imperial household was functioning under the Empress's supervision.

He was beginning to suspect that the Empress had been personally supervising a very large number of brooms.

And ladles.

And blankets.

And cushions.

He pressed two fingers against his forehead.

"Grand Secretary."

"Your Majesty?"

"How many more items are there?"

"Eighty-three remain under review."

The Emperor closed his eyes.

"Send me the report when they are finished."

"Yes, Your Majesty."

"And do not include the brooms in the next audience."

The Grand Secretary bowed.

"Understood."

He withdrew.

Zhao Jingheng sat alone with the report.

Then, very quietly, he said:

"She really does intend to leave."

**End of Chapter 2**
