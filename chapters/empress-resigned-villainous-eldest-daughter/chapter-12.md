# **Chapter 12: The Empress Has Been Asked to Stop Solving Everyone's Problems. The Marquis's Heir Has Been Asked to Start Solving His Own.**

**4th Day of the Fourth Month, 18th Year of Yonghe.**

The first difficulty with transferring responsibility was discovering that the person receiving it had already developed a habit of sending it elsewhere.

The second difficulty was discovering that the person transferring it had developed the same habit in reverse.

In Chengming Palace, Empress Xu Yuru discovered both before breakfast.

A report had arrived from the Imperial Household Administration concerning the distribution of medicinal supplies to senior palace attendants.

It was a modest matter.

The palace maintained a reserve of common medicines for attendants who had served for many years. The medicines were not expensive individually, but the supply required regular inspection, proper storage, and records of distribution. Certain preparations had to be replaced before their usefulness declined. Others could be retained longer if properly sealed.

The report contained a list of names, quantities, and dates.

It also contained a final line.

*Submitted for the Empress's approval.*

Yuru read it twice.

Then she placed it on the table.

"Who prepared this?"

Fang Ru, standing beside the desk, glanced at the report.

"An assistant in the Imperial Household Administration."

"Who checked the quantities?"

"The pharmacy storekeeper."

"Who checked whether the recipients were eligible?"

"The senior women's office."

"Who authorized the distribution?"

Fang Ru paused.

"The Empress's office has traditionally authorized it."

"Under which regulation?"

Fang Ru did not immediately answer.

Yuru looked at her.

"Is there a regulation?"

"There is an old instruction concerning the provision of medicines to senior attendants."

"Does it require my personal approval?"

"I would have to check the original wording."

"Then check it."

Fang Ru accepted the report.

Yuru turned to the next document.

It was a request for replacement bedding in one of the older residential buildings.

That report also ended with a request for the Empress's approval.

Yuru closed her eyes for a moment.

"Fang Ru."

"Yes, Your Majesty?"

"How many matters did we receive yesterday?"

"Thirty-one."

"How many required my personal decision?"

"Four, according to the register."

"And how many were sent here because someone thought I might decide more quickly?"

Fang Ru considered the question.

"Possibly the remaining twenty-seven."

"Then the register is improving."

Fang Ru looked uncertain whether that was praise.

Yuru took a sip of tea.

"It is also revealing how much work has been performed by people who do not wish to be responsible for their own work."

Fang Ru lowered her eyes.

"That is not an uncommon difficulty."

"No. It is merely an expensive one."

The Emperor had received a similar report before breakfast.

He had also received a request from the Ministry of Rites concerning a ceremonial seating arrangement.

The request was not unusual.

A senior official's wife had complained that her position during an upcoming palace gathering did not reflect the precedence of her husband's current office. The matter involved a ceremonial regulation, a change in the official's rank, and an earlier arrangement that had apparently been copied without checking the revised appointment record.

The Ministry of Rites wanted the Empress to decide.

The Emperor read the final sentence.

Then he looked at Gao Shun.

"Why?"

Gao Shun bowed.

"Your Majesty?"

"Why does the Ministry of Rites want the Empress to decide a matter governed by its own ceremonial regulations?"

"The ministry may believe Her Majesty's judgment would prevent further complaints."

"Would it?"

"Her Majesty is experienced in such matters."

"That was not my question."

Gao Shun remained silent.

Zhao Jingheng put down the report.

"Send it back. The Ministry of Rites should determine the correct precedence under the existing regulations. If the regulations are unclear, it should identify the unclear provision."

"Yes, Your Majesty."

"And tell them not to write that the Empress has approved an arrangement merely because she has read their report."

Gao Shun bowed again.

The Emperor picked up the medicinal-supply report.

He read it.

Then he put it beside the ceremonial complaint.

"These are different matters."

"Yes, Your Majesty."

"One concerns medicines. The other concerns precedence."

"Yes."

"Why are both waiting for the same person?"

Gao Shun had served in the palace long enough to know that the question was not asking him to identify a person.

It was asking him to identify an arrangement.

"Because the arrangement has existed for many years," he replied carefully.

Zhao Jingheng looked at him.

"Then it has existed for many years."

He summoned the Crown Prince.

Zhao Cheng'an arrived with a document case under his arm.

He had spent the previous evening examining the Eastern Palace's clothing and fuel accounts. He had also discovered that the steward responsible for recording household purchases had been using two different formats for the same category of expenditure.

The Crown Prince had not been pleased.

The steward had been even less pleased.

Neither had slept particularly well.

"Father," Cheng'an greeted him.

"Sit."

The Emperor pushed the medicinal-supply report across the table.

"Read this."

The Crown Prince examined it.

After a moment, he said, "This concerns the senior attendants' medical reserve."

"Who should decide it?"

"The Imperial Household Administration should verify the reserve and eligibility. The pharmacy storekeeper should confirm the quantity and condition of the medicines."

"And who should authorize distribution?"

"That depends on the existing regulation."

"Good. What if the regulation assigns authorization to the Empress?"

"Then the Empress has the authority under that regulation."

The Emperor nodded.

"And if it does not?"

"Then the office responsible under the regulation should act."

"What if the office is uncertain?"

"Then it should submit the relevant provision for clarification rather than sending the entire matter to the Empress."

Zhao Jingheng leaned back.

"Why did you answer correctly?"

Cheng'an looked at him.

"Because the question is straightforward."

"Then why did the Imperial Household Administration not answer it?"

The Crown Prince looked down at the report again.

He understood.

"Because it has become accustomed to treating Her Majesty's approval as a final safeguard."

"And what happens when that safeguard is withdrawn?"

"The administration must establish its own procedure."

"Can it?"

"It should be able to."

The Emperor gave him a flat look.

"That is not the same as saying it can."

Cheng'an accepted the correction.

"No. It is not."

The Emperor tapped the report.

"You will attend the meeting concerning the senior attendants' reserve. You are not to decide the matter for them. You are to identify the authority, the process, and the records that must exist."

"Yes, Father."

"And if someone asks you to send the matter to your mother?"

"I will ask why."

"Good."

The Emperor paused.

"Do not ask her afterward."

Cheng'an understood that instruction more clearly than he wished to.

"Yes, Father."

In Chengming Palace, Yuru was already examining the original regulation.

Fang Ru had retrieved it from the archives.

The paper was old, the ink slightly faded, and the language unnecessarily elaborate.

Yuru read the relevant passage.

Then she read it again.

"It does not say that I must approve every distribution."

"No, Your Majesty."

"It says the Imperial Household Administration must report unusual cases to the inner palace."

"Yes."

"And unusual cases are to be reviewed by the appropriate senior authority."

"Yes."

"Which senior authority?"

Fang Ru looked at the document.

"The wording does not specify."

Yuru put down the paper.

"Then the regulation is incomplete."

"It may have been understood through custom."

"Custom is useful when everyone understands it."

Fang Ru did not disagree.

Yuru drew a line beneath the sentence.

"Who has been making the decisions?"

"Usually the senior administrator, after consultation with the Empress's office."

"Then the office has been making the decisions."

"Yes."

"Why does the report say it requires my approval?"

Fang Ru hesitated.

"Because the final decision has traditionally been presented to Your Majesty."

"That is a description of habit, not authority."

Yuru took a fresh sheet.

She wrote three headings.

**Ordinary distribution.**

**Unusual or disputed cases.**

**Emergency provision.**

"Ordinary distribution should follow the eligibility list and verified stock. Unusual cases should be reviewed by the senior administrator, with a written explanation if an exception is made. Emergency provision should not wait for a meeting."

Fang Ru listened carefully.

"Should the Empress's office receive a monthly report?"

Yuru considered it.

"Not for approval. A summary may be useful during the transition, but it should go to the office responsible for oversight."

"Which office?"

"That is what we are determining."

Yuru stopped writing.

She looked at the report again.

For a moment, she considered calling the senior administrator and explaining the procedure herself.

It would take perhaps half an hour.

She could make the matter clear, answer the questions, and ensure the next distribution proceeded without difficulty.

It would also teach everyone that the correct way to resolve uncertainty was to wait until the Empress explained it.

She put down her brush.

"Arrange a meeting with the senior administrator. The Crown Prince will attend."

Fang Ru nodded.

"And I will not chair it."

Fang Ru's expression changed slightly.

"Your Majesty?"

"I will attend only if the matter requires clarification of the old regulation. Otherwise, they should conduct their own review."

Yuru looked at the report once more.

"Let us see whether they can finish a meeting without asking me to finish it for them."

At the Qingyuan Marquisate, Shen Mingyuan had discovered that being the heir was considerably less comfortable when the accounts were placed in front of him.

He sat in the eastern study with three ledgers, two bundles of receipts, and a sheet of paper on which he had been instructed to record what he knew about the northern warehouse.

He had written the date.

Then he had written:

*The northern warehouse was repaired last year.*

He had stopped there.

Steward Zhou De stood near the door.

"Young Master, the accountant has asked whether you remember the temporary movement of grain during the repairs."

"I have already said I do not remember."

"Yes, Young Master."

"Then why am I being asked again?"

"Because the former warehouse steward remembers that grain may have been moved."

"May have been?"

"Yes."

"That is not proof."

"No, Young Master."

Mingyuan looked at the blank space beneath his first sentence.

"Then what am I supposed to write?"

"Only what you know."

"I know there were repairs."

"Then write that."

"I know the warehouse was not empty."

"Then write that as well."

Mingyuan looked at him.

"Do you expect me to remember how many sacks were moved?"

"No, Young Master. If you do not remember, you should write that you do not remember."

Mingyuan put down his brush.

"This is absurd."

Steward Zhou remained still.

"Perhaps, Young Master. But the accountant cannot use an answer that does not exist."

Mingyuan stared at the paper.

He had expected the examination to be an accusation.

It would have been easier if it were.

He could have denied wrongdoing, demanded evidence, and complained about his sister's treatment of him.

Instead, the accountant kept asking questions that were difficult to argue with.

Who had ordered the repair?

Who had authorized the movement of grain?

Who had received it?

Who had recorded it?

Where had it gone afterward?

Mingyuan knew some answers.

He did not know others.

And the things he did know were not arranged in a way that helped him.

He picked up the brush again.

*The northern warehouse was repaired last year. I remember that the roof required substantial work. I do not remember the number of sacks moved during the repairs. I did not personally count them.*

He stopped.

"Is that enough?"

Steward Zhou glanced at the page.

"It is an answer."

"It is not a very useful one."

"It is more useful than a number invented to fill the space."

Mingyuan gave him an irritated look.

Steward Zhou did not withdraw the remark.

After a moment, Mingyuan continued.

He wrote that the repairs had been discussed during a family meeting. He remembered his father asking whether the work could be completed before the rainy season. He remembered the steward saying that part of the warehouse would need to be cleared.

He did not remember approving the movement of grain.

He did not remember receiving a report about the final quantity.

He wrote all of it.

When he finished, the page was still incomplete.

But it was no longer blank.

The accountant, Sun Qiming, read Mingyuan's statement in the afternoon.

He did not smile.

He did not accuse him of withholding information.

He simply placed it beside the northern warehouse ledger.

"Useful," he said.

Mingzhao, who was reviewing the estate's repair records, looked up.

"Which part?"

"The confirmation that the warehouse was partially cleared during repairs."

"That does not establish where the grain went."

"No."

"Nor does it establish that all forty-seven sacks were moved."

"No."

"Then it establishes only that movement was possible."

Sun Qiming nodded.

"Precisely."

Mingzhao leaned back.

She disliked the word *possible*.

It was a word that could be stretched to cover almost anything.

But she had hired an accountant to examine records, not to make the records more convenient for her.

"Have you received the former steward's notes?"

"Not yet. Steward Zhou has sent a messenger to the northern estate. The former steward believes he kept a private movement register during the repairs."

"Believes?"

"He has not found it."

"Then we wait."

Sun Qiming looked at her.

"That is the correct course."

"I am aware."

He returned to the ledger.

The northern warehouse discrepancy remained unresolved.

The available receiving records accounted for thirty-two sacks: eighteen in the ninth month and fourteen in the tenth.

The missing fifteen could not be assigned to temporary movement without evidence.

Nor could the forty-seven be treated as stolen merely because the records did not reconcile.

Mingzhao had written that distinction in the margin of her own copy.

She intended to keep it there.

The remaining two hundred and twenty-eight taels of the expenditure charged to Shen Mingyuan's residence were also still under examination.

The seventy-two taels for repairs to his study had been supported by a payment entry and the carpenter's confirmation. The original work order and final receipt were still being sought.

The other expenses were less cooperative.

Furniture had been mentioned.

A gathering had been mentioned.

Several household repairs had been mentioned.

There were no sufficiently detailed receipts for the entire amount.

Sun Qiming had divided the entries into categories.

**Supported.**

**Partially supported.**

**Unverified.**

Mingzhao examined the last category.

"Do not place all of these under his personal debt."

Sun Qiming looked up.

"Even where the expenditure was made for his residence?"

"An expenditure made for his residence is not automatically a personal debt. The estate may have authorized maintenance. We need to know what was ordered, what was delivered, and how it was classified."

"Then the final settlement may leave part of the amount with the estate."

"Or assign part to him. Or establish that some entries were never paid. We do not know yet."

Sun Qiming nodded.

Mingzhao returned the page.

"Keep the categories separate."

He made a note.

She had spent years insisting that her family distinguish between money they had spent and money they merely claimed to have spent.

She was not going to make the same mistake simply because the uncertainty now inconvenienced her brother.

That did not mean she had become generous.

It meant she wanted a settlement that could survive examination.

A knock came at the study door.

Qiao Yue entered.

"Young Lady, the northern residence inspector has arrived."

Mingzhao looked at the time.

"Already?"

"He arrived early. He says the canal road was clear."

"Then he can wait until I finish."

Qiao Yue hesitated.

"He has brought the second estimate."

"Put it on the table."

The estimate was placed beside the household accounts.

Mingzhao read it.

The roof repairs remained at one hundred and twenty taels.

Drainage work remained at thirty.

The inspector had added a more detailed list of smaller repairs: replacement of two damaged kitchen beams, repairs to the well cover, reinforcement of a section of the boundary wall, and replacement of several floorboards in the rear rooms.

The total was higher than the first estimate.

Mingzhao read it again.

"Why?"

Qiao Yue leaned forward.

"Why what?"

"Why has the estimate increased?"

"The inspector says the earlier examination did not include the kitchen beams."

"Why not?"

"He says the beams were covered by the ceiling boards."

Mingzhao put down the estimate.

"Then the first inspection was incomplete."

"Apparently."

"And the boundary wall?"

"The foundation is sound, but the upper section needs repair."

"How much?"

"Twenty-six taels."

Mingzhao looked at the figure.

"That is not a small repair."

"No."

"Did he provide measurements?"

"Yes. They are attached."

Mingzhao examined them.

The house was still suitable.

It had a clear title, a practical layout, and a location close enough to the commercial district without being directly on a busy street.

But suitability did not mean affordability.

The purchase price, repairs, furnishings, staff wages, kitchen supplies, security, and a reserve for unexpected expenses all had to be considered.

She would not exchange dependence on her father's household for dependence on a merchant who had sold her a house with concealed structural problems.

"Tell the inspector I want the roof and kitchen beams examined by a second carpenter."

Qiao Yue nodded.

"And ask the seller whether the price is negotiable in light of the revised estimate."

"Should I tell him we intend to buy?"

"No."

"Should I tell him we are still considering it?"

"Yes."

Qiao Yue folded the estimate.

"That sounds more expensive than simply buying it."

"It is certainly more time-consuming."

"Young Lady, you have been looking at houses for several days."

"And I have not yet bought a bad one."

Qiao Yue considered this.

"That is true."

Mingzhao returned to the ledger.

"Let us preserve the achievement."

At the Eastern Commercial Street, Lin Shuyun had received Zhou Wenqi's response.

The merchant was willing to accept payment on delivery rather than after inspection at the destination.

However, he wanted the right to reject any bolts that did not match the agreed quality.

Lin Shuyun read the letter twice.

Then she placed it in front of her business partner.

Mingzhao was not present. She had sent her comments through a written message, and Lin Shuyun was reviewing the offer independently.

The difficulty was not the payment arrangement.

It was the wording concerning quality.

The buyer wanted to inspect the cloth after arrival and reject anything that failed to meet his standard.

But his standard had not been clearly defined.

"How many bolts?" Lin Shuyun asked her clerk.

"Two hundred."

"How many have been inspected?"

"One hundred and eighty."

"And the remaining twenty?"

"Still being finished."

Lin Shuyun tapped the letter.

"Then he can inspect the two hundred bolts against the agreed sample and written specifications before dispatch."

The clerk nodded.

"What about defects discovered after delivery?"

"A short period for reporting concealed defects. But he cannot reject the entire shipment because he decides later that he dislikes the colour."

The clerk looked at the letter.

"Should we ask for another deposit?"

"No. The deposit is already thirty percent. That is sufficient if the remaining balance is paid on delivery."

"Then we need to specify the transport arrangements."

"Yes. Who pays the freight, who bears the risk during transport, and who signs for receipt."

The clerk wrote this down.

Lin Shuyun leaned back.

The offer was attractive.

Two hundred bolts would provide a useful return and release capital for the next production cycle.

But a profitable sale could become a troublesome one if the contract left too much room for disagreement.

She did not intend to accept a bad contract merely because the buyer had money.

She wrote her reply.

The buyer would receive an inspection opportunity before dispatch. The quality would be measured against the approved sample and the written specifications. The remaining payment would be due upon delivery. Any concealed defects would have to be reported within a fixed period, with the affected quantity identified.

If Zhou Wenqi accepted, the transaction could proceed.

If he did not, the stock would remain available for another buyer.

Lin Shuyun sealed the letter.

She had learned that a merchant who wanted a quick agreement was not necessarily a reliable one.

A merchant who disliked clear terms was sometimes even less reliable.

In the palace, the meeting about the senior attendants' medical reserve began shortly after midday.

The Crown Prince attended as instructed.

Fang Ru sat at the side of the room, but did not preside.

The senior administrator, Madam Zhao, chaired the meeting.

Present were representatives from the pharmacy store, the Imperial Household Administration, and the office responsible for senior attendants.

The first question was straightforward.

Who qualified for the reserve?

The senior attendants' office produced an eligibility list.

The pharmacy storekeeper confirmed the quantities in storage.

The administrator asked whether the list had been updated after recent transfers and retirements.

It had not.

There were three names of attendants who had left the palace service.

There were two names of attendants who had transferred to other residences.

There was also one senior attendant whose eligibility had been recognized but whose name had not been added to the list.

Madam Zhao looked at the records.

"Who updates this list?"

The senior attendants' representative answered, "Our office sends changes to the Imperial Household Administration."

"And does it?"

"We send monthly reports."

"Then why are the names not updated?"

The representative hesitated.

"The reports are received, but the pharmacy store uses the previous list."

The storekeeper looked displeased.

"We have not received an instruction to replace it."

Madam Zhao turned to the administrator.

"Who is responsible for issuing the updated list?"

The administrator looked at the documents.

The answer was not immediately clear.

The Crown Prince did not speak.

He had been instructed to observe the process and identify the missing authority.

After several moments, he asked, "Is there an existing regulation governing the update?"

The senior administrator looked at him.

"There is a provision requiring the Imperial Household Administration to maintain current records."

"Does that include the pharmacy list?"

"It should."

"Then why has the pharmacy store been using an old copy?"

The storekeeper replied, "We were not given a replacement."

"Who was required to provide it?"

No one answered immediately.

The Crown Prince waited.

It was an uncomfortable silence.

He recognized it.

He had encountered the same silence in his own household when he asked who had authorized an expense.

Madam Zhao finally said, "The administration should issue a corrected list. The senior attendants' office should submit changes within five days of any transfer, retirement, or new qualification. The pharmacy store should verify the list before each distribution."

"Who checks that the three records agree?" the Crown Prince asked.

Madam Zhao considered the question.

"The administration."

"At what interval?"

"Monthly."

"Then the procedure should say so."

The administrator looked at him.

"Your Highness, would that not create another register?"

"Only if the existing records cannot be compared."

The Crown Prince glanced at the documents.

"The problem is not the number of registers. It is that three offices are using three different versions of the same information."

Madam Zhao looked at the papers again.

After a moment, she nodded.

"That is correct."

The meeting continued.

They established a procedure for ordinary distribution.

The pharmacy store would confirm stock and condition. The senior attendants' office would confirm eligibility. The administration would issue the current list and reconcile the records monthly.

Unusual requests would be submitted with a written explanation.

Emergency medicines would be issued immediately when necessary, with the reason and quantity recorded afterward.

No one proposed that every ordinary distribution be sent to the Empress.

The Crown Prince noticed the absence.

It was a small achievement.

He was beginning to understand that the absence of an unnecessary approval could be more useful than the creation of another office.

When the meeting ended, Madam Zhao submitted the draft procedure to the Imperial Household Administration.

She did not send it to Chengming Palace.

The Crown Prince returned to the Hall of Mental Cultivation.

The Emperor was reviewing a report from the Ministry of Revenue.

"How did it go?"

"Three offices were using different versions of the eligibility list."

"Was the matter resolved?"

"The process was agreed upon. The revised list has not yet been issued."

"Good."

Cheng'an looked at him.

"Good?"

"An agreement is not the same as completion. You should know that by now."

The Crown Prince accepted the correction.

"The administration will issue the list. The senior attendants' office will report changes within five days. The pharmacy store will verify it before distribution. Monthly reconciliation will be required."

"Who approved the procedure?"

"Madam Zhao chaired the meeting. The administration is responsible for implementing it under the existing regulation."

"And your mother?"

"Was not required."

The Emperor nodded.

He looked almost satisfied.

Then he picked up another document.

"Now tell me why the Ministry of Revenue has sent me a report concerning the replacement of dining utensils."

The Crown Prince glanced at it.

"Perhaps they are also accustomed to sending matters to the highest available authority."

Zhao Jingheng looked at him.

"Do not become too pleased with yourself."

Cheng'an bowed his head.

"Yes, Father."

That evening, Yuru received the report from Madam Zhao.

She read the summary.

Then she examined the proposed procedure.

It was not perfect.

The monthly reconciliation was clear, but the report did not specify who would verify the condition of medicines already issued. The emergency provision was sensible, but it needed a time limit for the subsequent written record.

She marked both points.

Fang Ru stood beside her.

"Should we return it for revision?"

Yuru considered the question.

"Yes. But do not send it back as though the entire procedure is wrong. The main arrangement is sound."

Fang Ru nodded.

Yuru added a note.

*The ordinary distribution process is acceptable. Clarify responsibility for recording damaged or expired stock and specify the deadline for emergency records.*

She handed the paper back.

Fang Ru looked at the corrections.

"Your Majesty has not been asked to approve the distribution."

"No."

"Yet you have reviewed the procedure."

"That is different."

Fang Ru looked thoughtful.

Yuru set down her brush.

"An institution does not become independent merely because I refuse to read its documents. It must be able to ask for advice, correct mistakes, and act within its authority."

"Then Your Majesty will continue to review some matters?"

"During the transition, yes. But I must be careful about what I review."

She looked at the report.

"If I correct every sentence, they will wait for me to correct every sentence next time."

Fang Ru nodded slowly.

Yuru looked toward the window.

The palace gardens were quiet.

For years, she had believed that a well-managed household was one in which problems reached her before they became serious.

She had taken pride in noticing errors early, resolving disputes quickly, and preventing small difficulties from becoming public embarrassments.

She had also made herself the place where every problem eventually arrived.

The two things had become difficult to separate.

She would have to learn the difference.

And so would everyone else.

At the Qingyuan Marquisate, the family gathered for dinner.

Shen Guowei had received the latest report from Sun Qiming.

The northern warehouse remained under examination.

The seventy-two taels of study repairs had been partly supported.

The remaining two hundred and twenty-eight taels were still unverified.

The house valuation had increased because of additional repairs.

Lin Shuyun's buyer had not yet accepted the revised terms.

There was no final settlement.

There was, however, a growing number of documents.

Shen Guowei looked at the stack beside his plate.

"How many more reports are expected?"

Mingzhao calmly served herself a portion of vegetables.

"That depends on how many questions remain unanswered."

"Can the examination not be completed sooner?"

"Not accurately."

Madam Liang frowned.

"Your father is asking because the household cannot continue under this uncertainty."

"Then the household should provide the records."

"Mingzhao."

Mingzhao looked at her mother.

Madam Liang's voice was controlled.

"Your brother has begun reviewing the accounts. Your father has agreed to the independent examination. The stewards are cooperating. Surely there is room for patience."

"There is room for patience. There is no reason to replace evidence with haste."

Shen Mingyuan put down his chopsticks.

"I have already written a statement about the northern warehouse."

Mingzhao turned toward him.

"I have read it."

"Then you know I do not remember the number of sacks."

"Yes."

"Why does the accountant still need to examine the records?"

"Because your memory is not the only source of evidence."

Mingyuan looked irritated.

"Do you think I am lying?"

"No."

"Then what?"

"I think you do not remember. That is why we need the records."

He looked at her for a long moment.

It was not the answer he had expected.

He had been prepared for an argument.

Instead, she had denied him the satisfaction of one.

Old Madam Shen set down her spoon.

"Enough. The matter will be settled when it is settled. There is no need to turn every meal into an examination."

Mingzhao inclined her head.

"I agree."

Shen Guowei looked at her.

"You agree?"

"Yes. The accounts can be discussed during the day. We need not discuss them at dinner."

For a moment, the table became quiet.

Then Shen Mingxiu smiled faintly.

"That is the first sensible thing anyone has said this evening."

Madam Liang gave her a warning glance.

Mingxiu lowered her eyes and continued eating.

Shen Guowei looked at Mingzhao.

"Your transition schedule is still being reviewed."

"I know."

"Your brother has begun taking responsibility for the ordinary household accounts."

"I know."

"He will need time."

"He will have time."

"Then perhaps you can postpone your departure."

Mingzhao put down her chopsticks.

"Father, the transfer schedule is not the same as the date of my departure."

"What do you mean?"

"I can complete the transfer of records and explain unresolved matters. I can answer questions about the accounts. I can provide the names of the people who handled particular responsibilities."

She looked at him steadily.

"But I will not remain the permanent solution to every difficulty that appears after the transfer."

Shen Guowei's expression tightened.

"Nobody has asked you to do that."

Mingzhao glanced at the stack of reports beside him.

"No?"

He followed her gaze.

For a moment, he said nothing.

Then he looked away.

Madam Liang intervened.

"Your father is not asking you to manage the household forever. He only wants the family to be secure before you leave."

"Then the family should establish a management arrangement that does not depend on me."

Shen Mingyuan looked down at his plate.

He had begun to understand that the question was not whether he could perform every task his sister had performed.

It was whether he would take responsibility for the decisions that belonged to him.

He was not yet certain he wanted to.

But he had started reading the accounts.

That was more than he had done before.

Later that night, Mingzhao returned to her study.

Qiao Yue had placed the revised house estimate beside the accountant's report.

Mingzhao read both.

The house was becoming more expensive.

The estate examination was becoming more complicated.

The silk partnership was still waiting for a buyer's final acceptance.

Her family had not agreed to a final division.

Her brother had not yet proved capable of managing the household independently.

And the palace, though far away, continued to produce documents concerning ordinary matters that apparently required extraordinary attention.

Mingzhao looked at the house estimate.

Then at the property schedule.

She took a fresh sheet of paper.

At the top, she wrote:

**Matters That Must Be Completed Before Departure.**

She listed the transfer of accounts.

The independent valuation.

The settlement of documented advances.

The identification of unresolved liabilities.

The division of personal and family property.

The appointment of a new household administrator.

The transfer of records.

She stopped.

Then she added:

**Matters That Must Not Be Made Conditions of Departure.**

Under it, she wrote:

The younger brother's complete confidence.

The mother's complete approval.

The father's complete satisfaction.

The absence of all future household mistakes.

She looked at the last item.

Then she added:

The family's ability to function without asking where I am.

Qiao Yue, who had been arranging the documents, read the heading.

"Young Lady, that second list is shorter."

"It is more difficult."

"Why?"

"Because the first list requires work. The second requires me to stop accepting work."

Qiao Yue considered this.

"That does sound more difficult."

Mingzhao put down the brush.

Outside, the household was quiet.

Somewhere in the estate, a clerk was probably still searching for a receipt.

Somewhere in the palace, an administrator was probably rewriting a procedure.

Neither woman had resigned from anything yet.

Neither had left.

But for the first time, both had begun preparing for a life in which their absence would have to be treated as an ordinary fact rather than an administrative emergency.

And that, they were discovering, was a far more complicated undertaking than simply submitting a document.

**End of Chapter 12.**
