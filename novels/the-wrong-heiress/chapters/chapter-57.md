# **THE WRONG HEIRESS**

## **Chapter 57 — The Man Who Was Somewhere Else**

**Friday — 8:42 a.m.**

For several seconds, nobody moved.

The photograph lay on the table.

A man in a dark coat stood inside the hospital's private records corridor. His face was turned slightly towards the camera, but the resemblance was unmistakable.

Dorian Hale.

Or someone who had gone to considerable trouble to look like him.

Hale leaned closer.

“Enlarge it.”

Thomas Reed placed a magnifying lens over the photograph.

Hale studied the man's jaw, nose and the faint scar near his left eyebrow.

His expression hardened.

“That isn't me.”

Margaret Ellison folded her arms.

“We know.”

Hale looked at her.

“You might have mentioned that before showing me a photograph of myself committing a crime.”

“I wanted to see your reaction.”

“My reaction is that I dislike being shown photographs of crimes I didn't commit.”

Thomas cleared his throat.

“Can we establish what the photograph actually proves?”

Margaret nodded.

“It proves someone entered the private records corridor at 3:26 p.m. using a temporary access card registered under C. Voss. The person removed a sealed patient file and returned it seven minutes later.”

“And the face?” Hale asked.

“The photograph resembles you.”

“That's not evidence that I was there.”

“No,” Margaret agreed. “It is evidence that someone wanted the hospital record to suggest you were.”

Hale straightened.

“Then someone knew my face, my credentials and the hospital's access system.”

“Possibly,” Thomas said. “Or they knew enough about you to construct the appearance of an incident.”

Hale stared at the photograph again.

“Whoever did this had a reason to make my identity part of the record.”

“Perhaps,” Margaret replied. “But we don't know whether the photograph was intended to frame you, conceal someone else, or simply provide a false identity.”

Thomas turned towards Vane.

“You authorised the payment that preserved this record.”

Alistair Vane stood beside the archive shelves, his hands resting on the head of his walking stick.

“Yes.”

“Why?”

“To prevent the hospital from destroying the admission record.”

“Did you know who removed the file?”

“No.”

“Did you know who the patient was?”

“No.”

Thomas's expression remained unconvinced.

“That's a convenient collection of answers.”

Vane looked at him.

“I have spent twenty-one years being declared dead. Convenience is not something I have enjoyed.”

Hale rubbed his forehead.

“Wonderful. Everyone has a mysterious history, and I'm the one in the photograph.”

**9:06 a.m. — Ellison House, Records Room**

Hale requested the old administrative attendance register.

Margaret brought it from a locked cabinet.

“Why would your attendance records be here?” Thomas asked.

“Because I was a secretary. Secretaries kept records.”

“Not all secretaries.”

“Competent ones.”

Margaret opened the register.

The relevant date was marked in faded blue ink.

A council session had been held at the Vesper administrative headquarters from 2:00 p.m. until 5:00 p.m. Hale's name appeared among the attendees.

Beside it was his signature.

Thomas examined the page.

“A signature can be forged.”

“Yes,” Hale said. “Which is why you should look at the attached minutes.”

Margaret turned the page.

The minutes contained a series of handwritten corrections. Each correction had been initialled by the secretary responsible for the session.

Hale's initials appeared beside three amendments.

One concerned a delayed financial report.

Another concerned the temporary suspension of an executive access account.

The third concerned a dispute over an office allocation.

Thomas looked up.

“You were attending the meeting.”

“I was.”

“Can anyone confirm it?”

“Seven people signed the attendance sheet. Two of them were independent auditors. Their copies should still exist.”

Margaret checked the document number.

“Records series V-19. The original was distributed to the executive office and the external audit department.”

Thomas nodded.

“That gives us a way to verify it.”

Hale tapped the hospital photograph.

“And if the attendance record is genuine, someone wearing my face was in that corridor while I was sitting in a meeting.”

“Which means the hospital photograph is not a reliable identification,” Margaret said.

“It also means someone deliberately connected my name to an incident involving a private patient file.”

Vane watched them quietly.

Hale noticed.

“What?”

“Your attendance record confirms where you were,” Vane said. “It does not tell us who entered the hospital.”

“I'm aware.”

“Then we agree on something.”

“Don't become comfortable. It may be temporary.”

Thomas closed the register.

“We need the patient file.”

Margaret's expression changed.

“The hospital said it was sealed.”

“Returned to the records department, according to the incident report,” Thomas replied. “That doesn't mean it was never copied.”

Hale looked at the payment reference.

**M.V.**

“Dr. Simon Weller received the preservation payment,” he said. “He may know where the original record went.”

Vane's fingers tightened slightly around his walking stick.

“Weller was not supposed to retain a personal copy.”

Margaret turned towards him.

“You know that?”

“I approved the payment.”

“That wasn't my question.”

Vane paused.

“I was told the record would remain under hospital custody. I did not inspect the file.”

Thomas exchanged a glance with Margaret.

The distinction mattered.

Vane had preserved a record without knowing its contents. Or that was what he claimed.

**10:18 a.m. — Vesper Estate**

Felix Vesper had three screens open, two notebooks beside his keyboard and a half-eaten sandwich balanced on a closed laptop.

Maximilian stood behind him.

“Why is the sandwich on the laptop?”

“Because the desk is occupied.”

“By what?”

Felix gestured towards the screens.

“Evidence.”

“Your sandwich is not evidence.”

“It could become evidence if you keep questioning its placement.”

Maximilian looked ready to confiscate the entire desk.

Damian entered with a printed report.

“Dr. Simon Weller's current address has been confirmed.”

Felix immediately turned.

“Excellent.”

“Don't contact him yet,” Damian said. “We need to know whether he is still connected to the hospital.”

Cassian appeared in the doorway.

“Why don't we simply invite him to dinner?”

Maximilian looked at him.

“Because we are investigating a confidential medical record.”

“People have eaten dinner while discussing worse things.”

“Not at this house.”

Cassian considered that.

“Fair.”

Felix brought up a scanned hospital payment ledger.

“The payment to Weller was made the morning after the file was removed. It came from the old trust account. The reference was M.V.”

“Could it have been a hospital administrative fee?” Maximilian asked.

“Possibly. The description says confidential admission preservation.”

Damian studied the entry.

“Who requested it?”

“Vane's office.”

The room went quiet.

Maximilian looked towards the door.

“Bring Vane's original authorisation.”

Felix opened another folder.

“I already requested it.”

“From whom?”

“The archive.”

“Which archive?”

“The old one.”

Maximilian closed his eyes.

“Felix.”

“I'll specify next time.”

**11:02 a.m. — Aria's Apartment**

Aria was halfway through breakfast when Marcus called.

She answered after the third ring.

“If this is about another meeting, I am unavailable.”

“It concerns the hospital incident.”

“That sounds worse than a meeting.”

“Dorian Hale has been cleared of being physically present at the hospital. An old council attendance register places him elsewhere at the relevant time.”

Aria took another bite.

“So someone impersonated him.”

“That is the leading possibility.”

“Do we know who?”

“No.”

“Do we know who used the C. Voss access card?”

“No.”

“Do we know who the patient was?”

“No.”

Aria swallowed.

“Then what exactly have we learned?”

“That the hospital photograph cannot be treated as proof of Hale's involvement.”

“That is useful.”

Marcus waited.

“There's another development. Felix believes the preservation payment may lead to Dr. Simon Weller.”

“Then speak to him.”

“Maximilian wants to arrange a controlled interview.”

“Why?”

“He believes the doctor may have information concerning the missing patient record.”

“That's reasonable. Does he need me?”

Marcus hesitated.

“He asked whether you would attend.”

“No.”

“I'll inform him.”

“Don't let anyone tell the doctor I'm coming if I'm not.”

“Understood.”

Aria looked at the clock.

“Marcus?”

“Yes?”

“I want updates when something actually changes. Not every time somebody discovers another old document with a dramatic label.”

“I'll make a note.”

“Please don't make a document titled Dramatic Labels.”

A pause.

“I wasn't going to.”

“You hesitated.”

“I was considering whether it would improve our filing system.”

Aria ended the call.

**12:40 p.m. — Vesper Estate, Private Study**

Maximilian read the original payment authorisation.

The document carried Vane's signature, an old trust reference and a handwritten instruction.

**Preserve the confidential admission record. No duplication without independent custodial approval.**

Beneath it was a second line.

**Custodian to be notified before any release.**

Damian pointed to the wording.

“Who was the custodian?”

Felix checked the corresponding register.

“That's the problem.”

He enlarged the entry.

The field had been left blank.

Maximilian frowned.

“Blank?”

“Not crossed out. Not marked pending. Blank.”

Cassian leaned over his shoulder.

“Perhaps the custodian was deliberately omitted.”

“Or the authorisation was incomplete,” Damian said.

Felix shook his head.

“It was accepted and processed. The payment went through. The hospital recorded the instruction as valid.”

Maximilian looked at the signature.

“Then someone authorised the preservation without naming the person entitled to control the record.”

Cassian's expression lost its usual amusement.

“Which means the file could have been protected from destruction while remaining accessible to someone who knew how to retrieve it.”

Felix nodded.

“And someone did retrieve it.”

“Seven minutes,” Damian said.

“Seven minutes,” Felix repeated. “They took the file, then returned it. They didn't destroy it or alter it.”

Maximilian looked at the blank custodian field.

“Perhaps they weren't looking for the file itself.”

Cassian tilted his head.

“What else would they be looking for?”

“Something inside it.”

**2:15 p.m. — Central Women's Hospital, Administrative Office**

Dr. Simon Weller received the visitors in a small office overlooking the hospital courtyard.

He was older now, with silver hair and a carefully maintained desk.

He recognised the name Vesper.

He recognised the name Margaret Ellison.

When he saw Alistair Vane, his expression became noticeably still.

“You're supposed to be dead.”

Vane sat down.

“I have heard.”

Weller looked at the others.

“Why are you here?”

Thomas placed a copy of the payment authorisation on the desk.

“We are investigating a confidential admission record from nineteen years ago.”

Weller did not touch the paper.

“I cannot discuss patient records.”

“We aren't asking you to disclose a patient's medical history,” Margaret said. “We want to know what happened to a file you were paid to preserve.”

Weller's eyes moved towards Vane.

“You authorised that payment.”

“Yes.”

“You were told it would remain sealed.”

“It was.”

“Then what is the problem?”

Thomas placed the photograph beside the authorisation.

“Someone removed it.”

Weller studied the photograph.

For a moment, his face showed recognition.

Then it disappeared.

Hale noticed.

“You recognise the person?”

“No.”

“You hesitated.”

“I was looking at the corridor.”

“Not the corridor. The person.”

Weller pushed the photograph away.

“I don't know him.”

Hale leaned forward.

“He looks like me. I wasn't there.”

Weller stared at him.

Then he looked again at the photograph.

“I said I don't know him.”

Margaret's voice became colder.

“Dr. Weller, did you preserve the original patient file?”

“Yes.”

“Did you make a copy?”

“No.”

“Did anyone ask you to?”

Weller remained silent.

Vane spoke.

“Simon.”

The doctor's jaw tightened.

“You should not have come here.”

“Why?”

“Because the record was never meant to be found again.”

Thomas looked at him.

“By whom?”

Weller stood.

“I cannot answer that.”

Margaret rose too.

“You accepted money to preserve a record, and now you say it was never meant to be found?”

“I said it was never meant to be found again.”

The room fell silent.

Hale's eyes narrowed.

“Then someone found it once before.”

Weller looked at him.

And for the first time, he appeared genuinely afraid.

“Not someone,” he said quietly. “The person who had the right to collect it.”

“Who?” Thomas asked.

Weller turned towards the office door.

“I don't know.”

Margaret blocked his path.

“You just said someone had the right to collect it.”

“I said the person who had the right. I never said I knew their identity.”

“Who authorised the collection?”

Weller looked at the payment authorisation.

Then at Vane.

“The custodian.”

Vane's expression hardened.

“There was no custodian named.”

Weller's face drained of colour.

“Then you should ask who gave the hospital permission to release it.”

**3:04 p.m. — Outside the Hospital**

The group left without an answer.

Thomas carried the payment authorisation. Margaret held the photograph. Hale walked several steps behind them, visibly irritated.

Vane stopped beside the vehicle.

“Weller was afraid.”

“He was also withholding information,” Thomas replied.

“Those are not mutually exclusive.”

Margaret turned to Vane.

“You said the record was preserved under your instruction.”

“Yes.”

“You didn't tell us someone had already collected it.”

“I didn't know.”

“Weller seemed to believe otherwise.”

Vane looked towards the hospital entrance.

“He may have been referring to an authorised release that I never received.”

Hale folded his arms.

“Or someone used your name to make an unauthorised release look legitimate.”

Vane said nothing.

Thomas glanced at the document again.

The blank custodian field was no longer an administrative curiosity.

It was the centre of the problem.

A record had been preserved.

A file had been removed.

A hospital administrator had accepted payment.

And someone had apparently collected the right to control that file without leaving a name.

Margaret finally spoke.

“There is one person who may know how the release was authorised.”

“Who?” Thomas asked.

“Whoever maintained the hospital's confidential admission register.”

Hale looked at her.

“Is that person still alive?”

Margaret's answer came after a long pause.

“I don't know.”

Vane looked back at the hospital.

“Then we find the register.”

And somewhere inside the hospital, in a locked records room, the original admission register still carried the entry that had never been explained.

**M.V. — Release authorised.**

There was no signature beside it.
